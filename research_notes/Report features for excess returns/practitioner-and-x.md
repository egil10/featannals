# Practitioner and X evidence: filing-text features for future excess returns

Scope is the buy-side, vendor, blog, and X layer, roughly 2018–2026, with weight on 2023–2026 LLM practice. Academic canon is out of scope except where a practitioner restates a number. Every claim below is tagged **paper-backed** (including a blog that only restates a paper), **practitioner backtest** (the firm’s or blog’s own test, not peer-reviewed), **vendor-marketing**, or **social-unverified**. Snippets that could not be opened in full are marked as such.

## What do AQR, Two Sigma, Man/AHL, Acadian, Robeco, Research Affiliates, Alpha Architect, Quantpedia, and OSAM say in public about text-from-filings signals?

### Takeaway

The only large quant house that, in 2026, publishes its own multi-decade test of a filing-text equity signal is Research Affiliates: year-over-year Jaccard dissimilarity of 10-Ks/10-Qs still spreads about 2.9% a year in the top 1,000 US stocks through September 2025, is stronger after 2014 than before, and is most useful as a value-trap filter, not as a 22% long-short. Man AHL (2019) treats annual-report diffs and finance-specific sentiment as a research program but publishes no filing alpha. Robeco and Acadian publish NLP on disclosures, but the public pieces are earnings-call topics or climate-alignment scores, not excess-return factors. AQR’s public text paper is supervised news sentiment, not filings. No public Two Sigma or OSAM filing-text factor was found, and no Cliff Asness note that specifically kills text factors was found.

### Cited Findings

- **Research Affiliates, Que Nguyen (Partner, CIO Equity Strategies) and Joseph Shim (SVP, Strategy Research), June 2026, “Reading Between the Lines: NLP for Long-Horizon Factor Investing (Part 1 of 2).”** **[practitioner backtest]** On the 1,000 largest US stocks, April 1995–September 2025, quarterly cap-weighted buckets by Jaccard similarity of the latest 10-K or 10-Q versus the year-ago filing: big changers (Bucket 1) excess return −1.5%/year, steady-language (Bucket 3) +1.4%/year, spread about 2.9%. Four-factor annualized alpha −1.49% (t = −2.29) versus +1.76% (t = 2.59). Out-of-sample October 2015–September 2025 is wider, not narrower: Bucket 1 excess −2.4%, Bucket 3 +1.7%. No announcement-day jump; the long-short gap widens for 12 months (event-study cumulative abnormal returns). Annual rebalance compresses the spread to about −1.2% versus +1.1%. Inside already-risky cohorts (cheap, aggressive investment, unprofitable, high volatility, low momentum) the high-versus-low similarity return spread is 3.5–5.3%/year. They explicitly reject the idea that hedge-fund NLP has arbitraged the edge away, and they say a simple vocabulary overlap still works; Part 2 (not in this document) is where they plan to discuss embeddings and LLMs. They also say the signal does not warn on Enron, WorldCom, or SVB, because fraud conceals and liquidity crises happen between filings. Citigroup’s 2006 to 2007 10-K Jaccard fell from 0.72 to 0.50 while other large US banks’ scores rose slightly — their example that the signal is firm-specific, not a sector bet. — [Research Affiliates PDF](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf); firm account summary — [RA_Insights, 11 Jun 2026, post 2065133020336787745](https://x.com/RA_Insights/status/2065133020336787745)

- **Alpha Architect, 13 Jun 2016, “Want to Know the Secret to Inefficient Prices? Lazy Prices.”** **[paper-backed restatement]** Restates the then-working paper of Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen: long non-changers / short changers in annual and quarterly filings, 30–60 bps per month (up to about 7.6% over the following year). Risk Factors section: 5-factor alpha 188 bps per month, t = 2.76, “over 22% per year.” MD&A is where changes concentrate; Risk Factors, CEO/CFO language, and litigation language are what they call more informative. Bottom line they give readers: avoid stocks making big changes to Risk Factors. This is not an Alpha Architect live-strategy result. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/)

- **Alpha Architect, 6 May 2021, “Text-Based Factor Investing.”** **[vendor/research explainer, no return]** Uses 10-K business descriptions embedded into a dense vector and scored for semantic closeness to pre-Facebook (2006–07) “platform” articles, to build a platform-exposure score (Sparkline / Kai Wu). No portfolio return is quoted on the page retrieved. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/text-based-factor-investing/)

- **Alpha Architect, 27 Feb 2026, “Can AI tools help find the needle in the corporate filing haystack?”** **[paper-backed restatement; page body not fully fetched — bot wall, details from the search index only]** Summarizes a study of EDGAR filings for 500 companies, 1996–2023 (~278,000 filings). Look-ahead control described in the snippet: pretrain from scratch with a fixed knowledge cutoff of 2007 and iteratively update each firm-specific LLM. Snippet claims: exhibits carried roughly 150% more “high-information” content than the main filing; 55.2% of high-information content arrives via non-earnings current reports and other filings, versus 10.2% from earnings announcements, 14.3% from quarterlies, and 20.3% from annual reports; highest information-content decile had a 106% increase in absolute return on the disclosure date versus 24.2% for the lowest decile. Author names were not in the indexed snippet. Do not treat the percentages as verified against the article body. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/ai-tools-financial-disclosures/)

- **Man AHL, Slavi Marinov (Head of Equities Alpha, Systematic), 18 Jul 2019, “Natural Language Processing in Finance: Shakespeare Without the Monkeys.”** **[practitioner method note, no filing-strategy return]** Public case for NLP alpha is sentiment across news, annual reports, calls, and social text, but no backtest number is given. Concrete filing example: IBM’s 2016 10-K buried brand risk under “Failure of Innovation Initiatives”; the 2017 10-K split it out as “Damage to IBM’s Reputation” and listed eight sources. They argue machines should diff added, deleted, and modified risk factors and see which peers moved together. On dictionaries: Tetlock (2007) counted Harvard General Inquirer negatives; Loughran-McDonald (2011) showed almost 75% of those “negative” words were irrelevant in finance (tax, costs, loss, capital, cost, expense, expenses alone were more than a quarter of the negative count). Word counts still score “increase in dividend” as zero because no isolated word is polar. Their proposed stack is finance seed lexicons expanded with word2vec, dependency parsing for targeted sentiment, then end-to-end neural classifiers. They also flag FOMC wording changes and earnings-call speech emotion (citing Mayew and Venkatachalam 2012) as related, not as tested AHL factors. — [Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/shakespeare-without-the-monkeys)

- **Man AHL, “The Big Picture: AI,” document dated 21 Mar 2024 (indexed PDF; full PDF not read).** **[practitioner commentary, snippet only]** Says GPT raises the utility of unstructured text including company filings and is a cheaper substitute for labor-intensive cleaning, and that a machine can surface hundreds of signals where a quant finds about one a month. The same note warns that the danger of that search is data mining, and that they compare alphas to what the same search would find in random noise and look at information decay and the out-of-sample share of in-sample. No filing-text return is in the snippet. — [Man PDF](https://www.man.com/documents/download/5a8d1-65277-88749-3a182/Man_AHL_Analysis_The_Big_Picture:_AI_English_(United_States)_21-03-2024.pdf)

- **AQR, Zheng Ke, Bryan T. Kelly, and Dacheng Xiu, 19 Dec 2019, “Predicting Returns with Text Data.”** **[paper-backed, and it is news, not filings]** Supervised sentiment on Dow Jones Newswires: screen terms that predict returns, weight them with a topic model, aggregate with penalized likelihood. Claim is that this beats “sentiment scores used for stock return prediction (e.g., those sold by commercial vendors or built with dictionary-based methods).” AQR’s page does not apply the method to 10-Ks. — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Predicting-Returns-with-Text-Data)

- **Cliff Asness, AQR, 2 Jun 2015, “It’s Not Data Mining — Not Even Close.”** **[practitioner commentary, not about text]** Defense of value, momentum, and related factors against a data-mining charge. This search did not find an Asness note or located post that adopts Lazy Prices or that says text factors died after publication. Absence is a gap, not a quote. — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Perspectives/It-Is-Not-Data-Mining-Not-Even-Close)

- **Robeco quant charts, earnings calls rather than 10-Ks, no stock-level alpha.** **[practitioner descriptive, not a return factor]** Matthias Hanauer and Tim Vogel, 11 Aug 2023, “Quant Chart: Inside the Mind of the C-Suite”: NLP topic shares on S&P 500 calls through 4 Aug 2023 (pandemic and recession dominated early 2020); bag-of-words cannot relate words, so they point at embeddings. 26 Aug 2024, “Quant Chart: Swings in sector sentiment”: sector sentiment from calls via FinBERT or GPT-3/GPT-4-style embeddings; IT, communication services, and health care sentiment jumped with AI, digital media, and GLP-1s, while consumer sectors stayed weighed down by inflation. Explicitly a sales invitation for “dynamic quantitative theme investing,” not a published long-short. 4 Jun 2025, “Quant Chart: Who’s afraid of the Tariff Man?”: proprietary classifier on calls; most tariff sentences are neutral, the worried share rises in 2018–19 and April–May 2025, and a small set of firms frames tariffs as opportunity. Footnote says the same pipeline can be pointed at filings, news, interviews, and social text, but the chart is calls. — [2023](https://www.robeco.com/en-us/insights/2023/08/quant-chart-inside-the-mind-of-the-c-suite); [2024](https://www.robeco.com/en-uk/insights/2024/08/quant-chart-swings-in-sector-sentiment); [2025](https://www.robeco.com/es-latam/vision-de-mercado/2025/06/quant-chart-whos-afraid-of-the-tariff-man)

- **Acadian, February 2023, “A Machine Learning-Based Approach to Thematic ESG Investing” (GEO).** **[practitioner research, not an excess-return filing factor]** Supervised NLP on earnings transcripts, regulatory filings, and shareholder meetings to score energy-transition alignment, including whether decarbonization language names dates, baselines, and targets (they call this a credibility check against TCFD and SBTi, i.e. a greenwash filter). GEO quintiles line up monotonically with subsequent one-year peer-relative declines in carbon intensity, 2010–2020, and high scores coincide with science-based targets and a higher share of green patents. Hypothetical long-only portfolios, January 2016–August 2022, versus MSCI World: restricting the universe to the top 20% GEO names cut exposure to Acadian’s alpha model by nearly half relative to their standard global equity process; a portfolio-level GEO tilt kept most of that exposure and still cut weighted-average carbon intensity via stock selection rather than sector bets. No claim that filing tone predicts excess returns. A related public description of their ENGAGER tool (Investment News NZ, 14 Aug 2022, quoting the firm) is an NLP screen of corporate communications for greenwashing language, used to pick engagement targets, not to rank expected returns. — [Acadian](https://www.acadian-asset.com/investment-insights/esg/a-machine-learning-based-approach-to-thematic-esg-investing); [Investment News NZ](https://investmentnews.co.nz/investment-news/quant-manager-engages-greenwash-detector-in-esg-number-cruncher/)

- **Two Sigma and OSAM.** No public paper or insight page in this search describes a filing-text or 10-K/10-Q return signal. Two Sigma’s Form ADV describes a shared quantitative research platform and licensed analytics, not text features. OSAM’s public factor notes (shareholder yield, September 2019; value factor archive) stay on dividends, buybacks, valuation, and price trends, and say they do not read individual companies beyond model inputs. A 2026 content-farm page that attributes an “8.3% annualized alpha” earnings-call strategy to Two Sigma is not used; it cites no primary document.

- **S&P Global Market Intelligence (vendor, but it is the public “Lazy Prices is not enough” note), HedgeNordic guest column, 18 Jun 2026, “Not So Lazy Prices,” Liam Hynes (Head of New Product Development, Public Markets, Quantitative Research & Solutions) quoting Henry Chiang (quantitative researcher, QRS).** **[vendor-marketing; article body did not render, claims are from the indexed text]** Bag-of-words net positivity is called the “analog” era. Lazy Prices (Cohen et al. 2020) showed that year-on-year change in risk-section language carried signal even without knowing whether a risk was added or removed. Their line: “Lazy Prices is starting to be not so lazy anymore… Now we have the tools to take it much further,” meaning sentence-level topic, materiality, and polarity from an LLM. No new return number in the indexed text. — [HedgeNordic](https://hedgenordic.com/2026/06/not-so-lazy-prices/)

### Inferences

- Serious public adoption of Lazy Prices in 2025–26 is the sign of the trade (changers do worse) at a large-cap, low-turnover magnitude of a few percent a year, not the 188 bp/month Risk Factors sort. Research Affiliates is the document that both keeps the signal and cuts the marketed magnitude down.
- The post-publication-decay story that applies to many academic factors is not what RA reports for filing similarity: their 2015–2025 window is the stronger one. That is one firm’s backtest, with the usual hindsight and specification choices, and it conflicts with the fundamental-PM view on X that simple filing patterns have already been compressed (see the X section).
- Where LLMs show up in official quant-house writing (Robeco, Acadian, Man, S&P Global), the claimed use is topic- and credibility-aware reading of calls and disclosures. Almost none of those pieces publish a stock-level excess return for the LLM score itself.
- AQR’s public stance on text is “supervise the dictionary on the thing you want to predict, and do it on fast news,” which is a different horizon from filings.

### Gaps

- No located Asness, Israel, or other AQR author comment on Lazy Prices, 10-K tone, or LLM filing scores. The 2015 data-mining essay is about the classic factors.
- Two Sigma, Man AHL (beyond the 2019 method piece and the 2024 AI caution), and OSAM: no public filing-text performance number found.
- Research Affiliates Part 2 (deep-learning / LLM architectures) was announced and not in the Part 1 PDF.
- Alpha Architect’s February 2026 AI-filings summary could not be opened past a bot wall, so those percentages are unverified against the article.
- HedgeNordic / S&P Global column body did not render; only the indexed quotations were usable.

## Which vendors and datasets do practitioners cite, and what do they productize?

### Takeaway

The filing dataset that Quantpedia and QuantConnect actually wire into strategies is Brain’s Language Metrics on Company Filings: section-level sentiment, LM-style word-class shares, readability, lexical density, and year-over-year similarity, for both the full report and Risk Factors / MD&A. Amenity, ExtractAlpha, and Robeco’s public NLP are mostly earnings-call or news sentiment. Hudson Labs productizes multi-year 10-K diffs (accounting estimates, customer concentration) as an analyst copilot, without a return claim. Loughran-McDonald remains the dictionary everyone else is measured against. RavenPack-on-filings and a Kensho filing-factor spec were not found.

### Cited Findings

- **Brain Language Metrics on Company Filings (BLMCF).** **[vendor product spec]** Covers roughly 6,000 US stocks. QuantConnect documents history from January 2010, daily, about 5,000 names. Two blocks: (1) level metrics on the latest 10-K or 10-Q — financial sentiment; share of constraining, interesting, litigious, and uncertainty language; readability; lexical richness and lexical density; report length and mean sentence length; (2) change versus the prior same-period filing — deltas of those metrics, plus similarity overall and similarity within a language type (litigious, uncertainty, and so on). All of the above for the full report and for Risk Factors and MD&A. Sample field names in a Datarade extract include `SENTIMENT`, `SCORE_UNCERTAINTY`, `SCORE_LITIGIOUS`, `SCORE_CONSTRAINING`, `SCORE_INTERESTING`, `READABILITY`, `LEXICAL_RICHNESS`, `LEXICAL_DENSITY`, `SPECIFIC_DENSITY`, and the same set prefixed `RF_` for risk factors. Brain’s own summary cites Lazy Prices and Padysak (2020) as the inefficiency the dataset is built to trade. — [QuantConnect docs](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/writing-algorithms/datasets/brain/brain-language-metrics-on-company-filings); [product summary PDF](https://initialdataoffering.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/BLMCFV21summary.pdf); [Datarade field list](https://datarade.ai/data-products/brain-language-metrics-on-company-filings)

- **Loughran-McDonald lexicon, as used by practitioners.** **[paper-backed, still the baseline vendors and Man cite]** Man AHL (2019) calls the 2011 lists (~2,300 negative words, 350+ positive) the academic default precisely because the Harvard psychosocial dictionary mislabels finance. LM’s readability paper (Journal of Finance, 2014) argues Fog is misspecified for disclosures and that 10-K file size is a simpler readability proxy: no parsing, easier to replicate, correlated with other readability constructs. — [Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/shakespeare-without-the-monkeys); [Loughran and McDonald 2014](http://dx.doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12162)

- **Amenity Analytics.** **[vendor-marketing]** Products page: Fundamentals Key Driver scores the change in sentiment of “over 100 events” extracted from online news, earnings calls, or SEC filings. A separate Signal API is described as customized sentiment from earnings calls, not from 10-Ks. A 2023 Symphony case study uses the Margin Key Driver on earnings-call commentary about margin expansion and contraction; no Sharpe or alpha is stated. A 2020 AWS startup post says the firm processed on the order of a million texts a day and named Nasdaq and Moody’s as clients. No public filing-text backtest was found. — [products](https://www.amenityanalytics.com/products); [AWS, 1 Jul 2020](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/startups/how-amenity-analytics-serverless-architecture-analyzes-data/)

- **ExtractAlpha, 21 Aug 2024, “Understanding Market Sentiment with NLP.”** **[vendor-marketing]** Named production signals are China News Sentiment, Japan News Signal, IRP Sentiment Signal, and Transcripts Model US and Asia. The blog’s examples are news, social, and earnings reports. No 10-K/10-Q factor is listed. “Clients have reported significant performance improvements” is not quantified. — [ExtractAlpha](https://extractalpha.com/2024/08/21/understanding-market-sentiment-with-nlp/)

- **Hudson Labs, 2 Dec 2025, product note on multi-year 10-K analysis.** **[vendor-marketing, no alpha]** Argues generalist models (ChatGPT, Claude) miss content inside a single long 10-K, so redlines across filings fail. Their copilot queries up to six 10-Ks or twenty press releases at once. Worked examples they choose to advertise: tracking accounting-estimate changes (Amazon server useful life decreased from 6 years to 5 in 2025; Oracle increased from 5 to 6 in 2025) and pulling customer concentration and related-party disclosures out of footnotes across six or more years. That is a productized version of “what changed in the estimates and the customer list,” not a tested return factor. — [Hudson Labs](https://www.hudson-labs.com/post/product-announcement-industry-leading-multi-year-ai-analysis-of-10-ks-press-releases-and-more)

- **S&P Global Market Intelligence / the “Not So Lazy Prices” column.** **[vendor-marketing]** See the firm section. The product claim is LLM scoring of what a risk-section edit means (topic, materiality, polarity), on top of the Cohen similarity result. Kensho is mentioned only in an unreliable secondary table (below); no Kensho filing-factor datasheet was found.

- **Not used: a 3 Apr 2026 Finantrix roundup** that assigns processing-time and accuracy percentages to Bloomberg, FactSet, S&P Kensho, AlphaSense, Sentieo, and Amenity, and that assigns Two Sigma and Nomura specific NLP alphas. Those figures have no primary citation on the page. Treated as content-farm. — [Finantrix](https://www.finantrix.com/in-focus/alpha-architects-ai-first-investment/nlp-earnings-calls-10k-alpha)

### Inferences

- If a practitioner strategy page says “10-K NLP” and then names a dataset, in this literature it is almost always Brain, and the features are dictionary shares plus similarity, split by section — not an LLM prompt.
- The vendor move in 2023–26 is from “a sentiment number” to “a change, inside a topic, with a materiality tag” (Amenity events, S&P’s reading of Lazy Prices, Hudson’s accounting-estimate diffs). Return evidence has not moved with the product language.
- Call transcripts are the text type vendors will actually sell as a signal (ExtractAlpha, Amenity Signal API, Robeco). Filings are sold as a monitoring and redline product.

### Gaps

- No primary page found for RavenPack applied to 10-Ks/10-Qs, for S&P Kensho’s filing features, or for Sentieo/AlphaSense NLP features beyond the fact that comparison-across-filings is a workflow these tools market. AlphaSense’s acquisition of Sentieo was not re-verified here.
- No vendor datasheet in this search publishes a live, net-of-cost Sharpe for a filing-text factor.

## What do serious blogs record: definition, quoted return, and is it just the paper?

### Takeaway

Alpha Architect’s 2016 Lazy Prices note is a straight restatement, including the 188 bp figure. Quantpedia does three different things: a Brain-data study of its own in which low similarity of positive language earns about 5% a year (the opposite sign from Lazy Prices); a lexical-density long-short whose only retrieved performance number is a Sharpe of 0.69; and a straight restatement of a 10-K cyber-risk premium that the underlying paper says was much larger before the idea was public. A QuantConnect/Quantpedia long-only “filing language stability” book from 2020–2026 is a community backtest on Brain data, not an institutional result. The old Quantopian Lazy Prices notebook states the thesis and does not, in the text retrieved, report a tearsheet number.

### Cited Findings

- **Quantpedia / Matus Padysak, 10 Sep 2020, “The Positive Similarity of Company Filings and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns” (SSRN abstract id 3690461).** **[practitioner/blog backtest on Brain data, not a restatement]** Universe is the large names Brain covered (they say about the largest 1,000 US stocks), not Cohen’s ~4,000. Each month, rank on the most recent filing’s similarity of positive language only; hold one month; deciles. Bottom decile (language changed) annualized return 14.58%, vol 19.40%, return/vol 0.75, max drawdown −49.50%. Top decile 9.11%, vol 19.70%, 0.46, max drawdown −58.81%. Bottom-minus-top 5.47%, vol 6.48%, return/vol 0.84, max drawdown −10.43%, February 2007–May 2020. A dollar in the bottom decile from 28 Feb 2007 became $4.58 by 29 May 2020, versus $2.40 in the top decile and $1.84 in the spread. They say asset-pricing models and the change in filing sentiment do not explain it. Uncertainty-language similarity, on their preliminary sorts, keeps the Cohen sign (non-changers beat changers); positive-language similarity flips. The opening paragraph of the same post says “firms that do change reports outperform non-changers,” which is the wrong way around relative to Cohen and relative to the rest of the post. — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)

- **Quantpedia, 10 Sep 2021 blog and 16 Sep 2021 strategy page, lexical density of 10-Ks and 10-Qs.** **[blog backtest; Sharpe figure is from a thin index snippet of the strategy page]** Inspired by Joenväärä et al. on hedge-fund letters, not by a 10-K paper. Universe: top 500 US stocks by dollar volume. Sort on Brain lexical density and specific density (share of finance-domain words). Long top decile, short bottom decile. The blog’s economic claim is that denser, more “matter of fact” reports outperform. The strategy-page index exposed a Sharpe ratio of 0.69; the annualized return and the sample window were not retrieved from the page body. — [blog](https://quantpedia.com/how-to-use-lexical-density-of-company-fillings/); [strategy page](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/how-to-use-lexical-density-of-company-filings)

- **Quantpedia, 12 Dec 2023, “Cyber Risk and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns.”** **[paper-backed restatement of Celeny and Maréchal, SSRN 4587993]** doc2vec proximity of the whole 10-K (not only the cyber section) to MITRE ATT&CK descriptions; score is the average of the top 1% of paragraph similarities. Sample: 7,000+ US listings, January 2007–December 2022. High cyber-risk quantile excess return 18.72% per year. Long high / short low: average monthly excess 0.56%, about 6.93% per year, still significant against Fama-French five factors in their tests. The post quotes the paper’s own split: before the first SSRN mention of a cyber-risk premium in November 2020 (Florackis et al.), the long-short averaged 11.88% per year and was significant at 1%; the point of the split is that the premium was stronger before the idea circulated. Monthly excess returns rise from 0.88% (low) to 1.44% (high). They also say the score is almost uncorrelated with size, book-to-market, and beta, and that dictionary cyber counts do worse. — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/cyber-risk-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)

- **Quantpedia premium update (page dated in the index as a 30 July update) and Emily Xinyu Sun, QuantConnect research 20966, “Filing Language Stability as a Selection Signal.”** **[community backtest, not institutional]** Strategy #1304. Long-only the most stable filers among the 100 most liquid US equities, ranked monthly on Brain similarity, preferring the risk-factors score and falling back to the full report. Weights chosen to maximize Sharpe. Quantpedia’s card: backtest 2020–2026, indicative performance 21.09%, estimated volatility 35.15%, and “greater risk-adjusted returns than SPY.” That 21% is not an alpha; a 35% volatility long-only book over 2020–2026 is a bull-market absolute return. The QuantConnect research URL itself returned a shell, not the tearsheet, when fetched. A separate sentence on the same Quantpedia update, about a different strategy earning 7.5% a year from January 2023 to June 2026, should not be attached to this filing book. — [Quantpedia update](https://vvv.quantpedia.com/quantpedia-premium-update-july-30th/); [QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/research/20966/filing-language-stability-as-a-selection-signal/)

- **Quantopian archive notebook, “Analyzing Alpha in 10-Ks and 10-Qs.”** **[community replication writeup]** Thesis, attributed to Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen and to a QuantCon 2018 Cohen interview: short the largest text changes, long the smallest, next quarter. Says an Alphalens tearsheet was run. No mean return, Sharpe, or t-stat was in the text retrieved. — [Quantopian archive](https://quantopian-archive.netlify.app/forum/threads/analyzing-alpha-in-10-ks-and-10-qs.html)

- **Alpha Architect Lazy Prices (2016) and the 188 bp / 22% figure.** **[paper-backed restatement]** See the firm section. The same 22% number is repeated in 2026 marketing and social posts (Quantt, 27 Apr 2026; several X explainers). Quantt also repeats the paper’s claims of no announcement-day return, drift over 6–18 months, and that 86% of 10-K textual changes are negative in sentiment. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/); [Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)

- **Manager-sentiment, financial-constraints text, and climate-text factor pages.** Not found as Quantpedia or Alpha Architect strategy pages with their own quoted returns in this search. Climate text at Acadian is a thematic-alignment and carbon-intensity tool, not a published return factor. Financial-constraints text and “manager sentiment” as equity factors were not located on the practitioner sites searched.

### Inferences

- The blog layer has not replaced Cohen’s sign for overall similarity. It has added one real variant: similarity of positive language has the opposite cross-section in Padysak’s Brain sample, at about 5% a year long-short with a 0.84 return/vol and a small drawdown, in large caps, through May 2020. That is the most interesting non-restatement in the blog set, and it stops in 2020.
- Cyber-risk text is the clearest “this decayed once people wrote it down” result that a serious blog actually quotes, and the decay split is the paper’s, not the blog’s invention.
- Community long-only versions of Lazy Prices in the 2020s (QuantConnect, top 100, risk-factor similarity) are easy to over-read: 21% with 35% vol is not the 22% alpha of the academic quintile sort.

### Gaps

- Lexical-density annualized return, sample dates, and whether the 0.69 Sharpe is gross, were not retrieved from the strategy-page body.
- QuantConnect research 20966 body was behind the site shell, so the 21.09% figure is Quantpedia’s card, not a verified tearsheet.
- No Alpha Architect or Quantpedia page located that quotes a return for financial-constraints text, MD&A tone as its own strategy, or climate-disclosure text as an excess-return factor.

## What does credible X discussion from 2023 through 2026-10 actually say?

### Takeaway

Keyword search did not surface a 2024–26 thread in which a named portfolio manager replicates or buries the Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev GPT earnings-direction trade. What credible accounts do say: a former multi-manager PM argues there is rarely still alpha in the filings themselves; a Stanford lecturer running a fund argues LLMs add conviction to the consensus view and that the alpha is the case the model refuses to treat as special; a quant account tells followers to score new versus resolved risk-factor language rather than raw change; a retail disclosure-signal product publishes tone and “rewrite distance” as a separate input from earnings quality, with no test. Look-ahead bias, moat scores, and RPO-as-a-text-factor have no substantive practitioner thread in the searches run. The Kim-Muhn-Nikolaev paper is real, widely presented in 2024, and marked withdrawn on arXiv in February 2025 for reasons this search did not recover.

### Cited Findings

- **Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev, “Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models,” Chicago Booth.** **[paper-backed; later withdrawn from arXiv]** Design, from the October 2024 SUERF brief and the October 2024 Q Group slides (Muhn): anonymized balance sheet and income statement only, no MD&A and no industry label; years relabeled “Period t” so the model cannot use a memorized firm-year. Simple prompt accuracy is weak (SUERF figure: 0.523 without chain-of-thought). Chain-of-thought, which tells the model to find trends, compute ratios, then call the direction of next earnings, accuracy 60.4%. Analyst median accuracy 52.7% one month after earnings, rising to about 56.7% one or two quarters later. A stepwise logistic regression on 59 ratios: 52.9%. A specialized ANN on the same numbers: 60.4%, matching GPT. An ANN that also sees GPT’s written analysis: about 63.2%. Authors’ claim, including at Q Group: the edge is not training-set memory; equal-weight portfolios on the GPT call have positive alphas after five factors plus momentum, and a higher Sharpe than the ANN or analyst strategies. The SUERF brief does not print the Sharpe or the alpha. arXiv abstract 2407.17866 (v3, 20 Feb 2025) says the paper was withdrawn by Valeri Nikolaev and hosts no PDF. The withdrawal reason is not stated on that page. — [SUERF brief no. 1008, Oct 2024](https://www.suerf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/SUERF-Policy-Brief-1008_Kim-et-al.pdf); [Q Group slides, Oct 2024](https://www.q-group.org/resources/2024%20Fall%20Seminar/Presentations/9.%20Muhn_Q_Conference.pdf); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)

### Inferences

- The X consensus among people with real buy-side biographies is not “prompt a model with the 10-K and trade the answer.” Caughran says the filing alpha has been compressed and the model’s quant work is bad. Mak says the model is a consensus engine and the residual work is reading the raw filing for the non-default fact. Both are using LLMs. Neither is selling an LLM score as a factor.
- The trade ideas that keep showing up in 2026 posts are still pre-LLM: risk-factor diffs, tone versus its own history, and whether uncertainty language is being added or retired. Alphatica’s “distance” and Quant Beckman’s “resolved versus new risk” are attempts to stop treating every textual change as bad. Neither has a public test.
- Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev are the prompt the shortlist will be asked about. Two facts should travel together: the published design (anonymous statements, chain-of-thought, 60.4% versus 52.7%, and a claim that memory was ruled out) and the February 2025 arXiv withdrawal with no stated reason. It is also a numbers paper, not a filing-narrative paper. The authors say narrative context was stripped on purpose.

### Gaps

- No X post found in which a practitioner reports an out-of-sample Sharpe for a GPT or FinBERT 10-K score, or documents a failed replication of Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev.
- Why the arXiv paper was withdrawn was not stated on the abstract page. The SSRN version may still circulate; this search did not confirm its status.
- No credible thread on moat scoring, pricing-power language, backlog quality, or KPI consistency as a tested text factor. RPO appears once, as a reported Microsoft number beside a disclosure score.
- Look-ahead bias is discussed in the Kim design and in one Alpha Architect summary, not in a useful X thread.

## Which implementation lessons do practitioners actually repeat?

### Takeaway

Four lessons are repeated by more than one serious source: use the change, not the level; rank cross-sectionally and check it is firm-specific rather than a sector wave; cut the filing into sections, with Risk Factors carrying more return information than the full document in the Lazy Prices lineage; and do not expect the return on filing day — the public tests say the drift lasts months. “Dictionary baselines still win” is only half right: generic dictionaries are treated as dead, finance-specific dictionaries remain the baseline, and the one 30-year practitioner replication that still works is a non-LLM similarity score. Timestamping off EDGAR acceptance time, and a special role for charters, were not found in this layer. The look-ahead lesson is stated by the Kim paper (strip name and year) and, in a snippet, by a 2026 Alpha Architect summary (freeze the model’s knowledge date).

### Cited Findings

- **Changes, not levels.** Cohen as restated by Alpha Architect (2016), Research Affiliates (Jaccard versus the year-ago filing, not the level of sentiment), Brain’s second data block (deltas and similarities, which is what Quantpedia’s positive-similarity and QuantConnect’s stability books actually trade), Amenity’s Key Driver (“change in sentiment scores”), and Man AHL’s section on detecting added and deleted risk factors. Robeco’s public charts are levels of call sentiment and are not presented as stock-selection factors. — [RA PDF](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf); [Brain docs](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/writing-algorithms/datasets/brain/brain-language-metrics-on-company-filings); [Man](https://www.man.com/insights/shakespeare-without-the-monkeys)

- **The sign of “change” is not uniform.** Overall and uncertainty-language similarity: changers do worse (Cohen; Padysak’s uncertainty sort; RA). Positive-language similarity: low similarity does better, about 5.5% a year long-short in Padysak. Quant Beckman’s 2026 posts add a third cut that nobody has published a test of: new severe risks are a negative, removed uncertainty after a real turnaround is not the same signal. — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/); [Quant Beckman](https://x.com/quantbeckman/status/2116169414718464225)

- **Cross-section and peers, not a raw score.** RA’s Citigroup example is the clean version: Citi’s similarity collapsed in the 2007 10-K while JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and Bank of America edged up, so the signal was not “banks.” Padysak and the QuantConnect stability book are pure cross-sectional ranks. Acadian’s GEO is judged on peer-relative carbon intensity, not on the absolute volume of green words. — [RA PDF](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf)

- **Segment the filing.** Alpha Architect’s 2016 restatement: most of the editing is in MD&A, but the return is larger in Risk Factors (188 bp/month in the paper, t = 2.76) and in executive and litigation language. Brain and the QuantConnect stability writeup therefore store, and prefer, a risk-factors similarity separate from the full report. Man AHL’s only worked filing example is a risk-factor that was split out of a broader heading. A 2026 academic-style note indexed in search (Choi, Edinburgh, arXiv 2607.14174) finds the opposite aggregation pattern at sector and portfolio level (full 10-K beats Item 1A) and the reverse at the single-stock level (Item 1A better, especially for volatility). That is one unreplicated paper, not a practitioner standard, but it warns against assuming “risk factors always.” — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/); [arXiv 2607.14174](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14174v1)

- **Horizon and the missing announcement effect.** RA: no jump at release; Bucket 1 versus Bucket 3 cumulative abnormal return keeps widening out to 12 months; quarterly rebalance captures more than annual. Quantt’s 2026 restatement of Cohen says the same thing in the original sample (no filing-day return, drift over 6–18 months, no reversal). This is why RA tells long-horizon factor investors to ignore daily news sentiment, which they say decays in hours or days. A news-sentiment blog (Quant Decoded, 21 Mar 2026) saying NLP signals die in 1–5 days is about news, and should not be pasted onto filings. — [RA PDF](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf)

- **Where to use it in a book.** RA’s 2×2 sorts: similarity raises Sharpe inside every classic factor, and the return spread is 3.5–5.3% only inside the speculative half (cheap, aggressive, unprofitable, high-vol, low-momentum). Their recommended job is a value-trap filter, not a standalone 22% long-short. Cheap-and-stable returned 12.3% versus cheap-and-rewritten 8.5% in their exhibit. — [RA PDF](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf)

- **Dictionaries versus models.** Generic psychosocial dictionaries are treated as failed for 10-Ks (Man AHL recounting Loughran-McDonald: ~75% of Harvard negatives are not negative in filings). LM word lists are still the production features inside Brain (uncertainty, litigious, constraining). AQR’s news paper says a supervised score beats both dictionaries and commercial vendor sentiment, on newswires. RA’s live result uses neither: Jaccard on unique terms. Man’s 2019 piece argues word counts systematically miss phrases such as “increase in the dividend,” so a neural or targeted-sentiment layer is the research bet, not a published excess return. — [Man](https://www.man.com/insights/shakespeare-without-the-monkeys); [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Predicting-Returns-with-Text-Data)

- **Do not let the model trade a period it may have memorized.** **[paper-backed design, not a practitioner post-mortem]** Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev: drop the name, replace the year with Period t / Period t−1, and they report that accuracy survives that. Their CoT prompt is itself part of the method (ratios and trends, then a direction), and the naive prompt does not beat analysts. Alpha Architect’s February 2026 summary, unverified past the index snippet, describes a harder version for narrative models: pretrain with a 2007 knowledge cutoff and walk the model forward. No X thread was found in which someone showed a live GPT 10-K backtest collapsing once the cutoff was respected. — [SUERF](https://www.suerf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/SUERF-Policy-Brief-1008_Kim-et-al.pdf); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/ai-tools-financial-disclosures/)

- **Exhibits versus the body.** Only one source, and only as an indexed snippet: the Alpha Architect February 2026 summary says exhibits had about 150% more high-information content than the main filing, and that non-earnings 8-K items (accountant changes, bankruptcy, non-reliance on prior financials) were far denser than earnings releases. Charters were not mentioned. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/ai-tools-financial-disclosures/)

- **EDGAR acceptance timestamp.** Not found. RA’s event study is monthly, aligned to “stocks that had just released a filing.” Padysak explicitly ranks every month on the latest report already in hand, and notes this differs from Cohen’s rule of entering in the month after release. Nobody in this source set states a rule about acceptance datetime versus the filing date stamp.

- **What the signal is not.** RA: it did not flag Enron, WorldCom, or SVB. Fraud that keeps the boilerplate stable, and gaps that open inside a quarter, are outside the feature. Caughran’s claim that quants have already compressed recurring filing patterns sits unresolved next to RA’s stronger 2015–2025 spread.

### Inferences

- A shortlist that starts from what practitioners still defend should put “YoY similarity, section-level, especially risk factors, used inside value and low-quality, held for months” above any LLM prompt. The LLM pitch in public quant writing is that it might explain the edit Jaccard only detects. That second step is not yet a published excess return at any of these firms.
- The implementation error visible in the wild is trading the 22% figure, or trading the level of negativity, or trading the filing-day move. Practitioners who re-estimated the first of those got a few percent in liquid names. The second and third are not supported by the RA or Cohen restatements.
- Post-publication decay is not a blanket verdict. RA says similarity has not decayed. The cyber-risk paper, as quoted by Quantpedia, says that premium was much larger before November 2020. Those are different features.

### Gaps

- No practitioner source in this search said “timestamp with the EDGAR acceptance clock” or “read the charter.” Those may still be right; they are not attested here.
- No one published a horse race of LM delta versus FinBERT delta versus an LLM diff on the same 10-K panel with post-2020 returns.
- Man AHL and S&P Global describe the LLM upgrade and do not show that it adds return after Jaccard.

## Which practitioner features are not in the classic academic list, and do they deserve a look?

### Takeaway

The features with some practitioner evidence that are not just “Cohen again” are: positive-language similarity (sign flips, ~5% long-short in one Brain study through 2020), similarity interacted with value and distress (RA’s trap filter), and cyber-risk proximity of the whole 10-K (a priced characteristic in one paper, weaker after the idea was public). Accounting-estimate changes, customer-concentration extraction, topic-level call sentiment, and greenwash credibility checks are productized and untested as excess-return factors. Moat scores, pricing-power language, AI-adoption language, backlog quality, KPI consistency, and promise-tracking have no public test in this search. The Kim chain-of-thought result is about anonymous numbers, not about narrative, and the paper was withdrawn from arXiv.

### Cited Findings

- **Positive-language similarity, not overall similarity.** **[practitioner/blog backtest]** Padysak / Quantpedia / Brain, through May 2020: low similarity of positive language, long-short about 5.47% a year, vol 6.48%, return/vol 0.84, max drawdown about −10%, large caps. Uncertainty-language similarity kept the Lazy Prices sign in the same project. This is the main “the classic list is too coarse” result a serious blog actually measured. It has not been updated in the public post past 2020. — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)

- **Similarity as a value-trap filter.** **[practitioner backtest]** Not a new feature, a new job for the old one. RA, top 1,000, through September 2025: spread of 3.8% inside cheap stocks (12.3% versus 8.5%), 4.6% inside aggressive investment, 4.6% inside unprofitable, 5.3% inside high volatility, 3.5% inside momentum losers, versus about 1–2% inside the safe halves. They tell the reader to screen unstable narratives out of value, not to run a market-neutral text book as the whole strategy. — [RA PDF](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf)

- **Resolved risk language versus new risk language.** **[social-unverified]** Quant Beckman, October 2026: novelty, severity, and multi-filing persistence of new operational, legal, financing, or going-concern risks as a long-avoidance filter; contraction of old uncertainty language only counts if the business or the balance sheet has actually turned. This is the natural LLM task (classify the edit, do not just measure it) and it has no published return. — [post](https://x.com/quantbeckman/status/2116169414718464225)

- **Disclosure distance and own-history tone, separate from earnings quality.** **[social-unverified]** Alphatica’s October 2026 JPM and MSFT notes treat a rewrite-distance number and a 10- to 15-year tone rank as a signal that can disagree with a beat. Interesting because the two names that month were called in opposite directions. Not a factor until someone shows the series. — [JPM](https://x.com/alphaticaio/status/2109822107664196060); [MSFT](https://x.com/alphaticaio/status/2115511941456069032)

- **Accounting-estimate changes and customer concentration across years.** **[vendor-marketing]** Hudson Labs, December 2025, sells exactly the extraction: server-life cuts and extensions (Amazon, Oracle, 2025) and footnote customer concentration over six 10-Ks. Closest productized cousin of “KPI consistency” and “did the depreciation assumption move.” No return attached. — [Hudson Labs](https://www.hudson-labs.com/post/product-announcement-industry-leading-multi-year-ai-analysis-of-10-ks-press-releases-and-more)

- **Topic-conditioned sentiment on calls (AI, GLP-1, tariffs).** **[practitioner descriptive]** Robeco 2023–2025 quant charts show they can measure it with FinBERT- or GPT-style embeddings. They do not show that a stock’s residual topic sentiment predicts its residual return. A plausible research bet, not a result.

- **Credibility of forward-looking climate language.** **[practitioner research, wrong target variable]** Acadian GEO, February 2023: not “how often they say climate,” but whether plans name dates, baselines, and targets. Predicts next-year peer-relative carbon-intensity declines and SBTi or green-patent status. Does not predict excess returns in the note, and a naive “own the top 20%” book damaged their alpha model. Useful as a warning that a well-built text score can still be a bad stock-selection overlay if it is used as a universe cut. — [Acadian](https://www.acadian-asset.com/investment-insights/esg/a-machine-learning-based-approach-to-thematic-esg-investing)

- **Cyber-risk embedding of the full 10-K.** **[paper-backed, and the blog flags decay]** Celeny and Maréchal as quoted by Quantpedia: long high cyber risk, about 6.93% a year, 18.72% excess in the high quantile, but 11.88% before the November 2020 public mention and weaker after. Dictionary versions are what the authors say they beat. This is an academic feature a practitioner blog chose to amplify, not a vendor product with a live book. — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/cyber-risk-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)

- **Chain-of-thought on anonymous financial statements.** **[paper-backed, withdrawn from arXiv, not a narrative feature]** 60.4% directional accuracy versus 52.7% for analysts at one month, on par with a specialized ANN, and the authors claim a better Sharpe. The input is the statements with the prose removed. Combining the model’s written rationale with the ANN is the incremental result (about 63.2%). Do not file this under “LLM read the 10-K.” The February 2025 withdrawal, reason unpublished in the sources opened here, belongs in any shortlist note. — [SUERF](https://www.suerf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/SUERF-Policy-Brief-1008_Kim-et-al.pdf); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)

- **Lexical density and specific density.** **[blog backtest, thin performance disclosure]** Quantpedia’s claim is that information-dense filings, and filings dense in finance words, beat sparse ones in the top 500. Only a Sharpe of 0.69 was retrieved. Directionally different from “complexity is bad” (Fog, file size). Worth a look only if the unpublished return survives costs; the public record is not enough.

- **AI-mention counts, hedge-word diffs, moat scores, pricing power, RPO or backlog quality, promise-tracking across quarters.** No backtest found. The only concrete public versions are: @xinc3s describing an AI-mention-versus-revenue-line check and a hedge-word diff as a workflow (27 Oct 2026, no test); Alphatica citing Microsoft’s reported RPO as a fundamental beside a bearish filing-tone score (28 Oct 2026); Hudson Labs extracting multi-year estimate changes, which is adjacent to promise-tracking if “promise” means an accounting assumption rather than a CEO sentence. Caughran’s new-CEO prompt (22 Apr 2025) is a management-quality memo, and he does not claim it predicts returns by itself.

- **What looks dead, or at least untrusted, in this layer.** The 188 bp/month Risk Factors long-short as a number anyone should underwrite (RA’s liquid-universe spread is ~2.9%/year and they still like the sign). Generic Harvard tone on 10-Ks (Man / LM). Fog as a readability factor (LM 2014, file size instead). Filing-day event trades (no announcement effect in Cohen-as-restated and in RA). Standalone green-word counts as a stock picker (Acadian: a hard universe cut on GEO cut alpha exposure roughly in half). Any GPT backtest that does not strip identity and time (the Kim design exists because the authors thought memory would otherwise be the story; they say it was not, and the paper was later withdrawn). Cyber-risk text after the premium was publicized (the paper’s own pre/post split).

### Inferences

- For a later shortlist, the practitioner layer supports a small set of filing features that are still believed, in public, with a number attached: YoY similarity (RA, and they say it has not decayed), similarity inside value and low quality (RA), positive-language similarity as a separate and opposite sort (Quantpedia/Brain, stale as of 2020), and section-level LM-class deltas only as dataset features that those tests are built from. Cyber-risk text is believed as a risk premium by one paper and already shows a publication break.
- The LLM-era ideas that deserve a research slot, precisely because vendors and quants describe them and nobody has shown the return, are: classify the risk-factor edit (new and severe versus retired after a real improvement), track accounting-estimate and KPI definition changes across years, and require a revenue or backlog line before giving credit for an AI or pricing-power sentence. Those are the prompts. They are not yet factors.
- Moat scoring and promise-tracking should not be promoted on the basis of this search. They are absent, not disproven.

### Gaps

- No public buy-side backtest of moat, pricing power, AI adoption, backlog or RPO quality, KPI consistency, or cross-quarter promise-tracking.
- No updated Padysak-style positive-similarity result after May 2020, so the sign flip may not have survived the last five years. RA’s overall Jaccard did survive; that does not answer the positive-language question.
- Part 2 of the Research Affiliates NLP series, which is the one that was supposed to cover LLMs, was not available.
- Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev’s Sharpe and alpha are described as higher than the alternatives and are not printed in the SUERF brief. The arXiv withdrawal reason is unknown.
