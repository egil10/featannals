# LLM-era evidence: filing and disclosure scores versus future excess returns

Scope note for the report writer. This memo separates published or still-circulating results from withdrawn drafts and from secondary write-ups. Every return figure below is the source’s own number and sample. Nothing here is a recommended feature shortlist. As of 2026-10-30, the two best-known ChatGPT-and-filings papers from Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev have been withdrawn by the authors on arXiv. Do not cite their alphas as established.

## Which LLM measures, built from filings or earnings releases, have a documented long-short or alpha after the text is public?

### Takeaway
Almost none of the headline LLM-and-returns results are a clean, post-publication long-short on 10-K or 10-Q text. The flagship GPT financial-statement strategy was withdrawn after a failed internal replication. The one still-circulating generative-AI risk paper is about earnings-call transcripts, not filings, and its equity premia are short-sample and only partly significant. A 2026 arXiv paper finds a modest next-month alpha from LLM-tracked metric shifting in earnings calls, not in annual reports. Headline papers (Lopez-Lira and Tang) show next-day drift in news, which does not transfer to annual reports.

### Cited Findings
- Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev, “Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models” (arXiv:2407.17866), was withdrawn by Valeri Nikolaev on 20 February 2025 (version 3). The arXiv comment states: “A co-author identified inconsistencies in the data and analyses while attempting to replicate past analyses from the working paper. Accordingly, we have temporarily withdrawn the working paper from circulation while we review the research findings.” No PDF is available on the current record. The record was still withdrawn when checked for this memo. — [arXiv:2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)
- The withdrawn draft (HTML of version 2, 10 November 2024) is not usable as evidence. For the record of what was claimed, and why it is not a 10-K result: the input was anonymized, standardized balance sheets and income statements (Compustat balancing model; years relabeled \(t\) and \(t-1\); no narrative and no firm name), fed to GPT-4 Turbo with a chain-of-thought prompt. The target was the direction of next year’s earnings, not a textual score of the filing. Full sample 1968–2021, 150,678 firm-years, 15,401 firms. Analyst comparison sample 1983–2021, 39,533 observations, 3,152 firms. Directional accuracy: naive prior-change model 49%; analysts one month after the earnings release about 53%; analysts one-to-three months later 56% and four-to-six months later 57%; non-CoT GPT 52%; CoT GPT 60% on the analyst sample and 60.31% accuracy / 63.45% F1 on the full sample, versus an ANN at 60.45% accuracy / about 61.6% F1. A SUERF policy brief (16 October 2024), written before the withdrawal, quotes a median analyst accuracy of 52.7%. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2); [SUERF Policy Brief 1008](https://www.suerf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/SUERF-Policy-Brief-1008_Kim-et-al.pdf)
- Trading design in that same withdrawn draft: portfolios formed 30 June and held one year, after December fiscal-year results. Equal-weighted monthly Sharpe ratios reported as 3.36 (GPT), 2.54 (ANN), and 2.05 (logistic). Value-weighted: GPT 1.47, ANN 1.79, logistic 0.81. After 10 basis points of transaction costs, the GPT equal-weighted Sharpe is reported as 2.84 and the value-weighted Sharpe as 0.95. Equal-weighted six-factor (Fama–French five factors plus momentum) monthly alpha reported as 84 basis points, which the text calls 10% annually; the introduction says the Fama–French three-factor alpha “exceeds 12% per year.” Value-weighted six-factor alpha reported as 37 basis points (t = 2.43). ANN equal-weighted six-factor alpha 60 basis points (t = 1.89); logistic 43 basis points (t = 1.96); ANN value-weighted about 50 basis points. These figures come from a withdrawn draft whose own monthly Sharpe of 3.36 is not a credible live equity Sharpe, and they are not a strategy in 10-K language. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2)
- Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev, “From Transcripts to Insights: Uncovering Corporate Risks Using Generative AI” (arXiv:2310.17721, revised March 2025), is still listed on the authors’ arXiv page without a withdrawal comment, unlike the two papers below. Model: GPT-3.5-Turbo. Text: earnings conference-call transcripts, January 2018–March 2023, not 10-Ks or 10-Qs. The model writes a risk summary (document only) and a risk assessment (document plus the model’s general knowledge) for political, climate, and AI risk. The main validity test is future volatility (90-day implied volatility and abnormal realized volatility), not return. GPT measures subsume bigram dictionaries. Assessments dominate summaries. A one-standard-deviation increase in political-risk assessment is associated with 0.04 standard deviations of implied volatility and 0.06 standard deviations of abnormal volatility (Table 3, column 5, Panel A). Results are also reported on calls from January 2022–March 2023, after the model’s September 2021 training cutoff. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.17721); [author arXiv list](https://arxiv.org/a/kim_a_2.html)
- Equity pricing in that risk paper uses the full January 2018–March 2023 window, including the in-training period. Annualized risk is the average of the four quarterly assessment scores; zero-exposure firm-years are dropped. Portfolios are formed on 31 March of the following year (a delay of three or more months) and held one year. Equal-weighted quintile high-minus-low Fama–French five-factor alphas, as stated in the text: political risk 5.28% annualized (t = 1.51, not significant); climate risk 6.72% annualized (t = 1.90); AI risk 6.36% annualized (t = 2.31). Fama–MacBeth monthly-return coefficients on log annualized assessment, with size, book-to-market, profitability, investment, and past-return controls: climate 0.211 and AI 0.317, significant at conventional levels; political 0.077 (t = 1.36). The authors say the short sample likely understates t-statistics. A Chicago Booth research brief rounds this to strategies that “outperform their benchmark indices by around 5% each year,” which is coarser than the paper and should not be quoted in place of the three separate alphas. — [arXiv HTML, section 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.17721); [BFI brief, October 2023](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/From-Transcripts-to-Insights.pdf)
- The method does not automatically transfer to 10-K Item 1A. The paper never runs the prompt on filings. Its incremental value is the model’s judgment on top of the transcript (“assessments” beat “summaries” and beat Hassan-style and Sautner-style bigrams). Item 1A is already an enumerated risk list, so a summary prompt has less to add than it does on a call, and the general-knowledge assessment is exactly the channel Sarkar and Vafa show can leak the future (see failures below).
- Choi and coauthors, “From Text to Alpha: Can LLMs Track Evolving Signals in Corporate Disclosures?” (arXiv:2510.03195 version 4, 11 March 2026), is a still-circulating preprint, not a journal article. Text: earnings-call transcripts, not 10-Ks. Design: an LLM extracts performance metrics with contextual qualifiers; an embedding model measures semantic similarity to the same fiscal quarter one year earlier. The economic book buys low metric-shifting and sells high metric-shifting. Reported spread, which the conclusion calls the long-short five-factor alpha: excess return −0.41% (t = −2.08), three-factor alpha −0.48% (t = −2.40), five-factor alpha −0.52% (t = −2.55). The NER/string-match baseline from Cohen and Nguyen (2024) is insignificant in their tests, and they say the LLM alpha is more than twice that baseline. Fama–MacBeth tests use next-month returns. The pages retrieved do not name the LLM or the sample years, and they do not show whether −0.52% is high-minus-low or low-minus-high. Do not flip the sign. Transfer to MD&A year-over-year metric emphasis is plausible and untested. — [arXiv HTML v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v4)
- Lopez-Lira and Tang, “Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements? Return Predictability and Large Language Models,” is published in the Journal of Financial Economics, 2026, volume 184 (doi:10.1016/j.jfineco.2026.104335). It is a news-headline paper. Sample: headlines for 4,123 U.S. common stocks, October 2021–May 2024, chosen because the evaluated model’s knowledge cutoff is September 2021. GPT-4’s daily portfolio hit rate for the direction of the initial reaction is 93.3% for overnight headlines and 88.8% for intraday headlines; the abstract calls the initial reaction approximately 90% and explicitly “non-tradable.” Scores also predict a subsequent drift, stronger for small stocks and for negative news. Forecasting ability rises with model size. Strategy returns decline as LLM adoption rises. An Alpha Architect summary of the same JFE paper reports a daily-rebalanced overnight long-short pre-cost mean of 34 basis points per day, a 58% hit rate, and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 2.97, and 50 basis points, a 55% hit rate, and a Sharpe ratio of 2.63 for intraday news; those magnitudes were not on the journal abstract page retrieved here and should be treated as a secondary reading of the tables. This paper does not show that an annual-report or 10-Q score predicts returns. Headlines are short, the edge is mostly the opening print plus one to two days, and the asset is news, not a filing. — [ScienceDirect / JFE](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X26001066); [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/chatgpt-forecast-stock-price/)
- A 2025–2026 study of earnings press releases (arXiv:2509.24254; more than 138,000 releases, 2005–2023) finds FinBERT soft information explains announcement-day returns about as well as the earnings surprise. Earnings surprise alone has an \(R^2\) of 3.3%; adding the soft-information variable raises \(R^2\) as high as 4.4%. That is same-day explanatory power, not a future excess return after the release is public. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.24254v1)

### Inferences
- A feature is not “documented alpha after the text is public” unless three things are true at once: the score is a function of text that investors could read, the portfolio is formed after that text’s timestamp, and the paper has not been withdrawn. On that standard, the surviving candidates are the call-transcript risk assessments (marginal, equal-weighted, short sample) and the call-transcript metric-shift spread (about half a percent per month in a preprint, sign convention not fully checked). Neither is a 10-K result.
- The withdrawn financial-statement strategy would not have answered the filing question even if the numbers had replicated. It scores Compustat numbers, forms portfolios on 30 June, and never reads Item 1, Item 1A, or MD&A.
- Headline drift is the wrong base case for a filing feature. It is a one-to-two-day, small-stock, bad-news phenomenon in text that is already a headline. A 10-K score that only works if traded in the first minutes after acceptance is a different product, and these papers do not show that product.

### Gaps
- Model name, sample window, universe filters, and the long/short leg definition for arXiv:2510.03195 were not on the pages retrieved. The −0.52% figure should be re-read from the portfolio table before anyone uses it.
- Net-of-cost and value-weighted results for the risk-assessment premia are not reported in the sections retrieved. The 5–7% annualized figures are equal-weighted five-factor alphas before explicit trading costs.
- No retrieved paper shows a long-short on earnings-release text (as opposed to the earnings number) that survives after the announcement day and is net of costs.
- Journal status of the risk paper beyond arXiv was not confirmed. It is not in the withdrawn set.

## Verification of the named papers, later critiques, and the 2023–2026 task papers

### Takeaway
Two Kim–Muhn–Nikolaev papers that the public discussion still cites were withdrawn in 2025 for failed replication. The risk paper was not. Look-ahead bias in pretrained models is a documented failure mode, not a blog worry: models emit “COVID-19” from 2019 calls, and in-sample headline predictability is partly a memorization interaction that dies after the training cutoff. FinBERT, Garcia–Hu–Rohrer, and Cao–Jiang–Yang–Zhang do not deliver a tradable LLM score of the 10-K. No retrieved 2023–2026 paper trades an LLM score of moat, risk-factor materiality, guidance credibility, section-to-section contradiction, or maintenance-versus-growth capex.

### Cited Findings
- Withdrawal of the financial-statement paper: 20 February 2025, reason as quoted in the previous section. There is no evidence in the sources checked that it was published in the Journal of Accounting Research. Secondary pages in 2024 called it a Chicago Booth working paper. A SUERF brief in October 2024 repeats the pre-withdrawal abstract and is not a replication. — [arXiv:2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)
- The draft’s own look-ahead checks, which did not survive as citable results: inputs were anonymized numbers; the model could not guess the firm name or fiscal year, unlike Sarkar and Vafa’s test on anonymous text; a 2023 earnings prediction, outside the training window, was described as “similarly strong” on a smaller sample; performance was not especially good in the financial crisis or COVID, which the authors treat as inconsistent with memory of those episodes. GPT-3.5 was “considerably less impressive”; Gemini Pro was described as similar to GPT-4. None of this rehabilitates a withdrawn paper. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2)
- Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev, “Bloated Disclosures: Can ChatGPT Help Investors Process Information?” (arXiv:2306.10224), was withdrawn on 9 October 2025 (version 5). The comment states: “A co-author attempted to independently replicate key results from the working paper using the described approach, but the analyses did not yield results supporting the reported findings. Accordingly, we have withdrawn the working paper from circulation.” The abstract had claimed that unconstrained summaries of complex disclosures are shorter, more polarized in sentiment, and better at explaining stock-market reactions, and that a “bloat” measure is associated with lower price efficiency and higher information asymmetry. A January 2024 draft’s keywords show the text was MD&A and conference calls. The abstract’s market test is contemporaneous reaction, not a future long-short. Do not cite any magnitude from it. — [arXiv:2306.10224](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10224); [January 2024 draft front page via Google Scholar cache](https://scholar.googleusercontent.com/scholar?q=cache:k5XbiWk1enMJ:scholar.google.com/&hl=en&as_sdt=0,5)
- Sarkar and Vafa, “Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models” (SSRN, 28 June 2024; first version 11 March 2024). Direct test: events that were unpredictable at the information date should not appear in model output. They prompt Llama 2-70B with earnings calls from September–November 2019 and ask for 2020 risk factors. “COVID-19,” a string that did not exist at the call date, appears in 6.8% of generations. The Zoom call of 5 September 2019 produces “COVID-19 pandemic” and a remote-work competition narrative. They also find leakage in close U.S. House elections from candidate biographies. They argue that prompting does not reliably remove the bias, and that the fix is a model whose pretraining corpus contains only language produced before the analysis date and is free of survivorship bias. This is the critique the financial-statement draft tried to answer, and it is aimed at risk-from-text designs of the kind used in the transcripts paper. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4754678); [NBER conference draft](https://users.nber.org/~confer/conf_papers/2055/f205530/f205530.pdf)
- “The Memorization Problem” (arXiv:2504.14765; first version 15 April 2025, version dated 24 August 2026 in the HTML header) finds that LLMs recall exact pre-cutoff values of economic series, including index levels, unemployment, and GDP, with the errors concentrated after the cutoff. A later benchmark paper attributes to Lopez-Lira et al. (2025) the result that GPT-4o recalls S&P 500 closes with less than 1% error inside the training window and that errors “explode” after the cutoff. Author names on arXiv:2504.14765 were not on the page snippet retrieved here; do not invent them. The implication they state is that backtests inside the training window cannot separate forecasting from recall. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v1); [Look-Ahead-Bench citation of that result](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13770v1)
- “Detecting Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts” (arXiv:2512.23847 version 2) builds a look-ahead propensity score that is positive through the training window and collapses after the cutoff (illustrated for Llama-3.3-70B, December 2023 cutoff). Applied to the Lopez-Lira and Tang headline design on the in-sample window, a one-step move in the LLM signal predicts about 0.21% higher next-day return (one reported specification: 0.197%). A one-standard-deviation increase in look-ahead propensity raises the marginal effect by 0.077%, about 37% of the standalone effect. The interaction is statistically zero on the 2024 post-cutoff sample. A second application is earnings-call transcripts predicting capital expenditures, with the same pattern: amplification on high-propensity firm-dates, gone after the cutoff. Author names were not confirmed in the retrieved text. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v2)
- Glasserman and Lin, “Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis” (arXiv:2309.17322, September 2023), study GPT sentiment on news, not filings. They separate look-ahead (the model knows the return that followed the article) from a distraction effect (general knowledge of the named firm contaminates the sentiment read). Inside the training window, anonymized headlines outperform named headlines, so distraction dominates look-ahead, especially for large firms. Anonymization is therefore useful both for de-biased backtests and for live use. This does not clear filing backtests. It shows that two biases can offset, so a positive in-sample alpha is not evidence that memory is absent. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)
- Look-Ahead-Bench (arXiv:2601.13770; Mostapha Benhenda) is a trading-benchmark paper, not a filing study. Standard Llama 3.1 and DeepSeek models show alpha decay of more than 15 percentage points from the in-window period to a later window; the table reports Llama 3.1 70B decay of −15.25 points and DeepSeek 3.2 decay of −21.77 points, while the author’s point-in-time models stay near zero decay. Treat the table as evidence that in-window LLM trading alphas fail a cutoff test in that benchmark, not as a 10-K result. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13770v1)
- Huang, Wang, and Yang, “FinBERT: A Large Language Model for Extracting Information from Financial Text,” Contemporary Accounting Research 40 (2023): 806–841. This is a BERT model fine-tuned for finance, not a generative chatbot. On researcher-labeled sentences from analyst reports, out-of-sample sentiment accuracy is 88.2%, versus 62.1% for the Loughran–McDonald dictionary, 73.6% naïve Bayes, 72.6% SVM, 71.9% random forest, 75.1% CNN, and 76.3% LSTM. Negative-sentiment accuracy is 89.7%, versus under 60% for the non-BERT algorithms. The advantage is largest in small training samples and on finance-specific words. FinBERT also beats other models on ESG sentence identification. Other methods “underestimate the textual informativeness of earnings conference calls by at least 18%.” The paper does not report a long-short portfolio on 10-Ks, MD&A, or earnings releases. Araci’s earlier “FinBERT” is a different model, trained for sentiment on the Financial PhraseBank; it is a classification benchmark, not a return paper. A 2025 master’s thesis at the Higher School of Economics claims an MD&A FinBERT score relates to future S&P 500 returns; that is not a refereed result and is not used here as evidence. — [CAR / Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/1911-3846.12832); [Google Scholar record](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_lookup?title=FinBERT:+A+large+language+model+for+extracting+information+from+financial+text&author=Huang,+A.H.&author=Wang,+H.&author=Yang,+Y.&publication_year=2023&journal=Contemp.+Account.+Res.&volume=40&pages=806-841&doi=10.1111/1911-3846.12832)
- Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer, “The colour of finance words,” Journal of Financial Economics 147 (2023): 525–549. This is not an LLM. They extend Taddy’s (2013) multinomial inverse regression, with cross-validation, and color words by the stock-price reaction they accompany. The dictionaries are estimated on financial text and validated on returns around earnings announcements. A baseline regression of the earnings-call price reaction has an \(R^2\) of 1.7%. Loughran–McDonald terms that do not overlap the machine-learned list raise that to 2.1%. The machine-learned list that does not overlap Loughran–McDonald reaches 4.6% (bigrams 4.5%). The overlap list has the largest univariate \(R^2\), 5.4%, despite being the smallest list. In multivariate regressions the Loughran–McDonald terms lose marginal significance and sometimes load with the wrong sign. Keywords include earnings calls, 10-Ks, and the Wall Street Journal, but the \(R^2\) figures above are for the earnings-call event, not a post-filing holding-period alpha. Because the supervisor is the price reaction, the dictionary is not a pure ex ante text score; the cross-validation layer is their guard against overfitting. A 2025 Pacific-Basin Finance Journal paper reports that it replicates the U.S. result and extends the lexicon to China. — [JFE](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X22002422); [author PDF](https://file-lianxh.oss-cn-shenzhen.aliyuncs.com/Refs/Text/Garcia_2023_JFE_The-colour-of-finance-words.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3630898)
- Cao, Jiang, Yang, and Zhang, “How to Talk When a Machine Is Listening: Corporate Disclosure in the Age of AI,” Review of Financial Studies 36, no. 9 (2023): 3603–3642 (NBER w27950). Firms with more expected machine readers file documents that are easier for machines to parse and avoid words that algorithms, as opposed to human dictionaries, score as negative. Loughran and McDonald (2011) and, in the SSRN abstract, BERT from 2018 are the event studies used to attribute the drop in measured negativity to machine readership. The effect is stronger when the benefit of sentiment management is higher (external financing) or the cost is lower (litigation risk). NBER’s non-technical summary: mechanical downloads of 10-K and 10-Q filings rose from 360,861 in 2003 to around 165 million in 2016. The paper does not estimate a long-short alpha from an LLM reading of the filing. It is evidence that dictionary tone on filings is a manipulated object. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27950); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3683802)
- Kim and Nikolaev, “Contextualizing Profitability” (SSRN, dated 1 June 2024; Chicago Booth Research Paper 23-11; BFI Working Paper 2023-76, June 2023 draft title “Profitability Context and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns”). A language model (they name BERT, transformers, and neural nets, not a chatbot prompt) folds narrative context into operating profitability. The SSRN abstract says contextualized profitability outperforms conventional profitability statistically and economically, because the model separates transitory from persistent profit, and that the resulting factor eliminates the alpha on small extreme-growth portfolios, described as the largest failure of the Fama–French five-factor model. In the June 2023 draft, the three-factor alpha on small high-growth stocks is −0.51; adding profitability and investment factors cuts it to −0.29; replacing profitability with the context-adjusted factor cuts it to −0.13 and the alpha loses significance. The draft does not, in the pages retrieved, label those units as percent per month, so they are quoted as printed. This is the closest still-circulating Booth paper to “narrative in the annual report changes a return predictor,” and it is a contextual adjustment to a fundamental factor, not a standalone LLM long-short. Journal publication was not confirmed. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4459383); [BFI WP 2023-76](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/BFI_WP_2023-76.pdf)
- Kim, Muhn, Nikolaev, and Zhang, “Learning Fundamentals from Text” (SSRN, posted December 2024, revised 24 February 2025). An attention model learns which annual-report topics the market reacts to. Segment information, goodwill and intangibles, revenues, and operating income draw the most reaction; sustainability and governance are among the least important. That is contemporaneous information content, not a future-return strategy, and it was not in the withdrawn set in the sources checked. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5047947)
- Shaffer and Wang, “Harnessing Large Language Models for Core Earnings Measurement” (Harvard Business School working paper; also circulated as “Scaling Core Earnings Measurement with Large Language Models,” described as October 2024). Text: 10-K filings of U.S. public companies, 2000–2023, via the GPT-4o API. Two prompts. The out-of-the-box prompt, given only a definition of core earnings and the full 10-K, conflates core earnings with other constructs such as EBITDA. A sequential prompt (identify unusual losses, then unusual gains, then tabulate and aggregate) produces a measure that, in the HBS abstract, outperforms GAAP net income and Compustat’s OPEPS and OIADP in predicting average future earnings in most of their tests. Alpha Architect’s summary of the paper, which is secondary, reports an autoregressive coefficient of 0.917 for the sequential measure versus 0.849 for GAAP net income, with Compustat OPEPS and OIADP slightly higher at 1.0174 and 1.0178; mean absolute error of $1.58 versus $1.77 for GAAP net income and $1.56 for OPEPS; next-period net-income \(R^2\) of 70.86% versus 60.87% for GAAP net income; two-year \(R^2\) of 83.60% versus 66.57% for OPEPS; the sequential measure above GAAP net income only 61% of the time, versus 87% for OIADP and 64% for OPEPS; median gap versus GAAP under 10 cents per share, versus 90 cents for OIADP. No stock-return long-short is reported in the HBS abstract or the Columbia Blue Sky summary. — [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67100); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/can-artificial-intelligence-outsmart-seasoned-equity-analysts/); [CLS Blue Sky, 28 February 2025](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2025/02/28/scaling-core-earnings-measurement-with-large-language-models/)
- Searches for 2023–2026 papers that trade an LLM score of moat, risk-factor materiality, guidance credibility, contradiction between sections, or maintenance-versus-growth capex did not return a primary paper with a post-filing long-short. What did appear, and should not be upgraded into that evidence: a practitioner essay on what to ignore in 10-Ks (boilerplate risk factors) with no portfolio test; Bar-Hava (SSRN, December 2025), an interpretive study of non-GAAP narrative “loops” at five large-cap firms, with no return test; a LinkedIn discussion of GAAP-tag errors on FinTagging (DeepSeek-V3 F1 0.724 on extraction and 0.172 on tag selection; GPT-4o 0.602 and 0.166), which is an accuracy benchmark, not a return paper. — [FutureSearch essay](https://futuresearch.ai/ai-10k-analysis/); [Bar-Hava SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5841742)

### Inferences
- The replication failures are the central fact of this literature as of October 2026. “Bloated Disclosures” failed a co-author’s replication of the reported findings. The financial-statement paper was pulled for inconsistencies found while replicating. Secondary write-ups from 2023–2024 are stale.
- Look-ahead is not binary. Glasserman and Lin find that, on headlines, firm-specific memory can hurt sentiment accuracy (distraction) even while outcome memory should help. Sarkar and Vafa show leakage on the exact task the risk paper performs. The risk paper’s volatility results on 2022–23 calls are the right kind of check; its asset-pricing sample is not, because it pools 2018–2021 with the post-cutoff window.
- Cao, Jiang, Yang, and Zhang imply that any filing feature which is just “more positive than Loughran–McDonald” is partly a choice variable. Firms already avoid the words machines flag. A score that correlates with machine-negative word lists will be gamed. A score that asks a question managers cannot cheaply manipulate (a contradiction, a changed risk that is firm-specific, a non-GAAP addback that recurs) is the kind of task the strategic-disclosure result leaves open. That is a design implication, not a demonstrated alpha.
- Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer’s gain over Loughran–McDonald is real and narrow: higher \(R^2\) for the earnings-call announcement return, from a price-supervised dictionary. It is not evidence that a generative model reads the 10-K and earns a holding-period premium.
- FinBERT’s published contribution is classification accuracy on analyst-report sentences and a better measure of call informativeness. Using it as a 10-K sentiment factor and trading the score is an untested extra step. The announcement-day press-release \(R^2\) (3.3% to 4.4%) shows the text has same-day information. It does not show drift.

### Gaps
- No independent, published replication of the transcripts risk premia was found. The 2022–23 volatility check is the authors’ own.
- Author names and the final journal version, if any, of arXiv:2504.14765 and arXiv:2512.23847 were not confirmed beyond the titles and the Lopez-Lira attribution in Look-Ahead-Bench.
- “Contextualizing Profitability” return magnitudes beyond the small-growth alpha path (−0.51, −0.29, −0.13) were not retrieved, and the units are not labeled in the extracted sentences.
- No primary paper was found that pits an LLM “what changed in the 10-K” score against Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen’s similarity measures in a horse race and then trades the residual.
- Moat, guidance credibility, inter-section contradiction, boilerplate-versus-specific risk, and maintenance-versus-growth capex: no return paper found. Absence from these searches is not proof none exists; it is a gap.

## Which extraction tasks are LLMs actually better at than dictionaries and bag-of-words?

### Takeaway
The supported advantages are contextual sentiment classification, pulling recurring versus unusual items out of a full 10-K when the prompt is procedural, and matching performance metrics that have been reworded. They show up as higher classification accuracy, better prediction of future earnings, or a modest improvement on an existing call-transcript anomaly. They do not show up as a replicated long-short on filings. Generative “judgment” that adds facts from outside the document is the task most contaminated by training-set leakage.

### Cited Findings
- Sentiment in context, versus a dictionary: FinBERT’s 88.2% accuracy versus 62.1% for Loughran–McDonald is on labeled analyst-report sentences, with the largest gap on sentences other models call neutral and on negative sentences (89.7% versus under 60%). The paper’s explanation is context, not a longer word list. Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer’s machine-learned lists, which are not neural LLMs but are the published price-supervised alternative to bag-of-words, raise earnings-call announcement \(R^2\) from 2.1% (Loughran–McDonald only) to 4.6%, and the overlapping terms dominate Loughran–McDonald in multivariate regressions. — [Huang, Wang, and Yang, CAR 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/1911-3846.12832); [Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer, JFE 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X22002422)
- Numerical claim extraction and non-GAAP-style addbacks: Shaffer and Wang’s sequential prompt on the full 10-K is the direct evidence. The model is instructed to list unusual losses, then unusual gains, then aggregate. The resulting core-earnings series predicts future earnings better than GAAP net income in the authors’ tests and is not systematically income-increasing the way Compustat operating income is (61% of cases above GAAP, median gap under 10 cents, in the secondary summary). The same model with one unconstrained prompt fails and drifts toward EBITDA. So the task is feasible, and the prompt is part of the estimator. This is an earnings-prediction result, not a return result. — [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67100); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/can-artificial-intelligence-outsmart-seasoned-equity-analysts/)
- “What changed,” at the level of metrics rather than bag-of-words overlap: Choi et al. show that embedding similarity on LLM-extracted metrics beats spaCy NER plus string matching on the Cohen and Nguyen moving-target measure, with a five-factor spread of −0.52% (t = −2.55) against an insignificant NER baseline. The stated mechanism is that the LLM keeps qualifiers (“total revenue” versus a product-specific revenue) that NER collapses, and the embedding treats paraphrases as the same metric. The corpus is earnings calls. — [arXiv:2510.03195v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v4)
- Risk identification beyond keywords: on calls, GPT-3.5 assessments beat both GPT summaries and topic bigrams for subsequent volatility, including for political and climate risk, and the authors’ example is a regulatory issue (handset unbundling) that the transcript does not label as political risk but the model infers. That is the “uniquely LLM” claim in the cleanest form they offer: the model joins two pieces of the document and a piece of outside knowledge. The same outside knowledge is what produces “COVID-19” from a 2019 call in Sarkar and Vafa. Post-cutoff volatility tests mitigate that; they do not remove it from any backtest that includes pre-cutoff calls. — [arXiv:2310.17721](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.17721); [Sarkar and Vafa](https://users.nber.org/~confer/conf_papers/2055/f205530/f205530.pdf)
- What the market actually reacts to inside annual reports, from a different Booth paper: segment information, goodwill and intangibles, revenues, and operating income, not sustainability or governance. That ranks topics by contemporaneous reaction. It does not say an LLM summary of those topics predicts future returns. — [Learning Fundamentals from Text, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5047947)
- Persistent versus transitory profit, using narrative around the number: contextualized profitability is built for that distinction and is the channel Kim and Nikolaev claim for the improvement over operating profitability. Again, the traded object is a hybrid fundamental, not a text score. — [BFI WP 2023-76](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/BFI_WP_2023-76.pdf)
- Tasks with no return evidence in the sources searched: peer comparison, boilerplate versus firm-specific risk, maintenance versus growth capex, contradiction between sections, guidance credibility, and economic moat. Tag-level numerical extraction is a documented weakness, not a strength: on the FinTagging description retrieved second-hand, generative models extract the number and then assign the wrong GAAP concept most of the time (tag-selection accuracy about 0.17). — [Bar-Hava does not test returns](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5841742)

### Inferences
- Relative to dictionaries, the evidenced edge is disambiguation and structured extraction, not “the model understands the business.” FinBERT and the Garcia lexicons win on labeling. Shaffer and Wang win on a checklist a careful accountant could write down. Choi et al. win on paraphrase-robust change detection. None of these is a moat score.
- The tasks that look most LLM-specific and that a dictionary cannot do — inferring a risk the document does not name, judging credibility, declaring a moat — are also the tasks where pretraining leakage and prompt wording move the output. Sarkar and Vafa’s 6.8% COVID rate is the concrete example. Shaffer and Wang’s EBITDA failure is the concrete prompt example.
- A dictionary or a cosine similarity on MD&A will already capture a large “something changed” effect (next section). An LLM feature is only additive if it measures a change the overlap statistics miss, such as a reworded metric or a recurring addback. That is a hypothesis consistent with Choi et al. and Shaffer and Wang. It is not a completed horse race against lazy prices.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head of FinBERT sentiment versus Loughran–McDonald on a post-filing return horizon was found in a journal paper.
- No test was found of contradiction detection, peer comparison, or capex classification against future returns or even against human labels at scale.
- The FinTagging accuracies are from a secondary LinkedIn summary of a Stanford/Columbia benchmark, not from the benchmark paper itself. Use them only as a lead.

## Implementation constraints that decide whether a feature is usable

### Takeaway
The binding constraints in the papers that actually ran are prompt design, training-cutoff leakage, and the timestamp of the text, not an abstract context-window limit. A single pass over a full 10-K fails at core-earnings extraction. A chain-of-thought or sequential prompt changes the answer by enough to create or destroy the result. Portfolio formation months after the fiscal year-end, as in the withdrawn draft and in the risk paper, is not the same object as a trade on the EDGAR acceptance timestamp.

### Cited Findings
- Full-document prompting is not reliable even when the model accepts the input. Shaffer and Wang pass the full 10-K to GPT-4o. The one-shot prompt conflates core earnings with EBITDA. Three serial passes with a fixed procedure are what produce the measure that predicts future earnings. The withdrawn financial-statement draft’s own companion note says that a step-by-step 10-K and 10-Q prompt “is more prone to retrieval errors, and the accuracy of the information must be verified.” — [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67100); [arXiv HTML v2, companion-app footnote](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2)
- Prompt and model sensitivity is large where it was measured. In the withdrawn draft, non-CoT GPT accuracy is 52% and CoT accuracy is 60%; GPT-3.5 is materially worse; the authors say the result should not be taken for granted. Shaffer and Wang’s one-shot versus sequential split is the same fact on a real 10-K task that was not withdrawn. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2); [CLS Blue Sky](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2025/02/28/scaling-core-earnings-measurement-with-large-language-models/)
- Anonymization and “ignore any knowledge after date T” do not fix leakage. Sarkar and Vafa say prompting-based countermeasures are limited, and they recommend a model trained only on text from before the decision date. Glasserman and Lin find anonymization changes headline-sentiment returns, but because it removes distraction, not because it proves the backtest is clean. The look-ahead-propensity test finds the headline signal’s extra power is concentrated in high-memorization firm-dates and vanishes in 2024. — [Sarkar and Vafa](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4754678); [Glasserman and Lin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322); [arXiv:2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v2)
- As-of timing in the papers that report portfolios is deliberately slow, and still easy to get wrong. The withdrawn financial-statement strategy waits until 30 June for December year-ends. The risk paper averages the year’s calls and trades on 31 March of the next year. Lazy prices, the non-LLM benchmark, finds no announcement return at the filing and a drift that arrives only when later news or earnings confirm the change. A score formed at period-end, or at the earnings announcement date, is not available at the 10-K. A score formed at the stamped filing date but traded before the EDGAR acceptance time is look-ahead of a more ordinary kind. — [arXiv HTML v2, section 7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2); [risk paper, section 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.17721); [Lazy Prices SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)
- Machine readers already consume filings at scale, which is both a cost fact and a strategic fact. Mechanical downloads of 10-Ks and 10-Qs rose from 360,861 in 2003 to around 165 million in 2016, and firms adjusted wording in response. Any production feature that uses the same surface cues as widely deployed sentiment tools is reading text that has been written for it. — [NBER w27950](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27950)
- Section choice matters for a non-LLM measure and is a reasonable prior for an LLM measure. Lazy prices’ publisher abstracts single out MD&A (HBS/Journal of Finance abstract) and, in the SSRN abstract, language about the CEO and CFO, litigation, and the risk-factor section as the informative slices. The attention paper on annual reports singles out segments, goodwill, revenue, and operating income, and not ESG. Dumping the entire 10-K into one prompt mixes those slices with boilerplate. — [HBS Lazy Prices](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [SSRN Lazy Prices](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078); [Learning Fundamentals from Text](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5047947)

### Inferences
- Usable design, given only what these papers support: segment the filing (MD&A, Item 1A, the financial-statement notes) before the model sees it; use a procedural prompt with an audit trail of extracted spans, as in Shaffer and Wang, rather than a single holistic score; freeze the model or use a point-in-time model for any backtest; start the holding period only after the EDGAR acceptance timestamp, and if that timestamp is after the cash close, the next session. The after-the-close convention is market structure, not a number quoted from these papers.
- Cost and determinism were not measured in the sources retrieved. The economic constraint that was measured is replication: two widely presented GPT-on-disclosure designs did not reproduce. A feature that depends on a proprietary chat model’s default temperature is in the same class as the withdrawn CoT result until it is re-run on a frozen model.
- Context-window marketing is not the constraint. GPT-4o accepted the full 10-K in Shaffer and Wang and still failed the one-shot task. The failure mode is retrieval and task definition inside a long document, which their sequential design and the Booth companion note both flag.

### Gaps
- No retrieved paper reports dollars per filing, failure rates by 10-K length, or the share of 10-Ks that exceed a given context window.
- No retrieved paper reports seed-level or temperature-level stability of a filing score.
- The exact EDGAR field to use (acceptance datetime versus filing date, and the 16:00 Eastern cutoff) was not re-read from Loughran and McDonald or the Notre Dame SRAF documentation in this pass. The portfolio-timing facts above are from the strategy papers, not from a data manual.

## How large are these returns next to lazy prices, accruals, and post-earnings-announcement drift?

### Takeaway
The textual benchmark that does not need an LLM is larger and better published than any surviving LLM-filing result. Lazy prices, on 10-K and 10-Q changes from 1995–2014, reports monthly alphas up to 188 basis points in the most informative slices, with no reaction on the filing day itself. The accruals hedge, in a 2011 Management Science paper, had by then decayed to the point it was no longer reliably positive. Classic post-earnings drift is a multi-week to multi-quarter phenomenon tied to the earnings number; the LLM headline result is a one-to-two-day news drift and should not be compared with it as if it were the same object.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen, “Lazy Prices,” Journal of Finance 75, no. 3 (June 2020): 1371–1415. Sample: regular quarterly and annual filings, 1995–2014. A portfolio that shorts firms that change their language and buys firms that do not earns up to 188 basis points in monthly alphas, which the abstract calls over 22% per year. The SSRN abstract says changes referring to the CEO and CFO, litigation, or the risk-factor section are especially informative, and that the changes predict future earnings, profitability, news, and bankruptcies. The HBS/Journal of Finance abstract says the changes are concentrated in MD&A and highlights the executive-team and litigation language. Both abstracts agree there is no announcement effect: the return arrives later, through news, events, or earnings. The “up to 188 basis points” figure is the top of the range they report, not a claim that every similarity sort earns 22% a year. A 2026 blog’s “30 to 58 basis points” for the broad value-weighted sort was not in either abstract and is not used here. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078); [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331)
- Green, Hand, and Soliman, “Going, Going, Gone? The Apparent Demise of the Accruals Anomaly,” Management Science (2011). They study the hedge returns to Sloan’s 1996 accruals anomaly (The Accounting Review 71, no. 3: 289–315) and find that those hedge returns had decayed in U.S. markets to the point that they are, on average, no longer reliably positive. They attribute this partly to hedge-fund capital and trading in extreme-accrual firms, and more weakly to a smaller mispricing signal. Sloan’s original hedge-return number was not re-read from the 1996 paper in this pass, so it is not quoted. — [Management Science](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1320)
- Post-earnings-announcement drift is not an LLM result, and the magnitudes below are not from a 2020s replication. Wikipedia’s summary of Bernard and Thomas (1990) says zero-investment portfolios formed on earnings surprises earned abnormal returns of about 8–9% over a quarter, about 35% annualized before costs, and that about 25–30% of the drift falls in the three-day windows around the next three announcements. That page is secondary. A primary abstract on a related but different sort — extreme accounting losses versus extreme profits, not standardized unexpected earnings — finds a hedge of about 10% over the 120 trading days after the announcement, described as about 21% annualized, on 458,693 firm-quarters from 1976–2005, and incremental to PEAD, book-to-market, and accruals (Balakrishnan, Bartov, and Faurel, Journal of Accounting and Economics, 2010, as abstracted on ScienceDirect). — [Wikipedia, citing Bernard and Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410109000810)
- Scale comparison using only figures quoted above. Lazy prices: up to 188 basis points per month on a slice of 10-K/10-Q language change, 1995–2014, no day-0 effect, published in the Journal of Finance. Withdrawn GPT financial-statement draft: 84 basis points per month equal-weighted six-factor alpha, 37 basis points value-weighted, formed 30 June, not from filing text, and withdrawn. Risk-assessment calls: 5.28% (insignificant), 6.72% (t = 1.90), and 6.36% (t = 2.31) annualized equal-weighted five-factor alphas, 2018–early 2023, traded with a lag to 31 March. Metric-shift calls: a reported five-factor spread of −0.52% (t = −2.55), horizon consistent with a monthly test, preprint, earnings calls. Headline GPT: a subsequent drift of one to two days on top of a non-tradable open, with secondary Sharpe ratios near 3 that the authors say have declined as the tool spread. Accruals: the published 2011 conclusion is that the original hedge is no longer reliably positive. None of the LLM figures is net of a realistic, value-weighted, post-2022 cost estimate except the withdrawn draft’s 10-basis-point Sharpe adjustment.

### Inferences
- An LLM feature extracted from the 10-K has to beat lazy prices, not beat a naive buy-and-hold. Lazy prices is the same documents, a simpler statistic, a published journal, and a holding-period return that accrues after investors fail to notice the edit. An LLM “what changed” score that is correlated with cosine or edit similarity is mostly that anomaly, unless a horse race says otherwise. No such horse race was found.
- The honest size of a still-standing generative result is a few percent a year on equal-weighted call-based risk sorts, with t-statistics between about 1.5 and 2.3, or about half a percent per month on a preprint metric-shift spread. That is smaller than the upper-end lazy-prices slice, similar in spirit to a decayed anomaly, and not in the same family as the headline-day Sharpe ratios.
- Accruals are a warning about base rates, not a target to beat. A textual anomaly that was large in-sample and easy to explain can be gone by the time a second paper measures it. Green, Hand, and Soliman is the citation. The two 2025 withdrawals are the LLM-era version of the same caution.
- PEAD is the wrong comparison for a 10-K feature and the right comparison for an earnings-release feature. PEAD is drift in the earnings surprise, concentrated at later announcements, and already heavily traded. A language score on the earnings press release has to show incremental drift after the surprise, the call, and the filing. The FinBERT press-release paper raises same-day \(R^2\) from 3.3% to 4.4%. That is not incremental drift.

### Gaps
- The main quintile, value-weighted lazy-prices alpha (as opposed to the “up to 188 basis points” slice) was not retrieved from the Journal of Finance tables.
- Sloan’s 1996 hedge return was not re-read, so it is omitted on purpose.
- No post-2015 replication of lazy prices, and no post-2005 replication of Bernard and Thomas’s 8–9% quarterly spread, was pulled in this pass. Both anomalies may have decayed. The memo does not assume they have, and it does not assume they have not.
- Net-of-cost, capacity-weighted versions of the risk-premium and metric-shift strategies were not in the retrieved text.
