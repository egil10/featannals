# Classic pre-LLM textual features of filings and future excess returns

Scope note for the report writer. This catalogue covers US 10-K and 10-Q text features from the pre-LLM canon (dictionaries, Fog/file size, cosine and edit similarity, Naive Bayes, specificity). Evidence was checked against journal abstracts, author summaries, and a subset of open PDFs through 2026-10-30. Where a number could not be read off a primary table, it is marked unverified. No standalone return paper on Form 20-F, or on the glossy annual report to shareholders as distinct from the 10-K, was located. Earnings-press-release and news-story results are included only to mark the boundary.

How to read return claims. Four different objects get conflated in secondary writeups:

- Filing-window reaction: excess return over a few days starting on the EDGAR file date. Tradable only if the feature is known intraday and the window is not already closed. Loughran and McDonald's proportional negative-word result is not significant on days [0,1].
- Post-filing drift / delayed incorporation: returns after the filing is public, beyond the announcement window, not explained as compensation for risk. This is the object the downstream shortlist should prefer.
- Risk premium: a characteristic that earns higher average returns after standard factors, interpreted by the authors as priced risk (textual financial constraints).
- Fundamentals only: the text predicts earnings, persistence, investment, payouts, volatility, or betas, with no verified mean excess-return spread.

## For each feature, what is it, where is it measured, what is the sign and horizon, and what return is published?

### Takeaway

The only classic filing-text result with a large, published, post-filing long-short alpha is year-over-year document change ("lazy prices"): short changers, long non-changers, up to 188 basis points per month in alpha in the authors' strongest cut, on 1995–2014 US 10-Ks and 10-Qs, with no filing-date announcement effect. Level of Loughran–McDonald negative tone is a 4-day filing-window association, not a verified 12-month drift. Most other famous features (Fog, file size, competition mentions, constraining words, product-market fluidity, risk-factor counts, forward-looking intensity) predict earnings, risk, payouts, or volatility, not a documented excess-return spread. The two other return results that survive a serious factor model are aggregate manager sentiment as a contrarian market-timing signal (filings mixed with conference calls) and text-based debt constraints as an annualized Fama–French five-factor alpha of about 6.5 percent, which the authors treat as a risk premium.

### Cited Findings

**1. Loughran–McDonald negative, positive, uncertainty, litigious, strong modal, weak modal (full 10-K levels)**

- Paper: Tim Loughran and Bill McDonald, "When Is a Liability Not a Liability? Textual Analysis, Dictionaries, and 10-Ks," Journal of Finance 66(1), February 2011, 35–65. Sample: 10-Ks from 1994 to 2008; the quintile figure uses 50,115 10-Ks. Word lists: a financial negative list (Fin-Neg) plus positive, uncertainty, litigious, strong modal, and weak modal. About 73.8 percent of Harvard-IV negative word counts in these 10-Ks are words that are not negative in a financial context. Mean share of 10-K words on Fin-Neg is about 1.39 percent, versus about 3.79 percent on the inflected Harvard negative list. — [Loughran–McDonald 2011 PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1331573)
- Definition and section: proportion of words (and, separately, tf-idf weighted counts) in the parsed 10-K. Exhibits in `<TYPE>EX` segments are removed in the internet appendix parsing note. The same lists are also counted on the MD&A only, requiring an identifiable MD&A of more than 250 words. MD&A tone does not have a more discernible association with file-date excess returns than the full document. — [Loughran–McDonald 2011 PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)
- Sign and horizon: higher Fin-Neg proportion, lower filing-window excess return. The file-date return is the buy-and-hold stock return minus the CRSP value-weighted market return over days [0, 3], expressed in percent. In Fama–MacBeth regressions (firms grouped by quarter, Newey–West with one lag, estimates weighted by the number of filings because file dates cluster), Fin-Neg has a significantly negative coefficient, t-statistic 2.64, with controls for size, book-to-market, turnover, and Fama–French industry dummies. Harvard negative proportion is not significant. The regression coefficient itself was not reliably readable from the PDF text layer, so no basis-point magnitude is reported here. Absolute excess returns stay elevated through day 4 (t-statistics for days 0 through 5 of 12.8, 16.8, 12.9, 6.9, 3.6, and 1.7). Shrinking the window to [0, 2] leaves only the weighted measures significant; on [0, 1] none of the measures are significant. — [Loughran–McDonald 2011 PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)
- Other lists, same [0, 3] window, proportional weights, each list in its own regression with the same controls: Fin-Neg, uncertainty, strong modal, and weak modal are significant and negatively signed. Positive and litigious are not the significant return lists in that proportional-weight panel. With tf-idf weights, every list except litigious is significant for filing-period excess returns. Harvard and Fin-Neg (positive coefficients) are associated with higher abnormal volume in the 4-day window. Essentially every list is positively associated with subsequent stock-return volatility measured outside the 4-day window; litigious is the exception in the proportional panel. The paper also links the lists to unexpected earnings, 10b-5 accounting suits, and material weaknesses; those logit coefficients were not extracted. — [Loughran–McDonald 2011 PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)
- Differencing: normalized year-to-year differences of the proportional negative measures, in an internet-appendix regression parallel to Table IV, keep the same signs and significance. The authors say differencing does not clearly fix the noise problem, and they explicitly doubt that a reader tracks the prior 10-K's negative-word frequency. — [Loughran–McDonald 2011 PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)
- What this is not: the paper does not report a 3-, 6-, or 12-month long-short on Fin-Neg. A later non-journal note that sorts on the product of pessimism change and past returns (Chouliaras, 10-Ks 1992–2015, "higher than 5 percent" at nine months and "surpasses 7 percent" at twelve months) is an SSRN/Academia working paper, not the Loughran–McDonald result, and is not treated as established here. — [Academia page for Chouliaras](https://www.academia.edu/26934649/The_Pessimism_Factor_SEC_EDGAR_Form_10_K_Textual_Analysis_and_Stock_Returns)

**2. Document similarity / "lazy prices" (year-over-year change in 10-K and 10-Q language)**

- Paper: Lauren Cohen, Christopher Malloy, and Quoc Nguyen, "Lazy Prices," Journal of Finance 75(3), June 2020, 1371–1415. Sample in the working-paper abstract: complete regular quarterly and annual filings, 1995–2014. — [HBS faculty page](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [SSRN/NBER abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078); [published abstract via EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:75:y:2020:i:3:p:1371-1415)
- Definition: four similarity measures between this filing and the firm's prior filing — cosine similarity on bag-of-words term frequencies, Jaccard, minimum edit distance, and a simple similarity (word-level overlap). Firms are sorted into quintiles using the prior year's distribution of similarity scores. Q1 is changers (least similar); Q5 is non-changers. Stocks enter the portfolio in the month after the filing becomes public and are held for three months. Portfolio is long non-changers and short changers. — [NBER internet appendix w25084](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w25084/w25084.appendix.pdf); [Alpha Architect summary of the working paper](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/)
- Sign and published magnitude: high similarity predicts higher future returns. The published abstract states that the long-short "earns up to 188 basis points per month in alpha (over 22 percent per year)." The HBS abstract uses the same 188 bp / 22 percent figure and says reporting changes are concentrated in the MD&A, with language about the executive team (CEO and CFO) or litigation especially informative. The SSRN abstract adds the risk-factor section to that list. There is no announcement-window return; returns accrue when the information later shows up in news, events, or earnings. Changes also predict future earnings, profitability, news, and firm-level bankruptcies. — [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)
- The 188 bp figure is a ceiling ("up to"), not the baseline full-document sort. A 2016 Alpha Architect writeup of the working paper reports the broad long non-changers / short changers portfolio at a statistically significant 30–60 basis points per month, "up to 7.6 percent over the following year," and says risk-factor text and CEO/CFO, litigation, and lawsuit language are more informative than the bulk of the document. A 2026 secondary blog repeats "30 to 58 basis points per month" in value-weighted abnormal returns for the broad sort and attributes 188 bp per month (t = 2.76) specifically to the risk-factor slice. Those two secondary sources agree on the order of magnitude of the broad sort; the exact table cell behind 188 bp was not opened. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/); [Quantt, April 2026](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)
- Internet-appendix numbers that were read directly (Table A-1, risk-factor section only, post-Sarbanes-Oxley 2003–2014, quintiles from the prior year's similarity distribution, enter the month after release, hold three months, Q5 minus Q1). Equally weighted excess-return intercepts: cosine 0.44 percent (t = 1.27), Jaccard 1.11 percent (t = 3.15), min-edit 0.62 percent (t = 1.80), simple 0.60 percent (t = 1.98). Value-weighted excess-return intercepts: cosine 0.91 percent (t = 2.39), Jaccard 0.86 percent (t = 2.12), min-edit 0.64 percent (t = 1.63), simple 0.41 percent (t = 1.16). The same table reports Fama–French 3-factor alphas (market, size, value) and a 5-factor alpha that adds momentum and liquidity, not profitability or investment. The equally weighted Jaccard 3-factor intercept in that table is 1.15 percent. These are not the 188 bp cell. — [NBER internet appendix](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w25084/w25084.appendix.pdf)

**3. MD&A tone change (not the level)**

- Paper: Ronen Feldman, Suresh Govindaraj, Joshua Livnat, and Benjamin Segal, "Management's Tone Change, Post Earnings Announcement Drift and Accruals," Review of Accounting Studies 15(4), December 2010, 915–953. — [RAS abstract via IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v15y2010i4d10.1007_s11142-009-9111-x.html)
- Definition and section: change in positive-versus-negative word tone of the MD&A in Forms 10-Q and 10-K relative to the prior periodic SEC filing. Not the full 10-K, and not the level of tone.
- Sign and horizon: short-window returns around the filing are significantly associated with the tone change after controlling for accruals and earnings surprises. The drift test is a portfolio held from two days after the SEC filing date through one day after the next quarter's preliminary earnings announcement. Tone change adds to that drift beyond accruals and earnings surprises. Part of the drift is the ability of tone change to predict the next quarter's earnings surprise, but not all of it. The incremental information is stronger when the firm's information environment is weaker. — [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v15y2010i4d10.1007_s11142-009-9111-x.html)
- No portfolio return in percent or basis points was verified from the paper. An Academia.edu page stating roughly 0.20 percent, −0.31 percent, and 0.37 percent looks machine-summarized and is not used.

**4. 10-K complexity as a moderator of underreaction, not as a signed signal**

- Paper: Haifeng You and Xiao-Jun Zhang, "Financial Reporting Complexity and Investor Underreaction to 10-K Information," Review of Accounting Studies 14(4), 2009, 559–586 (published online 4 November 2008). — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-008-9083-2); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=985365)
- Finding: there are unusual volumes and price moves around 10-K file dates. The abnormal price move is positively associated with future accounting profitability, so the filing contains news about future performance. Prices then drift for twelve months after the filing. Underreaction is stronger when the 10-K is more complex. A later paper states that You and Zhang use a larger 10-K word count as the complexity measure; that definition was not re-read in You and Zhang's own text. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-008-9083-2); [citing paper on file-size change](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343549989_The_information_content_of_10-K_file_size_change)
- Sign: complexity does not, in the abstract, predict the sign of the return. It predicts that the market's initial reaction is too small. A trading use is therefore an interaction: go with the sign of the filing-window return, sized up when the document is long. No 12-month hedge return in percent was verified.

**5. Word-power tone (return-weighted terms, full 10-K)**

- Paper: Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Di Wu, "Word Power: A New Approach for Content Analysis," Journal of Financial Economics 110(3), December 2013, 712–729. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13002328)
- Definition: term weights estimated from past market reactions to words, rather than equal weights inside a hand-built positive or negative list. Applied to 10-Ks. The same approach is also applied to IPO prospectuses, where the document score is negatively related to IPO underpricing.
- Sign and horizon: document tone is significantly related to filing-period returns for both positive and negative words, which equal-weighted dictionary papers generally did not achieve for positive words. Tone remains related to returns for up to two weeks, which the authors read as a short underreaction that is corrected inside two weeks. They also say the choice of term weights is at least as important as the completeness of the word list. No basis-point long-short was extracted. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13002328); [Nomura slides](https://www.nomura.com/events/global-quantitative-equity-conference/resources/upload/Word%20Power.pdf)

**6. Price-supervised finance dictionaries and bigrams ("colour of finance words")**

- Paper: Diego Garcia, Xiaowen Hu, and Maximilian Rohrer, "The Colour of Finance Words," Journal of Financial Economics 147, 2023, 525–549 (available online 18 January 2023). Dictionaries are built by extending Taddy (2013) with a cross-validation layer, using stock-price reactions as the supervisor, including bigrams so the same word can take different signs in context. Corpora named in the abstract and draft: earnings calls, 10-Ks, and WSJ. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3630898); [Studocu copy of JFE pages](https://www.studocu.com/tw/document/national-taipei-university/behavioral-finance/2023-jfe-the-colour-of-finance-words/72587376); [December 2021 draft](https://sites.insead.edu/facultyresearch/research/file.cfm?fid=68633)
- Return result that was verified: the dictionaries outperform Loughran–McDonald bag-of-words when predicting stock-price movements out of sample. A December 2021 draft says the ML dictionaries are validated on returns around earnings announcements, and that dictionaries trained on earnings calls predict 10-K filing price reactions better than the Loughran–McDonald dictionaries (and vice versa is also studied). No long-horizon long-short alpha was extracted. This is a better measurement of short-window tone, not a published 12-month factor. — [INSEAD draft](https://sites.insead.edu/facultyresearch/research/file.cfm?fid=68633)

**7. Abnormal positive tone / tone management (earnings press releases, not 10-Ks)**

- Paper: Xuan Huang, Siew Hong Teoh, and Yinglei Zhang, "Tone Management," The Accounting Review 89(3), 1 May 2014, 1083–1113. — [AAA abstract](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/89/3/1083/3532/Tone-Management)
- Definition: ABTONE is the residual from a regression of positive tone on quantitative fundamentals (performance, risk, and complexity). Corpus is earnings press releases, not Item 7 or the 10-K.
- Sign and horizon: ABTONE predicts negative future earnings and cash flows. It has a positive stock-price effect at the earnings announcement and a delayed negative reaction over the next one and two quarters. That is a reversal (overreaction to strategic tone), the opposite of post-earnings-announcement drift. ABTONE is positively associated with just meeting or beating thresholds, future restatements, SEOs, and M&A, and negatively associated with stock-option grants. Balance-sheet-constrained and older firms use tone management more relative to accruals management. No basis-point magnitude was extracted. — [AAA](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/89/3/1083/3532/Tone-Management); [Scribd copy of the manuscript](https://www.scribd.com/document/1005957081/8)

**8. Aggregate manager sentiment (10-Ks, 10-Qs, and conference calls, market level)**

- Paper: Fuwei Jiang, Joshua Lee, Xiumin Martin, and Guofu Zhou, "Manager Sentiment and Stock Returns," Journal of Financial Economics 132(1), April 2019, 126–149. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302770)
- Definition: a monthly index of aggregated textual tone across corporate financial disclosures. A secondary summary says the inputs are 10-Ks, 10-Qs, and conference-call transcripts, with the series built over 2003–2014. The call transcripts mean this is not a pure filing feature. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302770); [Hedge Fund Alpha summary](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/manager-sentiment-and-stock-returns/)
- Sign and horizon: higher manager sentiment predicts lower future aggregate excess market returns. Monthly in-sample R-squared is 9.75 percent. A one-standard-deviation increase is associated with a −1.26 percent change in the expected excess market return the next month. Out-of-sample R-squared is 8.38 percent over January 2007–December 2014. Higher sentiment also precedes lower aggregate earnings surprises and higher aggregate investment growth. In the cross-section, manager sentiment negatively predicts stock returns, especially for firms that are hard to value and costly to arbitrage. The authors' interpretation is market-wide overvaluation when managers are optimistic, so the index is contrarian. It is not "buy stocks whose own 10-K is negative." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302770)

**9. Text-based financial constraints as a return factor**

- Paper: Matthias M. M. Buehlmaier and Toni M. Whited, "Are Financial Constraints Priced? Evidence from Textual Analysis," Review of Financial Studies 31(7), July 2018, 2693–2728 (published online 25 January 2018). — [Oxford Academic abstract](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/31/7/2693/4824924); [author PDF, revised 5 November 2017](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/261048/1/Content.pdf)
- Definition: three narrative measures from annual reports, capturing constrained access to equity markets, to debt markets, and to external finance generally. Not a single word list of the Bodnaruk type (see item 16).
- Sign and horizon: constrained firms earn higher returns. The returns move together and are not explained by the Fama–French (2015) five-factor model. The strategy is most profitable among large, liquid stocks. Strongest for debt constraints: a portfolio on that measure earns an annualized risk-adjusted excess return of 6.5 percent. The authors' Harvard corporate-governance summary of the same paper reports annualized risk-adjusted excess returns of 6.5 percent (debt), 3.7 percent (general), and 3.0 percent (equity) for the zero-cost portfolios, and a 7.2 percent annualized alpha on the debt measure inside the top market-capitalization percentile after averaging out size quantiles. The authors interpret this as compensation for financial risk, not as slow reading of the 10-K. Sample-period start and end dates were not extracted. — [Oxford](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/31/7/2693/4824924); [Harvard Law summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/03/10/are-financial-constraints-priced-evidence-from-textual-analysis/); [PDF](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/261048/1/Content.pdf)

**10. Fog index and annual-report length (fundamentals, not returns)**

- Paper: Feng Li, "Annual Report Readability, Current Earnings, and Earnings Persistence," Journal of Accounting and Economics 45, 2008, 221–247. Sample: more than 50,000 firm-years. Readability is the Gunning Fog index and document length. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410108000141); [PDF](https://care-mendoza.nd.edu/assets/152198/li_jae_2008.pdf)
- Findings: firms with lower earnings have harder-to-read annual reports (higher Fog and longer). An earnings increase versus last year is associated with an easier subsequent report. The earnings-level effect is statistically significant but small. Firms with easier reports have more persistent positive earnings. An interquartile change in readability has an effect on persistence similar to an interquartile change in absolute total accruals scaled by book assets. The paper does not report a stock-return long-short.

**11. File size as a readability proxy (volatility and earnings news, not mean returns)**

- Paper: Tim Loughran and Bill McDonald, "Measuring Readability in Financial Disclosures," Journal of Finance 69(4), August 2014, 1643–1671. — [Wiley abstract](http://dx.doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12162); [Harvard Law author summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/)
- Definition they recommend: file size of the complete submission text file on EDGAR, with no parsing. They argue Fog is poorly specified in financial text because one component (complex syllables) treats ordinary words such as "management," "corporation," "customers," and "revenue" as complex, and sentence length is hard to measure in filings. An AAII summary says Fog scores on nearly 66,707 10-Ks barely vary and more than 90 percent imply a postgraduate reading level. Treat the 66,707 and 90 percent figures as secondary. — [Harvard Law](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/); [AAII](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/10k-filing-size-impacts-volatility)
- What file size predicts: after controls, larger 10-K file size is associated with higher post-filing abnormal return volatility, higher absolute standardized unexpected earnings, and higher analyst-forecast dispersion. The authors say this is not just firm complexity. They do not report a mean excess-return spread. — [Harvard Law](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/)
- Later caveat on the proxy: Samuel Bonsall, Andrew Leone, Brian Miller, and Kristina Rennekamp, "A Plain English Measure of Financial Reporting Readability," Journal of Accounting and Economics 63(2–3), April 2017, 329–357, propose the Bog Index (active voice, hidden verbs, and other plain-English attributes). They warn that a large part of the time-series variation in 10-K file size is HTML, XML, and embedded PDFs, not the underlying prose. No return spread for the Bog Index was verified. — [Penn State abstract](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-plain-english-measure-of-financial-reporting-readability/)

**12. Year-over-year MD&A modification score (announcement magnitude, not drift)**

- Paper: Stephen V. Brown and Jennifer Wu Tucker, "Large-Sample Evidence on Firms' Year-over-Year MD&A Modifications," Journal of Accounting Research 49(2), May 2011, 309–346. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1452977)
- Definition: a score for how much this year's MD&A differs from last year's. Firms with larger economic changes modify more. The absolute stock-price response to the 10-K filing is positively associated with the modification score. Analyst earnings-forecast revisions are not associated with the score. Over the decade they study, modification scores fell even as MD&As got longer, and the price reaction to the modification score weakened. This is an announcement |return| result. It is not the Cohen–Malloy–Nguyen drift, and the two papers point in different directions about whether investors notice MD&A edits at the filing (see question 2).

**13. Boilerplate, length, redundancy (description of the corpus, not a return factor)**

- Paper: Travis Dyer, Mark Lang, and Lorien Stice-Lawrence, "The Evolution of 10-K Textual Disclosure: Evidence from Latent Dirichlet Allocation," Journal of Accounting and Economics 64(2–3), 2017, 221–245. Sample period 1996–2013. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2741682)
- Finding: 10-Ks became longer, more boilerplate, stickier, and more redundant, and less specific, less readable, and less tilted toward hard information. Using 150 LDA topics, new FASB and SEC requirements explain most of the length increase, and three topics — fair value, internal controls, and risk-factor disclosures — account for virtually all of it. No return regression was reported in the abstract. The implication for feature design is that raw length and raw stickiness are heavily regulatory.

**14. Risk-disclosure increases (volatility and volume, not a verified equity long-short)**

- Paper: Todd Kravet and Volkan Muslu, "Textual Risk Disclosures and Investors' Risk Perceptions," Review of Accounting Studies 18, 2013, 1088–1122. — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-013-9228-9); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1736228)
- Definition: annual changes in textual risk disclosures inside 10-Ks (risk sentences, not only the post-2005 Item 1A). Annual increases are associated with higher stock-return volatility and trading volume around and after the filing, and with more dispersed analyst-forecast revisions. The authors read this as risk disclosures raising perceived risk, rather than resolving uncertainty. The association is weaker for firm-level disclosures that deviate from other firms in the same industry and year, which they interpret as evidence that those idiosyncratic disclosures are more boilerplate. That interpretation conflicts with later specificity results (item 15) and should not be smoothed over. No mean-return long-short was stated in the abstract.

**15. Item 1A risk-factor categories, specificity, and additions versus deletions**

- Categories. John L. Campbell, Hsinchun Chen, Dan S. Dhaliwal, Hsin-min Lu, and Logan B. Steele, "The Information Content of Mandatory Risk Factor Disclosures in Corporate Filings," Review of Accounting Studies 19(1), March 2014, 396–455. From 2005, Item 1A must discuss the most significant factors that make the company speculative or risky. Firms facing more risk disclose more. The mix across systematic, idiosyncratic, financial, legal, and tax risk tracks the type of risk the firm faces. The disclosures are reflected in systematic risk, idiosyncratic risk, information asymmetry, and firm value. An SSRN wording says the authors are the first to show that when managers increase negative qualitative disclosure, market-based risk measures rise. This is a risk-and-value result, not a published post-filing equity alpha. — [Monash abstract](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/the-information-content-of-mandatory-risk-factor-disclosures-in-c/); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1694279)
- Specificity. Ole-Kristian Hope, Danqi Hu, and Hai Lu, "The Benefits of Specific Risk-Factor Disclosures," Review of Accounting Studies 21(4), 2016, 1005–1045. Specificity is an algorithmic score of how specific Item 1A language is. Firms with high proprietary costs are less specific. Controlling for determinants, the market reaction to the 10-K is positively and significantly associated with Specificity; the draft reports a positive relation with the absolute three-day cumulative abnormal return around the filing, and also looks at abnormal short-window volume. Analysts assess fundamental risk better when disclosures are more specific. Horizon is the filing window, and the return object is the absolute reaction, not a signed drift. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2749132); [PDF](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/soa_research/article/2648/viewcontent/BenefitsSpecificRisk_FactorDisclosures_2016.pdf)
- Additions and deletions of individual risk-factor captions. Matthew Glendening, Elaine Mauldin, and Kenneth Shaw-style result is actually: a 2023 Accounting Review paper, "Changes in Risk Factor Disclosures and the Variance Risk Premium," The Accounting Review 98(6), October 2023, 327–352 (the Columbia draft read here is the working-paper version). Changes are additions and removals of individual risk factors in Item 1A. Greater changes are associated with a lower variance risk premium, i.e. less uncertainty about firm risk, and the individual-factor changes are incremental to coarser textual risk measures. This prices uncertainty in the options-implied VRP, not an equity long-short. Author names on the published TAR page were not fully confirmed against the draft header in the pages retrieved; cite the journal article for the result and the draft for the design. — [TAR abstract](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/98/6/327/11227/Changes-in-Risk-Factor-Disclosures-and-the); [Columbia draft](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/CEASA/Events%20Page/changes_risk_factor_disclosures_variance_risk_premium.pdf)
- A related but unpublished-looking paper, Bei Chen and Runfeng Yang, "Risk Factor Similarity and Economic Links," studies cosine similarity of Item 1A across firms and defines risk peers as the top 5 percent. A cached draft reports that a portfolio long the highest peer-return group and short the lowest earns 1.777 percent average monthly excess return (t = 3.70) and an alpha spread of 1.803 percent (t = 3.33) after Fama–French controls. The same cache says the link subsumes several cross-firm momentum patterns and is stronger when the focal firm gets less attention, when limits to arbitrage are higher, when the peer is outside the industry, and when risk-factor text has changed. Journal publication was not confirmed. Treat as a lead, not as canon. — [Google scholar cache](https://scholar.googleusercontent.com/scholar?q=cache:udHDV8ECyWsJ:scholar.google.com/&hl=ja&as_sdt=2005&sciodt=0,5)

**16. Constraining words (liquidity events, not a verified return spread)**

- Paper: Andriy Bodnaruk, Tim Loughran, and Bill McDonald, "Using 10-K Text to Gauge Financial Constraints," Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, published online 20 October 2015. — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/using-10k-text-to-gauge-financial-constraints/6636946FFF406F3D0C1A40557082601D); [25 August 2015 manuscript](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/wbs/subjects/finance/events/seminars/using_10-k_text_to_gauge_financial_constraints.pdf)
- Definition: frequency of a lexicon of constraining words in the 10-K. Correlation with the SA and WW constraint indexes is very low, and unlike those indexes the frequency is not highly correlated with market capitalization.
- What it predicts, from the August 2015 manuscript: more constraining words are associated with a higher likelihood of future dividend omission (+10.32 percent), a lower likelihood of dividend increases (−6.46 percent), a lower likelihood of equity recycling (−23.24 percent), and a higher likelihood of underfunded pensions (+2.34 percent). Stronger in the cross-section than in the time series. The published abstract lists the same events and does not state a stock-return long-short. Do not treat this as established excess-return predictability. It is also a different object from Buehlmaier and Whited's narrative constraint measures, which do have a return result.

**17. Hoberg–Maksimovic text constraints (investment, not returns)**

- Paper: Gerard Hoberg and Vojislav Maksimovic, "Redefining Financial Constraints: A Text-Based Analysis," Review of Financial Studies 28(5), May 2015, 1312–1352. They score 10-K text into annual measures for firms that discuss equity-market financing issues and debt-market financing issues. Equity-constraint firms are funding growth options, suffer more after large unexpected negative shocks, and often say they have undisclosed proprietary information. Debt constraints are distinct and linked to covenant violations. The measures predict investment cuts after negative shocks better than standard indexes. The abstract does not report a return premium. — [SciSpace / RFS abstract](https://scispace.com/papers/redefining-financial-constraints-a-text-based-analysis-3wc8ts5rsy)

**18. Competition mentions in the 10-K (profitability of investment, return claim dropped or unverified)**

- Paper: Feng Li, Russell Lundholm, and Michael Minnis, "A Measure of Competition Based on 10-K Filings," Journal of Accounting Research 51(2), May 2013, 399–436. Management's competition disclosures produce a firm-level competition measure related to, but distinct from, industry Herfindahl. Both the across-industry and within-industry components are related to future rates of diminishing marginal returns on new and existing investment. The journal page notes that an earlier version of part of the work was titled "The impact of competitive intensity on the profitability of investments and future stock returns." The published abstract does not state a stock-return result. Count this as fundamentals unless a return table is re-opened. Data pointer in the paper: http://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feng/ — [Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2012.00472.x)

**19. Product-market fluidity and text-based industry competition (payouts; one boom-bust return result)**

- Fluidity itself. Gerard Hoberg, Gordon Phillips, and Nagpurnanand Prabhala, "Product Market Threats, Payouts, and Financial Flexibility," Journal of Finance 69(1), 2014, 293–324. Fluidity measures how much rival firms' product descriptions, taken from 10-K product text, are changing relative to the firm's own products. It is not MD&A tone. Higher fluidity is associated with a lower propensity to pay dividends or repurchase, lower dividend amounts, and higher cash. In the draft's quintiles, 48.6 percent of the lowest-fluidity firms pay dividends versus 9.1 percent of the highest-fluidity firms, and cash rises from 8.2 percent of assets to 21.5 percent. No fluidity-sorted equity alpha was stated. Data library (coverage extended to 2023, overall 1989–2023) is at the Hoberg–Phillips site. — [Draft PDF via SciSpace](https://scispace.com/pdf/product-market-threats-payouts-and-financial-flexibility-s33c4ury21.pdf); [data library](https://hobergphillips.tuck.dartmouth.edu/)
- Return evidence that does exist is a different design. Gerard Hoberg and Gordon Phillips, "Real and Financial Industry Booms and Busts" (NBER working paper w14290, August 2008; SSRN last revised 20 July 2022). In competitive industries, high industry-level valuation, investment, and new financing are followed by sharply lower operating cash flows and abnormal stock returns. Analyst estimates are positively biased and returns comove more in those booms. In concentrated industries the relations are weak. The abnormal-return magnitude was not extracted. The feature is text-based competition interacted with an industry boom, not a standalone sort on fluidity or on tone. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1261476)

**20. Forward-looking statements in MD&A**

- Tone of forward-looking sentences, supervised. Feng Li, "The Information Content of Forward-Looking Statements in Corporate Filings — A Naive Bayesian Machine Learning Approach," Journal of Accounting Research 48(5), December 2010, 1049–1102. Corpus: forward-looking statements in the MD&A of 10-Ks and 10-Qs. Average FLS tone is positively associated with future earnings after other determinants. Firms with better current performance, lower accruals, smaller size, lower market-to-book, less return volatility, lower MD&A Fog, and a longer history tend to have more positive FLS. Diction, General Inquirer, and LIWC tone do not positively predict future performance. On returns: when managers "warn" — MD&A tone positive when accruals are negative, or tone negative when accruals are positive — accruals are not associated with future returns. The return result is an interaction that shuts off accrual mispricing, not a standalone tone drift. There is no systematic improvement in MD&A information content over the sample despite tighter MD&A rules. — [Wiley abstract](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00382.x)
- Quantity of forward-looking MD&A, not tone. Volkan Muslu, Suresh Radhakrishnan, K. R. Subramanyam, and Keji Lim, "Forward-Looking MD&A Disclosures and the Information Environment," Management Science 61(5), May 2015, 931–948. Note the journal is Management Science, not The Accounting Review. Firms provide more forward-looking MD&A when stock prices reflect future earnings poorly. More of those disclosures improve, but do not eliminate, that inefficiency. Effects are stronger for operations-related forward-looking sentences, for disclosures before 2000, and for loss firms. This is a future-earnings-response result, not a long-short alpha. — [SciSpace abstract](https://scispace.com/papers/forward-looking-md-a-disclosures-and-the-information-3niyzindg0)

**21. Self-serving attribution**

- Feng Li, "Managers' Self-Serving Attribution Bias and Corporate Financial Policies," manuscript dated March 2012 (journal publication was not confirmed in this pass). On more than 30,000 MD&As from 1994–2007, managers use more first-person pronouns relative to second- and third-person pronouns when performance is better. A regression of that self-reference tendency on returns has a coefficient of 0.11 (t = 4.40, year-clustered). Firms whose managers score higher on this bias have higher investment-cash-flow sensitivity, more negative market reactions around acquisition announcements, higher leverage, more repurchases, and fewer dividends. The stock-return evidence is an acquisition announcement reaction, not a post-10-K drift. — [PDF](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files/f84c92eee602b014fba808ae80a2b27b.pdf)
- A later large-sample MD&A study, Gong (2024), Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance, finds poorly performing firms attribute results to external factors, splits external attributions into a credible part and a strategic part, and finds investors react more strongly to performance when external attributions are credible, especially if the firm does not have a reputation for strategic external attributions. Executive pay is less sensitive to performance when strategic external attributions are higher. Again an announcement/contracting result, not a verified post-filing alpha. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jcaf.22707)
- Older and non-US-equity-canon: Clapham and Schwenk, Strategic Management Journal 12(3), March 1991, 219–229, study attributions in letters to shareholders of public utilities. The self-serving pattern is there, and its relation to subsequent EPS growth is generally negative. Letters to shareholders, not 10-K items, and not stock returns. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/smj.4250120305)

**22. News tone, only as the ancestor that does not transfer one-for-one**

- Paul C. Tetlock, Maytal Saar-Tsechansky, and Sofus Macskassy, "More Than Words: Quantifying Language to Measure Firms' Fundamentals," Journal of Finance 63(3), June 2008, 1437–1467. Corpus is firm-specific news (Dow Jones), not filings. The fraction of negative words forecasts lower earnings, beyond analyst forecasts and historical accounting data. Stock prices underreact with a small, one-day delay. Predictability of earnings and returns is largest for stories about fundamentals. A daily strategy is identified for the intraday Dow Jones news wire, not for the slower Wall Street Journal. — [PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_tetlock-saar-tsechansky-macskassy-jf-2008.pdf)
- Loughran and McDonald (2011) show that the Harvard negative list those news studies rely on is the wrong list inside 10-Ks, and that the 10-K reaction, when it exists, is a multi-day filing window rather than a one-day news delay. Porting a news pessimism signal onto a 10-K is not supported.

**23. Method survey, not a return paper**

- Tim Loughran and Bill McDonald, "Textual Analysis in Accounting and Finance: A Survey," Journal of Accounting Research 54(4), 2016, 1187–1230. The survey's abstract stresses that textual methods are imprecise and reviews bag-of-words, readability, word lists, Zipf's law, cosine similarity, and Naive Bayes. It is the right checklist for implementation traps. It is not itself a source of long-short magnitudes; those were not extracted from the survey body. — [Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-679X.12123); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2959518)

### Inferences

- If the shortlist is restricted to features with a verified post-filing excess-return spread, the first-tier classic inputs are: (i) year-over-year similarity of the filing, especially risk-factor, litigation, and CEO/CFO language; (ii) MD&A tone change versus the prior periodic filing, as a drift signal through the next earnings announcement; (iii) the sign of the initial 10-K reaction interacted with document complexity, over twelve months; (iv) text-based debt-market constraints, held as a risk premium rather than as underreaction; (v) aggregate manager tone, as a contrarian market factor, and only if conference-call text is allowed in the input set. Word-power tone is real but the drift dies inside two weeks.
- Level of Fin-Neg, Fog, file size, competition-word counts, constraining-word counts, fluidity, Item 1A length, and forward-looking sentence counts should not be sold as 12-month return factors on the evidence above. They belong in a fundamentals or risk block, or as conditioning variables.
- The sign is not stable across features. High negative tone, high document change, high abnormal positive tone, high manager sentiment, and high textual constraints do not all point to lower future returns. A single "sentiment score" that adds them up will mix underreaction, overreaction, and a risk premium.

- Feldman, Govindaraj, Livnat, and Segal (2010) portfolio drift in percent was not verified.
- You and Zhang (2009) do not, in the sources opened, state the 12-month hedge return or the exact complexity formula. The "word count" definition is from a citing paper.
- The precise specification behind Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen's 188 basis points (which similarity measure, equal versus value weight, which section, which factor model, in-sample versus post-SOX) was not read in the published tables. The internet-appendix risk-factor cells that were read are smaller (about 40 to 115 basis points per month).
- Buehlmaier and Whited sample years, and Hoberg and Phillips boom-bust abnormal-return magnitudes, were not extracted.
- Henry's earnings-press-release tone measure, Davis, Piger, and Sedor's optimistic tone and future ROA, and any dedicated 20-F or glossy-annual-report study were not re-opened. No return result for those corpora is claimed here.
- Author names on the 2023 TAR variance-risk-premium paper were not fully locked to the Columbia draft.
- Chen and Yang's risk-peer 1.8 percent monthly alpha is from a cached working paper, not a confirmed journal article.

## Which results are post-filing drift, same-day reactions, risk premia, or earnings-only?

### Takeaway

Most of the canon is either a filing-week reaction or a prediction of earnings, volatility, or payouts. Clean post-filing drift — returns that keep moving after the document is public, with no filing-date jump — is rare. Lazy prices is the cleanest case: the authors emphasize there is no announcement effect. MD&A tone change and the 12-month 10-K underreaction are drift, but they sit on top of a real filing-window reaction. Textual constraints are presented by their authors as a risk premium. Abnormal tone in press releases is a reversal. Aggregate manager sentiment is contrarian market timing.

### Cited Findings

- Drift, little or no announcement effect. Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen: "Unlike typical underreaction patterns, we find no announcement effect," and returns arrive later through news, events, or earnings. Long non-changers, short changers. Holding period in the appendix design is three months after the month of the public release, rebuilt monthly. — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:75:y:2020:i:3:p:1371-1415); [NBER appendix](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w25084/w25084.appendix.pdf)
- Drift on top of an announcement reaction. Feldman et al.: significant short-window association of MD&A tone change with returns, plus an incremental drift from day +2 after filing through day +1 after the next preliminary earnings announcement, partly because tone change predicts the next earnings surprise. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v15y2010i4d10.1007_s11142-009-9111-x.html)
- Drift of the filing's own news, amplified by complexity. You and Zhang: the filing-window price move lines up with future profitability, and prices continue to drift for twelve months, more so when the 10-K is complex. The text feature is a friction, not the sign. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-008-9083-2)
- Very short drift. Jegadeesh and Wu: 10-K tone is still related to returns for up to two weeks, then the gap is gone. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13002328)
- Filing-window reaction only. Loughran and McDonald: [0, 3] excess returns; insignificant on [0, 1]. Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer: out-of-sample prediction of price movements and of 10-K filing reactions. Hope, Hu, and Lu: absolute three-day CAR around the filing rises with Item 1A specificity. Brown and Tucker: absolute filing reaction rises with MD&A modification. — [Loughran–McDonald PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [Garcia draft](https://sites.insead.edu/facultyresearch/research/file.cfm?fid=68633); [Hope PDF](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/soa_research/article/2648/viewcontent/BenefitsSpecificRisk_FactorDisclosures_2016.pdf); [Brown–Tucker SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1452977)
- Reversal, not drift, and not a 10-K. Huang, Teoh, and Zhang: positive announcement return to abnormal positive tone in earnings press releases, negative returns over the following one and two quarters. — [AAA](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/89/3/1083/3532/Tone-Management)
- Contrarian market premium, mixed corpus. Jiang, Lee, Martin, and Zhou: high aggregate manager sentiment, lower next-month market excess return (−1.26 percent per standard deviation). Cross-sectional leg is concentrated where valuation is hard and arbitrage is costly, which is the pattern of mispricing rather than of a filing-level underreaction to bad words. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302770)
- Risk premium. Buehlmaier and Whited: higher returns to textually constrained firms, not explained by Fama–French five factors, largest in large liquid stocks, interpreted as pay for financial risk. Debt-constraint annualized risk-adjusted excess return 6.5 percent. — [Oxford abstract](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/31/7/2693/4824924)
- Risk quantities, not mean equity returns. Kravet and Muslu: volatility and volume. Campbell et al.: beta, idiosyncratic volatility, information asymmetry, firm value. Loughran and McDonald 2011 and 2014: post-filing volatility. The 2023 TAR paper: variance risk premium falls when individual risk factors are added or removed. — [Kravet–Muslu SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1736228); [Campbell SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1694279); [Harvard Law on file size](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/); [TAR 2023](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/98/6/327/11227/Changes-in-Risk-Factor-Disclosures-and-the)
- Earnings or real outcomes only, on the evidence opened. Li 2008: earnings persistence. Li 2010: future earnings from Naive Bayes FLS tone, plus a conditional shutoff of the accrual anomaly. Muslu et al. 2015: stock prices reflect future earnings more when forward-looking MD&A is richer, without a trading-rule alpha. Bodnaruk, Loughran, and McDonald: dividend omissions, dividend increases, equity recycling, underfunded pensions. Hoberg and Maksimovic: investment cuts. Hoberg, Phillips, and Prabhala: dividends, repurchases, and cash. Li, Lundholm, and Minnis: diminishing marginal returns on investment. Dyer, Lang, and Stice-Lawrence: composition of the 10-K over time. — sources in the feature catalogue above.
- Tension to preserve. Brown and Tucker find that investors' absolute filing-date reaction is larger when the MD&A was modified, while Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen find no announcement effect to document changes and a large subsequent alpha. Plausible reasons, not tested here: absolute versus signed returns, MD&A-only versus full filing, and "was it modified at all" versus "how unusual is the wording relative to the cross-section of changers." They should not be merged into one feature story.

### Inferences

- A model that is supposed to forecast excess returns after the filing is public should start from changes (similarity, tone deltas, added and deleted risk headings), not from the level of a negative-word percentage. The level is mostly already in the 4-day window, and even that window is fragile inside day 0–1.
- Label Buehlmaier–Whited and Jiang et al. so a later shortlist does not treat them as the same economic bet as lazy prices. One pays you if constraints are risky, one pays you if managers as a group are too optimistic, one pays you if nobody reads the redline.

### Gaps

- No source opened here decomposes lazy-prices alpha into a component correlated with subsequent earnings news versus a component that remains after earnings are controlled, beyond the authors' statement that 10-K changes predict earnings, profitability, news, and bankruptcies.
- Whether Feldman tone-change drift survives modern factor models is not in the abstract.
- The one-day Tetlock delay was not re-estimated on filings. Loughran and McDonald's own [0, 1] versus [0, 3] split is the filing-side evidence, and it says the proportional dictionary signal is not a same-day reaction.

## What survives size, value, momentum, profitability, investment, and industry controls?

### Takeaway

Almost none of these papers run the full modern battery (size, value, momentum, profitability, investment, and industry) and then still show a post-filing mean return. The explicit Fama–French five-factor survivor is text-based financial constraints, especially debt constraints, and it is stronger in large liquid stocks, which is the opposite of a limits-to-arbitrage underreaction. Lazy prices survives a factor model that includes market, size, value, momentum, and liquidity in the appendix that was read; profitability and investment were not in that factor list. Loughran–McDonald's filing-window result survives size, book-to-market, turnover, and industry, but not a return-factor model, because the left-hand side is a 4-day excess return.

### Cited Findings

- Loughran and McDonald 2011. Fama–MacBeth, industry dummies, size, book-to-market, turnover. Fin-Neg remains significant (t = 2.64) for [0, 3] excess returns. Momentum, profitability, and investment are not in the specification. The dependent variable is not a monthly characteristic-adjusted return. — [PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)
- Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen appendix Table A-1 (risk-factor section, 2003–2014). Q5−Q1 returns are reported as excess returns, Fama–French 3-factor alphas (market, size, value), and 5-factor alphas defined in that table header as market, size, value, momentum, and liquidity. Several similarity measures remain significant on that 5-factor intercept (for example the equally weighted Jaccard 3-factor intercept is 1.15 percent per month). Profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) are not named in the table header that was read. Industry adjustment of the similarity score itself is not described in the pages read; quintiles use the prior year's cross-sectional distribution. — [NBER appendix](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w25084/w25084.appendix.pdf)
- Buehlmaier and Whited. The constraint return "cannot be explained by the Fama and French (2015) factor model," which is market, size, value, profitability, and investment. They average out size quantiles so the spread is not a size effect. A zero-cost debt-constraint portfolio earns 6.5 percent annualized risk-adjusted excess return; the authors' summary also gives 7.2 percent annualized alpha for the debt measure in the top market-cap percentile. Momentum is not listed as one of the five factors they partial out in the abstract. The premium is largest for large, liquid stocks, so a limits-to-arbitrage story is a poor fit. — [Oxford](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/31/7/2693/4824924); [Harvard Law](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/03/10/are-financial-constraints-priced-evidence-from-textual-analysis/)
- Jiang, Lee, Martin, and Zhou. The headline test is a time-series predictive regression for the market excess return, so cross-sectional factor controls are not the point. The cross-sectional stock-return predictability is stronger for difficult-to-value and costly-to-arbitrage firms. A full size/value/momentum/profitability/investment sort was not extracted. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302770)
- Feldman et al. Incremental to accruals and earnings surprises, and dependent on the information environment. No size, value, momentum, profitability, or investment factor test is in the abstract. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v15y2010i4d10.1007_s11142-009-9111-x.html)
- Li 2010. FLS tone predicts future earnings after "other determinants of future performance." The accrual-return interaction is explicitly a control for the accrual anomaly: the anomaly is absent when tone warns. Industry and factor controls for a tone long-short were not claimed. — [Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00382.x)
- Li, Lundholm, and Minnis. The competition measure has both across-industry and within-industry variation, and each is related to future diminishing returns. That is an industry control on a profitability outcome, not on stock returns. — [Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2012.00472.x)
- Bodnaruk, Loughran, and McDonald. Constraining-word frequency has low correlation with market capitalization, unlike the SA and WW indexes, and predicts financing events in the cross-section. Again not a factor-adjusted return. — [manuscript](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/wbs/subjects/finance/events/seminars/using_10-k_text_to_gauge_financial_constraints.pdf)
- Campbell et al. and Hope et al. include "numerous determinants" or show that disclosure tracks pre-existing risk. Those are controls for whether the disclosure is boilerplate, not a demonstration that a long-short survives Fama–French five factors. — [Campbell SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1694279); [Hope SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2749132)
- Chen and Yang cache: the peer-momentum alpha of 1.803 percent (t = 3.33) is "after controlling for Fama and French" factors. Which factors, and whether profitability and investment are included, was not readable in the fragment. — [cache](https://scholar.googleusercontent.com/scholar?q=cache:udHDV8ECyWsJ:scholar.google.com/&hl=ja&as_sdt=2005&sciodt=0,5)

### Inferences

- The control hurdle the user cares about (size, value, momentum, profitability, investment, industry) is cleared, on the pages read, only by Buehlmaier and Whited, and even there momentum is not named and the economic story is risk, not underreaction. Lazy prices clears market, size, value, momentum, and liquidity in one appendix table, which is close but not the same list.
- Industry matters in two different ways that a later model should not collapse. Loughran and McDonald put industry dummies in the short-window regression because Harvard negative words proxy for industries (their example is commodity language). Li, Lundholm, and Minnis show competition language has a within-industry component that still predicts diminishing returns. Kravet and Muslu find industry-year-relative risk language behaves differently from raw risk language. An LLM score should be peer-adjusted inside industry-year, not only raw.

### Gaps

- No opened paper reports a textual long-short that is explicitly residualized on momentum, profitability, and investment together and is still a drift rather than a constraint premium.
- Post-filing alphas for Feldman tone change, You–Zhang complexity-interacted drift, and Jegadeesh–Wu two-week tone, after those controls, were not found.
- Whether lazy-prices alpha is industry-neutral (demeaning similarity inside industry-year) was not in the appendix pages read.

## What has decayed after publication, and what failed replication?

### Takeaway

There is no McLean–Pontiff-style journal study of this specific text-feature set. The general anomaly result is that published predictor portfolios lose about 26 percent of their return out of sample and about 58 percent after publication. The only direct re-test located for filing tone is a 15-page 2019 student thesis: the Loughran–McDonald 4-day Fin-Neg pattern replicates in 1994–2008 and disappears in 2008–2018. That is a weak decay flag, not a failed journal replication. Separately, several famous measurement choices failed inside the original papers: Harvard, Diction, General Inquirer, and LIWC do not do the job in 10-Ks; Fog does not measure financial readability; raw positive-word counts fail unless they are reweighted.

### Cited Findings

- General benchmark, not text-specific. R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff, "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?," Journal of Finance, forthcoming as of the January 2015 draft. Across 97 predictors, portfolio returns are 26 percent lower out of sample and 58 percent lower post-publication. The gap between those two (about 32 percent) is their estimate of publication-informed trading. Declines are larger for predictors with higher in-sample returns. Post-publication, predictor portfolios become more correlated with each other. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2156623). A later study of systematic strategies finds a similar post-publication Sharpe drop (about 43 to 50 percent depending on the writeup) and finds that publication year alone explains a large share of decay. Those samples are CRSP/Compustat anomalies, and the authors say they are not built from text features. — [Taylor & Francis summary](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14697688.2022.2098810)
- Filing-tone decay flag, low quality. An Aalto University thesis approved in 2019 (15 pages, advisor Theresa Spickers) rebuilds a frequency-based Fin-Neg sort and daily returns for 1994–2008 and for 2008–2018. The author reports that the 4-day negative-return pattern replicates in the original window and that "the effect of decreased returns disappears" in 2008–2018. The author suggests, without a trading-volume or publication-date break test, that investors may have implemented the strategy after the 2011 publication. This is not a journal replication, the design was not audited here, and the thesis itself concludes that frequency-based negative tone is not sufficient to predict returns reliably. — [Aalto PDF](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/253e57c2-b522-4078-82cb-4524ac8c3d2e/content)
- Failed dictionaries, inside the original studies. Harvard-IV negative counts have no reliable relation to 10-K [0, 3] excess returns; Fin-Neg does (Loughran and McDonald 2011). Diction, General Inquirer, and LIWC tone of MD&A do not positively predict future performance, while Naive Bayes tone of forward-looking sentences does (Li 2010). — [Loughran–McDonald PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [Li 2010 Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00382.x)
- Failed readability measure. Fog is poorly specified for filings; file size dominates it for volatility, absolute earnings surprise, and forecast dispersion (Loughran and McDonald 2014). File size itself is later shown to be contaminated by HTML, XML, and PDFs (Bonsall, Leone, Miller, and Rennekamp 2017). Neither paper shows Fog predicting mean returns and then failing; the failure is of the measure. — [Harvard Law](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/); [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-plain-english-measure-of-financial-reporting-readability/)
- Positive words. Proportional positive counts are not a significant filing-return predictor in Loughran and McDonald's proportional panel. They become significant once tf-idf weighted (same paper, term-weight panel) or once weighted by historical return reactions (Jegadeesh and Wu 2013). The failure is of equal-weighted positive unigrams, not of the idea that positive language can matter. — [Loughran–McDonald PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [Jegadeesh–Wu](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13002328)
- MD&A usefulness declined inside the sample, not after an anomaly publication. Brown and Tucker document falling MD&A modification scores and a weakening price reaction to those scores over the decade before their 2011 paper, even as MD&As lengthened. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1452977)
- Risk-factor informativeness may have fallen after the financial crisis for reasons other than academic publication. A ResearchGate paper, "Are Risk Factor Disclosures Still Relevant?," reports weaker market reactions and weaker links from Item 1A language to next-period bankruptcy rank in 2009–2014 than in 2006–2008, and says the decline is not explained by repetition. This was not read as a journal version and is only a decay lead. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326778437_Are_Risk_Factor_Disclosures_Still_Relevant_Evidence_from_Market_Reactions_to_Risk_Factor_Disclosures_Before_and_After_the_Financial_Crisis)
- No post-publication replication of lazy prices, of Feldman drift, of Buehlmaier–Whited constraint alphas, or of Jiang manager-sentiment R-squared was found in this search. Lazy prices' sample ends in 2014 and the paper appears in the Journal of Finance in 2020, so a true post-publication window exists and was not tested here. A 2026 vendor blog repeats the original 188 bp figure; it is not an out-of-sample test. — [Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)

### Inferences

- Expect the 4-day Fin-Neg trading rule, if it ever paid, to be the most decayed object, because it is simple, it was published in the Journal of Finance in 2011, and the only re-test located says it is gone after 2008. Do not confuse that with decay of lazy prices, which is a different feature and has no public post-2020 replication either way.
- Measurement failures (wrong dictionary, Fog, raw file size, equal-weighted positive words) are better documented than economic decay. An LLM rebuild should treat those as settled negative results.

### Gaps

- No journal replication or post-2020 out-of-sample test of Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen was found.
- The Aalto thesis does not report factor-adjusted alphas, transaction costs, or a break date tied to the Journal of Finance publication month. Its 2008–2018 sample overlaps the original sample in 2008 and may differ in parsing and in the return window.
- McLean and Pontiff's 97 predictors were not checked one-by-one to see whether any textual characteristic was in the set. The abstract's examples are standard cross-sectional predictors.
- Decay of Item 1A informativeness after 2008 is from a non-journal PDF and was not matched to a stable citation.

## How should an LLM re-implement these features, without inventing a new claim?

### Takeaway

The papers already say what a dictionary and a cosine miss. Score changes against the firm's own last filing, not the document in isolation. Score sections separately, and do not average Item 1A, MD&A, and the footnotes into one tone number. Separate boilerplate that is shared with industry-year peers from sentences that name a firm-specific exposure, a number, or a deleted heading. Reweight or recontextualize words; equal-weighted Harvard or even equal-weighted Fin-Neg is the version that is weakest out of sample. Residualize tone on the quantitative statements in the same filing before calling it tone management, and do not expect that residual to have the same sign as raw negative tone. Do not turn Fog or gross file size into a return feature.

### Cited Findings

- Use a finance-specific or price-supervised lexicon, not a general one. Harvard negative words are about three-quarters misclassified in 10-Ks and do not predict filing returns. Diction, General Inquirer, and LIWC do not predict future performance from MD&A. Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer's price-supervised dictionaries, with a cross-validation holdout and with bigrams, beat Loughran–McDonald bag-of-words out of sample for price movements, and call-trained dictionaries transfer to 10-K filing reactions better than the static list. — [Loughran–McDonald PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [Li 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00382.x); [Garcia draft](https://sites.insead.edu/facultyresearch/research/file.cfm?fid=68633)
- Term weights matter as much as the list. tf-idf makes every Loughran–McDonald list except litigious significant for filing-window returns, including positive words. Jegadeesh and Wu's weights, fit on past return reactions, are what make positive language work, and their document scores have low correlation with tf-idf scores built on the same lexicon. An LLM that emits a single "negative" probability should be calibrated to returns or to subsequent earnings, not asked to imitate a 2011 word list, if the goal is the Jegadeesh–Wu or Garcia object. If the goal is to replicate Fin-Neg, the list must stay fixed. — [Loughran–McDonald PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [Jegadeesh–Wu](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13002328)
- Changes beat levels for the drift that exists. Feldman et al. use the change in MD&A tone versus the prior 10-Q or 10-K. Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen use four overlap measures versus the prior filing and show that the return is in the change. Loughran and McDonald themselves find that simply differencing negative-word proportions does not clearly dominate levels for the 4-day window, so a delta of a dictionary count is not a substitute for a real redline. The LLM version supported by lazy prices is: align sections, list material added and deleted sentences, and down-weight edits that are pure boilerplate. — [Feldman IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v15y2010i4d10.1007_s11142-009-9111-x.html); [Cohen HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [Loughran–McDonald PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)
- Section, not the whole file. For the 4-day Fin-Neg association, MD&A-only counts are not a clearer signal than the full 10-K. For subsequent returns in Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen, changes are concentrated in MD&A, but the especially informative language is executive-team, litigation, and, in the working-paper abstract, the risk-factor section. Campbell and Hope are Item 1A only. Li 2010 and Muslu et al. are forward-looking sentences inside MD&A, not the whole MD&A. Dyer, Lang, and Stice-Lawrence show that length growth is mostly fair value, internal controls, and risk factors. An LLM should emit separate scores for Item 1 (business), Item 1A, Item 3 (legal proceedings), Item 7 (MD&A), and the notes, and should not be graded on a single document-level cosine. — catalogue sources above
- Peer-relative and specificity, grounded in what the papers actually measured. Hope, Hu, and Lu's specificity (concrete risk-factor language, associated with larger absolute filing-window returns and better analyst risk assessment) is the supported alternative to adjective counts. Kravet and Muslu already compare the firm's risk text to the same industry-year. Campbell et al. show that the category of risk (systematic, idiosyncratic, financial, legal, tax) is the informative object, not a single pessimism score. The 2023 TAR result says the addition or deletion of a named risk heading moves the variance risk premium even after coarser tone measures. A defensible LLM output is: list added headings, deleted headings, and whether each heading is specific (names a customer, a lawsuit, a covenant, a number) or shared with peers. Do not claim that this list has a published equity long-short; the published consequences are |CAR|, volatility, betas, and VRP. — [Hope PDF](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/soa_research/article/2648/viewcontent/BenefitsSpecificRisk_FactorDisclosures_2016.pdf); [Kravet–Muslu SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1736228); [Campbell SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1694279); [TAR 2023](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/98/6/327/11227/Changes-in-Risk-Factor-Disclosures-and-the)
- Residual tone versus raw tone. Huang, Teoh, and Zhang's result is that the part of positive tone not explained by performance, risk, and complexity predicts worse future earnings and a later return reversal. Raw positive tone does the opposite job in dictionary papers. An LLM "tone management" flag has to be the residual, and the corpus those authors used is the earnings press release. Applying the same residual idea to a 10-K is an extrapolation, not a published result. — [AAA](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/89/3/1083/3532/Tone-Management)
- Do not rebuild Fog or gross file size. Loughran and McDonald 2014 reject Fog and propose complete-submission file size as a proxy for how hard it is to get valuation-relevant information, linked to volatility and absolute surprise, not to mean returns. Bonsall et al. 2017 show that file-size variation is largely markup and attachments. Dyer et al. 2017 show length is regulation. If an LLM is asked for readability, the supported target is whether the valuation-relevant sentences are buried, not the Gunning formula and not the EDGAR byte count. — [Harvard Law](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/); [Bonsall abstract](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-plain-english-measure-of-financial-reporting-readability/); [Dyer SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2741682)
- Forward-looking versus backward-looking. Li 2010's Naive Bayes tone is computed on sentences identified as forward-looking, and that is what predicts earnings. Whole-document dictionary tone does not. Muslu et al. further split operations-related forward-looking disclosures, which are the ones that move price efficiency. An LLM should tag tense and target (operations versus liquidity versus litigation) before scoring tone. — [Li 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00382.x); [Muslu SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/forward-looking-md-a-disclosures-and-the-information-3niyzindg0)
- Attribution language is a different feature from tone. Li's 2012 manuscript uses first-person versus other pronouns in MD&A, conditional on performance. Gong (2024) uses external causal attributions and tries to split credible from strategic. Neither is a post-filing drift factor in the sources read. An LLM can tag "we executed" versus "the economy deteriorated" as a separate field. It should not be folded into Fin-Neg. — [Li manuscript](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files/f84c92eee602b014fba808ae80a2b27b.pdf); [Gong 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jcaf.22707)
- Constraints and competition are narrative facts, not sentiment. Buehlmaier and Whited's return result comes from whether the annual report says the firm cannot raise debt or equity. Bodnaruk, Loughran, and McDonald use a constraining-word list and get financing events, not a verified alpha. Hoberg and Phillips' return evidence uses product-description similarity and industry booms, not negative adjectives. An LLM should answer "does this filing say they are shut out of the debt market, and is that new?" as its own feature, with the sign "higher future average return if you believe the constraint premium," not "lower future return because the wording is gloomy." — [Buehlmaier PDF](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/261048/1/Content.pdf); [Bodnaruk manuscript](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/wbs/subjects/finance/events/seminars/using_10-k_text_to_gauge_financial_constraints.pdf)
- Timing. Anything copied from Loughran and McDonald's proportional lists is a [0, 3] association that fails on [0, 1]. It is a poor feature for a strategy that rebalances monthly after the filing is old. Anything copied from Jegadeesh and Wu has a two-week shelf life. Feldman tone change is held until the next earnings release. Lazy prices is held about three months. Jiang sentiment is a one-month market forecast. Mixing them in one label called "12-month text alpha" is not supported. — catalogue sources above
- What not to invent. No opened paper shows that an LLM judgment of "MD&A quality," a transformer embedding, or a chain-of-thought redline outperforms cosine similarity or Fin-Neg in a post-filing long-short. Garcia et al. is the closest published upgrade, and it is still a supervised dictionary tested on short-window price moves. The legitimate LLM use, given this literature, is to produce the features the papers already showed matter and that bag-of-words measures badly: section-level edits, specific versus boilerplate risk headings, added versus deleted risks, forward-looking versus backward-looking tone, and constraint language residualized on the numbers.

### Inferences

- A practical feature vector that stays inside the evidence would be, per filing once it is public: similarity to the prior filing by section (MD&A, Item 1A, legal, CEO/CFO sentences); a redline of added and deleted Item 1A headings flagged as specific or peer-shared; MD&A tone change on forward-looking sentences only, using a finance lexicon or a price-supervised one; a residual of positive tone after the filing's own earnings, accruals, and risk numbers, labeled as a reversal candidate and down-weighted because the published evidence is press releases; a debt-constraint narrative flag, labeled as a risk premium; document length only as a moderator of how large the initial underreaction might be, and only after stripping exhibits and markup. Levels of Fog, Harvard negative counts, and gross file size should be omitted as return features.
- Peer adjustment should be inside industry-year, because that is the comparison Kravet and Muslu, Campbell, and Li–Lundholm–Minnis actually use. A cosine to the prior filing already differences out the firm's own boilerplate; it does not difference out an industry shock that rewrote everyone's risk factors in the same quarter. Cohen-style similarity and industry-relative similarity answer different questions.

### Gaps

- No paper opened here estimates the incremental R-squared of a human or LLM reading of the redline over the four mechanical similarity measures in Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen.
- Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer do not report whether their dictionaries predict 3- to 12-month returns after the filing window. Transferring their short-window win into a drift feature would be a new claim.
- The right specificity rubric for an LLM (named entities, quantities, covenant thresholds) is only partly specified by Hope, Hu, and Lu's algorithm, which was not re-implemented here. Copying their published measure is safer than inventing a new one and claiming their |CAR| result.
- Whether conference-call tone should be stripped out of Jiang et al.'s manager-sentiment index, leaving a pure 10-K/10-Q aggregate, was not tested in the sources read. The published index mixes the two.
