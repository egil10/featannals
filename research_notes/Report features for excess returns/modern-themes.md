# Modern filing-text themes and future excess returns (evidence notes, 2015–2026)

Scope for the report writer: firm-level disclosure themes an LLM could score, and whether published evidence shows they predict future excess stock returns (alpha or post-filing drift) rather than only fundamentals, volatility, valuation levels, or a risk premium. Coverage window used here runs through 2026-10-30. No final top-30 shortlist. Numbers below appear only when a retrieved source states them. Working papers and student theses are labeled as such. Earnings-call measures are included only when they are the canonical paper for a theme the assignment names, and are marked as not 10-K text.

## 1. Cybersecurity risk language in Item 1A / Item 1C

### Takeaway
Florackis, Louca, Michaely, and Weber (RFS 2023) is the primary result: a 10-K Item 1A cybersecurity-risk score earns a large positive return spread that the authors interpret as a priced risk premium, not as underreaction, because high-score firms do badly when cybersecurity concern rises. A published replication that kills the premium was not found; Item 1C itself has no return evidence in the sources retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Construct: firm-level cybersecurity risk from textual analysis of 10-K “Item 1A. Risk Factors,” built by comparing cybersecurity-risk disclosures of firms that were hacked with firms that were not. The score identifies firms that discuss cybersecurity extensively, is higher in IT-intensive industries, correlates with characteristics of firms later hit by attacks, and predicts future cyberattacks. Data companion: Florackis, Louca, Michaely, and Weber, Chicago Booth Research Paper No. 23-01 / SSRN. — [SSRN data paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4319606); [RFS abstract via EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.); [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/36/1/351/6585907)
- Publication: Chris Florackis, Christodoulos Louca, Roni Michaely, and Michael Weber, “Cybersecurity Risk,” Review of Financial Studies 36(1), 351–407 (2023); NBER w28196. JEL G12, G14, G32. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28196); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.)
- Disclosure window used in the authors’ textual comparison, as described by Florackis’s university summary: 10-Ks from 2007 to 2018. — [University of Liverpool Management School](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/management/blog/research/predicting-cybersecurity-nightmares/)
- Sign and magnitude: high-exposure portfolios outperform. Equal-weighted long–short excess return of 66 to 69 basis points per month, stated as 8.3% per year; value-weighted analogue 7.9% per year. NBER working-paper text: “up to 8.3% per year in terms of equal-weighted (7.9% value-weighted) returns.” — [NBER PDF text mirror](http://dx.doi.org/10.3386/w28196)
- Horizon: Fama–MacBeth regressions show a positive relation, and “cybersecurity risk predicts cross-sectional variation in stock returns up to 12 months into the future.” — [NBER PDF text mirror](http://dx.doi.org/10.3386/w28196)
- State dependence, which is the authors’ risk-premium evidence: high-exposure firms “perform poorly in periods of high cybersecurity risk” / “in periods of increasing attention to cybersecurity risk.” Liverpool summary: the premium compensates investors “despite poor performance in periods of high cybersecurity risk.” — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.); [CESifo working-paper blurb](https://www.ifo.de/en/cesifo/publications/2020/working-paper/cybersecurity-risk); [Liverpool](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/management/blog/research/predicting-cybersecurity-nightmares/)
- Interpretation in the source: priced cross-sectional cybersecurity risk (a premium with a bad state), plus a separate real outcome that the score predicts future attacks. Not presented as filing-day drift. — [NBER PDF text mirror](http://dx.doi.org/10.3386/w28196)
- Item 1C is a different object. A 2025 Computers & Security study of 61 ransomware targets (2018–2021) notes that the SEC’s cybersecurity rule created Item 1C for risk-management and governance disclosure, mandatory for all public companies starting December 2023. That paper does not report a return premium. It does say boilerplate is a known problem in these filings and cites prior work that regulatory pressure without rigid requirements reduced valuable information (Li et al. 2018; Nelson and Pritchard 2016) and that generic recycled text reduces informativeness (Gao et al. 2020). It lists Florackis et al. (2023) as one of several papers that proxy cybersecurity with disclosure length or phrase frequency. — [ScienceDirect, Computers & Security article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167404825003153)
- No retrieved source is a published replication or critique that re-estimates the 8.3% / 7.9% spread out of sample or shows it is spanned by Fama–French five factors plus momentum. EconPapers shows 12 citations as of the crawl, which is not a critique. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.)

### Inferences
- Best current reading: a characteristic premium tied to Item 1A language, horizon inside a year, with the risk-premium signature (loses money when the cyber-risk state is bad). It is not evidence of post-filing drift.
- The score can still load on boilerplate volume. Florackis et al. discipline the dictionary with hacked versus non-hacked language, which is stricter than counting the phrase “cybersecurity risk,” but it is not an LLM materiality judgment that isolates a quantified incident from generic Item 1A text.
- Item 1C (governance process) should not be pooled with Item 1A (risk exposure) until someone shows Item 1C language predicts returns.

### Gaps
- Exact factor model in the published RFS tables (which of market, size, value, momentum, profitability, investment are partialled out of the 66–69 bp) was not in the retrieved pages. Do not describe the 8.3% as a five-factor alpha unless the table is re-opened.
- No post-2022 out-of-sample replication, and no return result for Item 1C, was found.
- No horse-race of dictionary cyber score versus an LLM that flags only quantified incidents or material breaches.

## 2. Climate and ESG disclosure text versus the carbon premium and the greenium

### Takeaway
Firm-level climate text does not have one settled excess-return fact. Sautner, van Lent, Vilkov, and Zhang’s call-based exposure has an insignificant unconditional realized premium and only a small, time-varying expected premium. A ClimateBERT Item 1A study reports a physical-risk premium and no stable transition-risk premium. The Bolton–Kacperczyk carbon premium is an emissions result, not a text result, and by 2024–2026 it is contested on scaling, vendor estimates, and look-ahead timing. Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor’s greenium says past green outperformance was a surprise, so expected returns on green assets are lower, not higher.

### Cited Findings
- Sautner, van Lent, Vilkov, and Zhang, “Firm-level Climate Change Exposure” (Journal of Finance, forthcoming as of the SSRN revision dated 19 February 2023): machine-learning keyword discovery on earnings calls, not 10-Ks. Three exposures: opportunity, physical, and regulatory. More than 10,000 firms, 34 countries, 2002–2020. Predicts real outcomes (jobs in disruptive green technologies and green patenting). “Contain information that is priced in options and equity markets.” — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3642508)
- Same authors, “Pricing Climate Change Exposure” (SSRN, 24 April 2022): S&P 500, attention in earnings calls, 2005–2020. Unconditional risk premium extracted from realized returns is insignificant. It was positive before the financial crisis and rose afterward. Forward-looking expected-return proxies give an unconditionally positive premium, with maximum values of 0.5% to 1% per year, depending on the proxy, between 2011 and 2014. The premium is lower after 2015, especially when the proxy accounts for higher opportunities and lower crash risk of high-exposure stocks. The priced part “primarily originates from uncertainty about climate-related upside opportunities.” In the time series the premium is negatively associated with green innovation, Big Three holdings, and ESG fund flows, and positively associated with adaptation programs. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3792366); [HKU summary, 18 Dec 2024](https://hkujcesgri.hku.hk/pricing-climate-change-exposure/)
- HKU numerical path for the expected-premium proxies: from 2011, an MW-based premium rose to about 0.5% per year in 2012 and a GLB-based premium to about 1% per year between 2012 and 2014; since 2015 both reverted to almost zero, with MW slightly higher. — [HKU summary](https://hkujcesgri.hku.hk/pricing-climate-change-exposure/)
- An earlier February 2021 draft of the pricing paper (sample framed as 2003–2019) says the unconditional realized premium is zero, masking a rise from zero in 2003 to 2% per year before the financial crisis and a sharp decline into negative territory in 2007–2009. Treat 2% as a draft time-series description, not the later SSRN’s unconditional result (insignificant). — [Banque de France PDF, February 2021](https://acpr.banque-france.fr/sites/default/files/medias/documents/20210921_vilkov_etal_2021.pdf)
- Same research group, “Values Discovery” (SSRN, 9 October 2023): RAG method on 2.1 million conversations, 379,605 earnings calls, 85 countries, 2003–2024. After 2015, portfolios sorted on values-oriented climate questions earn 2.2% higher annual returns than portfolios of firms with generic climate questions, “consistent with buying pressure from impact investors.” This is a realized-return difference on call content, attributed to demand, not a 10-K text premium. Analysts incorporate only about half of new information into the next forecast; values-oriented questioning closes roughly one-fifth to one-third of that gap (fundamentals / information production, not a return magnitude). — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4596668)
- 10-K text, valuation and hedge covariance, not an unconditional alpha: Berkman, Jona, and Soderstrom, “Firm-Specific Climate Risk and Market Valuation,” Accounting, Organizations and Society (2024). ClimateRisk scores climate-relevant 10-K excerpts by length and word relevance (Ceres/CookESG, Russell 3000). Market valuations are significantly negatively associated with ClimateRisk and with an industry-year standardized version, incremental to Bloomberg environmental disclosure quality, and the slides say the measure outperforms emission intensity, MSCI, and Sustainalytics in explaining valuation. A hedge long high ClimateRisk and short low has the strongest negative association with a US-news climate-concern index: high text-climate-risk stocks fall when climate concerns rise. Coefficient more negative after the Paris Agreement. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361368224000072); [AUT slides naming the 2024 AOS paper](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/947214/2.-Henk_AUT_CC.pdf)
- 10-K Item 1A and a claimed physical-risk premium: Vu Le Tran, Thomas Leirvik, Morten Parschat, and Petter Schive, “Climate-Change Risk and Stocks’ Return” (working paper, 24 May 2023). They use a climate-risk dataset from a Google BERT model refined by Kölbel et al. (2022), who fine-tuned BERT on Item 1A sentences into general, transitional, and physical climate risk rather than sentence counts. Physical climate risk has a positive premium of 1.5–2.9% per year, not attributed to other known risk factors or industry differences. “There is no consistent premium related to climate transitional risk.” Working paper, not a journal confirmation retrieved here. — [Author PDF via Google scholar cache](https://scholar.googleusercontent.com/scholar?q=cache:wjQ-azAhDYsJ:scholar.google.com/&hl=th&as_sdt=0,5)
- Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor, “Dissecting Green Returns,” Journal of Financial Economics 146(2), 403–424 (November 2022); NBER w28940. Not a filing-text measure. Green assets’ high realized returns reflect unexpectedly strong increases in environmental concerns, not high expected returns. German green bonds outperformed higher-yielding non-green twins as the greenium widened. US green stocks outperformed brown as climate concerns strengthened. Ex ante, implied cost of capital is lower for green than brown throughout the sample (“consistently negative equity greenium”). Ex post, realized returns purged of climate-concern and earnings shocks also imply lower expected returns for green stocks. Setting climate shocks to zero makes the green-minus-brown counterfactual essentially flat; green would have underperformed with no surprises to climate concerns or to green-versus-brown earnings. A green factor explains much of value’s recent underperformance. Bond illustration only: on 8 September 2020 the green-bond greenium used as the expected gap was −1.6 bps. No equity greenium in percent per year was in the retrieved pages. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3870942); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X22001672); [BFI summary PDF](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Dissecting-Green-Returns.pdf)
- Bolton and Kacperczyk carbon premium is emissions, not text. “Carbon Premium Around the World” (CEPR DP14567, April 2020): stock-return premium on carbon emissions for over 14,400 firms in 77 countries; higher emissions associated with higher stock returns in Asia, Europe, and North America; direct and supply-chain (indirect) emissions both matter; premium rising in recent years. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3594188)
- Secondary summary of Bolton and Kacperczyk, “Do Investors Care about Carbon Risk?”: after size, book-to-market, momentum, and other anomalies, a one-standard-deviation increase in the level and the change of scope 1 emissions is associated with 1.8% and 3.1% higher annualized returns; scope 2, 2.9% and 2.2%; scope 3, 4.0% and 3.8%. The summary says the premium is not explained by unexpected profitability and that the authors read it as compensation for carbon-emission risk. These percentages are from the secondary write-up, not from a JFE table opened in this session. — [Advisor Perspectives, summarizing the 2020 study](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2022/02/07/carbon-emissions-emerge-as-a-factor-that-explains-returns)
- Critique: Aswani, Raghunandan, and Rajgopal (2024a, as cited by a 2026 ECGI draft) find the carbon premium insignificant for emissions intensity (emissions scaled by sales) or when using disclosed rather than estimated emissions. Bolton and Kacperczyk’s comment says absolute emissions are the right transition-risk measure, that ARR’s results do not contradict a premium on levels and changes, and that the premium for firms that disclose emissions is positive. They also say there was no significant premium just before Paris and a large premium after. — [Bolton and Kacperczyk comment PDF](https://scispace.com/pdf/are-carbon-emissions-associated-with-stock-returns-comment-3fkilr26.pdf); [ECGI draft PDF, dated in crawl 2026-10-22](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/2026-10/fin-940-2023_does_the_carbon_premium_reflect_risk_or_outperformance.pdf)
- ECGI finance working paper “Does the carbon premium reflect risk or outperformance?” (file fin-940-2023; PDF timestamp in crawl 22 October 2026): replicates Bolton–Kacperczyk for 2005–2017 and extends the sample to 2005–2023. Reports no significantly positive coefficients on emissions intensities, and states consistency with Aswani, Raghunandan, and Rajgopal (2024a) that the premium is driven by the specification choices those authors criticize. Author names were not on the retrieved pages; do not invent them. — [ECGI PDF](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/2026-10/fin-940-2023_does_the_carbon_premium_reflect_risk_or_outperformance.pdf)
- Timing critique, secondary: Markwat, Hanauer, and Swinkels, “The Illusion of the Carbon Premium,” as described by Gertjan Verdickt (Investment Officer, 23 March 2026). Claim: Bolton–Kacperczyk associate full calendar-year emissions with returns inside that same year, so November–December emissions are aligned with January returns before those emissions have occurred. When emissions are lagged to information investors could have had, the premium “disappears completely”; without sector effects coefficients are insignificant; reported-only emissions show no positive relation. The column calls the original result a disguised revenue premium. This is a column summarizing a paper, not the paper’s tables. — [Investment Officer](https://cms.investmentofficer.com/en/columns/gertjan-verdickt/carbon-premium-never-existed)
- A Journal of Climate Finance survey article states the conflict cleanly: panel regressions in Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021, 2022) are the strongest carbon-premium evidence; Aswani et al. (2022) argue estimated emissions make results unreliable; most studies are US public firms; methodology choices are consequential. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2949728023000020)

### Inferences
- Do not treat “climate text predicts alpha” as established. The call-based Sautner premium is about zero in realized returns and at most roughly 0.5–1% in expected-return proxies, and it faded after 2015. The 2.2% values-oriented call spread is a post-2015 demand/realized-return result.
- The only retrieved Item 1A return premium is physical risk in a 2023 working paper (1.5–2.9%), with transition language not priced. That is the opposite of treating all climate words as one signal. Berkman et al. show 10-K climate language explains valuation and climate-news covariance beyond emissions intensity and ESG scores; that is a hedge and a discount, not a positive alpha.
- Text exposure after emissions data: Berkman is the incremental-to-emissions result, and it is about valuation and news betas. A horse-race in which 10-K climate text predicts future excess returns after scaled, lagged, reported emissions was not retrieved.
- Pástor–Stambaugh–Taylor and Bolton–Kacperczyk are easy to mash together and should not be. PST: green has lower expected returns; high past green returns were unexpected concern shocks. BK: brown/high-emission has higher realized returns, read by the authors as a risk premium. Later critiques say the BK realized premium is fragile to intensity, disclosed-versus-estimated emissions, and lagging. Neither object is an LLM score of the 10-K.
- If a feature is built, split opportunity versus physical versus regulatory language. Pooling them mixes a possible opportunity premium, a hedge that loses when concern rises, and a contested emissions characteristic.

### Gaps
- Li, Shan, Tang, and Yao 10-K climate dictionary: not found in this search. Do not cite it.
- Journal status of Tran et al. (2023) and of Markwat, Hanauer, and Swinkels was not verified beyond the working-paper PDF and the March 2026 column.
- Equity greenium magnitude in percent for Pástor–Stambaugh–Taylor was not in the retrieved pages (only the sign, and −1.6 bps for one green bond on one day).
- No retrieved paper estimates Sautner-style exposure from 10-Ks and then shows incremental alpha after a correctly lagged emissions control.

## 3. Political, regulatory, trade, and policy risk language

### Takeaway
The standard firm-level political-risk measure (Hassan, Hollander, van Lent, and Tahoun, QJE 2019) is built from earnings calls, not 10-Ks, and the published effects are volatility, investment, hiring, and lobbying, not a cross-sectional return premium. A related SSRN paper reports a small priced premium of about 0.5–1.0% per year per standard deviation, only for firms that do not manage political risk. A 10-K-native political, trade, or regulatory premium was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Hassan, Hollander, van Lent, and Tahoun, “Firm-Level Political Risk: Measurement and Effects,” Quarterly Journal of Economics 134(4), 2135–2202 (2019); NBER w24029. Construct: share of the quarterly earnings call devoted to political risk. Method: political bigrams near synonyms for risk or uncertainty, term-frequency weighted, divided by call length (PRisk). PSentiment conditions on positive and negative tone words. Validation: picks calls that actually discuss political risks; intuitive time and sector variation; correlates with actions and stock-market volatility. Most variation is firm-level, not sector-by-time and not heterogeneous loadings on aggregate political risk. Dispersion of firm-level political risk rises when aggregate political risk is high. Topic-level political talk predicts lobbying on that topic next quarter, not on other topics. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24029); [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3419283); [data host](https://www.policyuncertainty.com/firm_pr.html)
- Real and volatility outcomes, not returns: increases in the measure associate with higher stock-return volatility and lower investment, planned capital expenditure, and hiring; firms later donate more, link to politicians, and lobby. Results hold after controlling for news about the mean of political shocks (PSentiment-type news) as opposed to risk. Sample on the data page: 9,481 US-listed firms, 2001–2016, with GVKey, PRisk, PSentiment, Risk, and Sentiment. — [NBER December 2017 revision PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24029/revisions/w24029.rev1.pdf); [policyuncertainty.com](https://www.policyuncertainty.com/firm_pr.html)
- Return result is a different paper: Gorbatikov, van Lent, Naik, Sharma, and Tahoun, “Is Firm-Level Political Exposure Priced?” (SSRN, 4 November 2019). Machine-learning firm-specific political risk, “independent of those returns.” A one-standard-deviation increase is associated with a 0.5% to 1.0% increase in annual returns. A related non-price measure of the mean of political shocks is used to separate discount-rate news from cash-flow news. Political risk is priced only for firms that do not actively manage it. Topic components are priced to different degrees. SSRN working paper; journal publication not confirmed here. The abstract does not say the text source is the 10-K. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3480494)
- Interpretation: Hassan et al. QJE is fundamentals and volatility. Gorbatikov et al. is a small risk premium, shut off when the firm manages political exposure (lobbying/donations), which is consistent with priced risk that can be hedged operationally, not with a stable underreaction alpha.

### Inferences
- For a filing-text feature, Hassan’s call measure is the wrong document. It is still the right benchmark: firm-level, topic-specific, and mostly idiosyncratic. An Item 1A or MD&A political score that only rediscovers industry-time regulation talk is not the object that mattered in QJE.
- 0.5–1.0% per year per standard deviation is economically small next to the cybersecurity spread. If it is a premium, factor-adjusted alpha after profitability and investment may be thin. Not shown in the SSRN abstract.
- Trade and regulatory topics are inside Hassan’s decomposition (lobbying is topic-specific) but this search did not retrieve a trade-war or tariff 10-K dictionary with a return premium.

### Gaps
- No retrieved paper constructs political, trade, or regulatory risk from 10-Ks (as opposed to calls) and reports a factor-adjusted return spread.
- Gorbatikov et al. factor controls, sample window, and whether the text is calls or filings were not in the abstract.
- Aggregate EPU indices were not reviewed; they are not firm-level filing text.

## 4. Supply-chain, customer, and geopolitical concentration narrative

### Takeaway
The filing-native return fact in this theme is the major-customer link that Cohen and Frazzini extract from SEC customer disclosures; this session did not re-open their published alpha, so no magnitude is stated. Customer-concentration level has conflicting pricing evidence: a higher implied cost of equity in JAE 2016, but lower realized excess returns in a University of Houston working paper. Reshoring evidence is an announcement-window valuation effect, not post-filing drift from 10-K narrative.

### Cited Findings
- The economic-link design uses filings. A University of Houston customer-concentration paper states that its customer information comes from the Compustat segment-link file compiled by Cohen and Frazzini (2008) and Cen et al. (2017), and that SFAS 131 requires disclosure of major customers in the 10-K. That is the filing source of the link. The Cohen and Frazzini (2008) supplier-return predictability magnitude was not re-retrieved in this session; do not invent it. — [UH PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/yerramilli/CustomerConcentration.pdf)
- Same UH paper’s own return result, which is about concentration level, not about customer-return shocks: on average, firms with major customers (more than 10% of revenue) have significantly lower excess returns than firms without major customers, after commonly used risk factors and firm characteristics. Stronger for small firms, young firms, and firms with lower analyst coverage (sentence cut off in the extract). They interpret major customers as lower systematic risk. Implied cost of capital is sensitive to the ICC measure; using one model-based ICC, firms with major customers have a lower ICC, in the same direction as their realized-return result. — [UH PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/yerramilli/CustomerConcentration.pdf)
- Opposite cost-of-capital sign: Journal of Accounting and Economics article (February 2016, doi 10.1016/j.jacceco.2015.03.005), “Customer concentration risk and the cost of equity capital.” A more concentrated corporate customer base is associated with a higher cost of equity and a higher cost of debt. Stronger when losing a major customer is more likely or more costly. A concentrated base of safer government customers is associated with a lower cost of equity. A supplier that depends on one or more major customers for at least 10% of revenue has a cost of equity 21.2 basis points higher, described as about $7.99 million per year of extra equity financing cost for the average supplier. This is an implied-cost / risk result, not a realized excess-return alpha. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410115000336)
- Fundamentals and disclosure quality, not alpha: a 2025/2026 British Accounting Review paper finds that suppliers with a more concentrated customer base produce less readable 10-Ks (9,554 US firms, 1994–2020), attributed to obfuscation, and that the combination of concentration and poor readability is associated with lower valuation and greater crash risk. A 1% increase in major-customer HHI is associated with a 1.11% higher Bog index and a 19.19% larger 10-K file size for the average firm. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S089083892500085X)
- Chinese listed firms, 2007–2021: customer concentration raises stock-price volatility via more volatile future operating cash flows and ROE. Volatility and fundamentals, not a US excess-return premium. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521925007392)
- Reshoring is an event study of announcements, not a 10-K language holding-period test. Cheng, Tang, Lo, Yeung, and Lam, Management Science 71(4), 3253–3282 (April 2025): text mining of reshoring risks plus market reactions to 281 reshoring initiatives by 132 US public firms, announcements from 2009 to 2022. Four risk types: foreign-currency fluctuation, IP protection, reshoring type (in-house, insourced, or outsourcing-to-outsourcing), and location politics. More positive reaction when reshoring under high currency fluctuation or from weak-IP countries. More negative when the announcement is insourced reshoring or the location is a Democrat- rather than Republican-led state. No significant reaction to outsourcing-to-outsourcing reshoring. — [PolyU record](http://hdl.handle.net/10397/109378)

### Inferences
- Two different trades are being conflated. (i) Slow characteristic: having a concentrated customer base. Evidence conflicts (higher ICC in JAE 2016 versus lower subsequent excess returns in the UH paper). Do not code “customer concentration narrative ⇒ positive alpha.” (ii) Slow link plus fast customer shock: the supplier’s return after the customer’s news, which is the Cohen–Frazzini mispricing design. The link is filing-extracted; the event time is the customer’s information release, not the supplier’s next 10-K.
- Single-source and geopolitical-concentration language in MD&A was not shown to predict a subsequent return premium. Reshoring “language” predicts the announcement return, which is a valuation jump, not drift after the 10-K.
- Readability results say concentration is a setting where narrative is more obfuscated. An LLM that only counts customer names will mix a risk disclosure with a growth narrative. The JAE cross-section (government customers lower the cost of equity; corporate customers raise it) is exactly the materiality split a keyword count misses.

### Gaps
- Cohen and Frazzini (2008) long–short magnitude, horizon, and factor adjustment were not re-read. Leave the number blank until the JF article is opened.
- No retrieved US excess-return paper on single-source language, nearshoring/reshoring words inside 10-Ks, or geopolitical supplier concentration as a characteristic that predicts post-filing alpha.
- UH paper author list and journal status were not on the extracted pages (hosted on Yerramilli’s Bauer faculty page).

## 5. Product-market competition, fluidity, and economic-moat language

### Takeaway
10-K competition language and product text predict profitability dynamics, payout policy, peer comovement, and the valuation level. They do not, in the sources retrieved, deliver a standalone moat premium in subsequent excess returns. Where returns show up, it is a conditional mispricing result: competitive industries that have just boomed subsequently earn lower abnormal returns. Product uniqueness raises valuation and does not reverse, so it is not an alpha.

### Cited Findings
- Li, Lundholm, and Minnis, “A Measure of Competition Based on 10-K Filings,” Journal of Accounting Research 51(2), 399–436 (May 2013). Construct: number of competition references in the 10-K, dropping phrases such as “less competitive,” scaled by document length. Fundamentals, not stock returns: return on net operating assets mean-reverts faster, and returns on new net operating assets diminish faster, when management refers to competition more. Mean-reversion coefficient on RNOA is −0.192 in the lowest decile and −0.278 in the highest. After that mean reversion, the rate of diminishing returns on new NOA is −0.086 in the lowest decile and −0.144 in the highest. The measure is related to industry Herfindahl but has within-industry variation that industry HHI cannot have. “Limited and indirect evidence” that management misleads about competition; on the whole the disclosures track performance in a way that looks like real firm-level competition. — [Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2012.00472.x); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1908338); [journal PDF extract](https://scispace.com/pdf/market-competition-earnings-management-and-persistence-in-5c97pxtlme.pdf)
- Hoberg, Phillips, and Prabhala, “Product Market Threats, Payouts, and Financial Flexibility,” Journal of Finance 69(1) (February 2014). Fluidity is the change in a firm’s product space caused by rivals’ moves, from 10-K product descriptions. Outcome is financial policy: lower dividends and repurchases, lower amounts, fewer initiations, more omissions, and higher cash, especially when external finance is tight. The circulated paper’s 10-K sample is fiscal years ending 1997–2008. No stock-return premium is stated in the retrieved abstract or introduction. — [JF PDF](https://cafral.org.in/sfControl/content/Speech/47201610034PM-Hoberg_Phillips_Prabhala_Paper.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1787315)
- Hoberg and Phillips, “Real and Financial Industry Booms and Busts,” Journal of Finance 65(1) (February 2010). In competitive industries, high industry valuation, investment, and financing are followed by sharply lower operating cash flows and abnormal stock returns; analyst forecasts are positively biased and returns comove more. In concentrated industries these relations are weak. Interpretation in the paper: participants in competitive industries do not fully internalize the negative externality of industry competition. Magnitudes: a one-standard-deviation increase in relative industry valuation is associated with a 3% decline in operating cash flows; a one-standard-deviation increase in industry financing with a 5.5% decline in operating cash flows. In competitive growth industries, annual abnormal stock returns for the highest quintile of relative industry valuation are over 3 percentage points lower than the lowest quintile at industry level; firm-weighted, the abnormal-return difference exceeds 10 percentage points. — [JF PDF](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/gordonph/intellcont/JF%202010-1.pdf); [AFA issue page](https://afajof.org/issue/volume-65-issue-1/)
- Hoberg and Phillips, “The Stock Market, Product Uniqueness, and Comovement of Peer Firms” (working paper, 11 October 2012). Firms with more unique product portfolios have higher valuations than matched peers. “These higher valuations also are long-lasting, as firms with more unique products do not experience ex-post abnormal return reversals.” Text-based peers explain own-stock returns better than traditional industry peers. Valuation level is priced; it is not followed by abnormal-return reversal. — [USC PDF](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/gordonph/intellcont/hoberg_phillips_The_stock_market_and_product_uniqueness-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- “Moat” language is close to profitability in disguise for the Li–Lundholm–Minnis object: fewer competition words line up with slower decay of RNOA and slower diminishing returns on new investment. A return regression that does not control for current profitability, investment, and the persistence of profitability will treat a moat score as alpha when it is an earnings-quality characteristic. The paper itself does not show a residual return.
- Fluidity is a threat to cash-flow stability that shows up in payout and cash, i.e. a risk/financial-policy outcome. That could in principle be a premium, but the JF 2014 paper retrieved here does not estimate one.
- The clean excess-return result is conditional and looks like mispricing: competitive industries after a valuation and financing boom. A static long–short on “high competition words” is not what Hoberg and Phillips (2010) identify. The signal needs the interaction of competition and stretched industry valuation.
- Uniqueness/moat raises the price and then does not mean-revert in returns. Using it as a short (expensive quality) or a long (underreaction to moat) is not supported by that working paper.

### Gaps
- No retrieved paper sorts on fluidity, TNIC HHI, or Li–Lundholm–Minnis competition and reports FF5+momentum alphas.
- Hou and Robinson (2006) industry-concentration return premium, and later census-data critiques, were not re-opened. Do not import those magnitudes.
- Whether an LLM “economic moat” score adds anything to competition-word counts plus current ROE is untested in the sources found.
- Publication status of the 2012 uniqueness paper was not confirmed.

## 6. Innovation and AI-adoption language in 10-Ks

### Takeaway
Valuation effects and subsequent excess returns are different. ChatGPT-window repricing and resume-based AI investment show up in prices and growth. Keyword mentions in the 10-K do not move the three-day filing return. Where a post-ChatGPT return spread exists, it is in earnings-call exposure or in LLM risk scores that are largely between-sector, and it was not there as a stable premium before ChatGPT. Alignment of AI disclosure with actual AI employment is the 10-K result that claims long-horizon outperformance, without a retrieved basis-point figure.

### Cited Findings
- Disclosure diffusion, not returns. A 2026 MDPI paper: share of S&P 500 firms disclosing AI as an Item 1A risk rose from 17% in fiscal 2022 to 61% in fiscal 2024 and 77.6% in fiscal 2025. Generative-AI dictionary share was zero through 2021, 0.2% in 2022 (one firm-year), 3.9% in 2023, 26.5% in 2024, and 39.5% in 2025. The paper’s literature review, which is not its own return test, attributes three related facts to other work: Felten et al. (2021) occupation-level AI exposure; Babina et al. (2024) resume-based AI investment associated with higher sales, employment, and market valuations; Eisfeldt et al. (2023) a workforce generative-AI exposure whose Artificial-Minus-Human portfolio earned 5% in the two weeks after the ChatGPT release. Those three are valuation, growth, or a two-week event return, and none of them is an Item 1A keyword score. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/19/7/473)
- 10-K keywords and indices: Ante and Saggu, arXiv:2501.01763 (3 January 2025). NLP on annual 10-Ks of 3,395 NASDAQ firms, 2011–2023. Binary and frequency-weighted AI scores; four AI indices. ChatGPT-launch event study: higher AI engagement had significantly greater positive abnormal returns. Indices “perform on par with or surpass” 14 AI-themed ETFs and the Nasdaq Composite on risk-return, with higher average daily returns and risk-adjusted metrics “without increased volatility.” This is index performance and an event reaction, not a stated FF5 alpha on post-filing drift. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01763)
- Filing-window null for 10-K AI words: “Analyzing the market’s reaction to AI narratives in corporate filings” (ScienceDirect, September 2025, S105752192500465X). CAR from −3 to +3 days around the 10-K, FF3 benchmark, estimation window −350 to −100. Introductions of AI keywords, actionable language, speculative language, and irrelevant language have insignificant CARs (keyword coefficient 0.003, t ≈ 0.65 in the displayed table; other introduction coefficients similarly insignificant). The paper states: “We do not find any effect of AI keyword introductions on cumulative abnormal returns.” Continuous mentions are described as statistically significant for valuation in unreported tests but much smaller than first-time introductions. Fundamentals table: “Actionable Treated” five-year coefficient −0.282 (p = 0.037); shorter horizons in that table are insignificant. That profitability coefficient is a fundamentals result, not a return. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105752192500465X)
- Earnings calls, not 10-Ks: Basu et al., “Firm-level AI exposure: measurement, determinants and pricing implications” (Strathclyde PDF, 2026). Managers frame AI as opportunity, productivity, and innovation, assessed favorably, not as risk. Larger, cash-rich, higher-valued, younger, less-levered firms have greater exposure. A one-standard-deviation increase in the exposure measure is associated with approximately 1.37% higher annual returns, significant only after the ChatGPT launch. A US long–short of high-exposure versus no-exposure firms earns no abnormal return before the launch once large-cap growth tilts are accounted for, but earns a large abnormal return afterward. Full-sample FF5 alpha of the high-minus-none spread is 37 basis points per month, and “the alpha appears only in the post-ChatGPT period.” — [Strathprints PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/97605/1/Basu-etal-SSRN-2026-Firm-level-AI-exposure-measurement-determinants-and-pricing-implications.pdf)
- 10-K disclosure versus real AI labor: Barrios, Campbell, Johnson, and Liu (SSRN, 23 August 2024), “Signals or Smoke? The Determinants and Informativeness of Corporate Artificial Intelligence (AI) Disclosures.” AI disclosers are innovative, in AI-intensive industries, and under more investor scrutiny. AI disclosure is positively associated with subsequent operating efficiency and AI patents even after AI human-capital investment (fundamentals; disclosure as a signal of confidence, not only of headcount). Firms that align high AI disclosure with substantial AI employment get larger operating benefits than mismatched firms. “These aligned firms significantly outperform peers in long-term stock returns.” No basis-point figure in the abstract. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5133107)
- LLM scoring of Item 1A, student thesis, not a journal: Sjöberg Dahlén and Salamon, Stockholm School of Economics (2026), “Artificial Intelligence Risk: Evidence from LLM-Assessed 10-K Filings, 2020–2025.” Three LLMs score S&P 500 Item 1A on displacement, operational, regulatory, and cybersecurity risk. Post-2023 equal-weighted long–short monthly FF5 alphas of 0.59–0.77%, concentrated in value and low-profitability firms. 54–66% of the spread is between-sector. Firms with higher AI exposure earn significantly lower market-adjusted returns on days when Google search attention to AI regulation and safety is high. Authors’ reading: pricing is “primarily through sector composition rather than through firm-level disclosure intensity.” — [Uppsatser.se](https://www.uppsatser.se/uppsats/725e74bfc6/)

### Inferences
- Separate three objects: (i) valuation level and the November 2022 repricing (Eisfeldt two-week 5%; Ante–Saggu event returns; Babina valuations); (ii) the null three-day reaction to adding AI words in the 10-K; (iii) subsequent returns, which in Basu et al. are post-ChatGPT only and, in the thesis, mostly sector. A pre-2023 10-K AI dictionary is not supported as a risk premium.
- Keyword counts mix opportunity language (how managers actually talk, per Basu) with Item 1A risk language (the S&P 500 wave documented for 2022–2025). The ScienceDirect split into actionable versus speculative versus irrelevant already shows the market does not price the introduction. An LLM should score adoption-with-evidence versus boilerplate risk, and the Barrios result says the interaction with real AI employment is the part tied to long-run returns.
- The thesis is a warning for feature design: an LLM Item 1A AI-risk score can look like a 0.6–0.8% monthly FF5 alpha and still be mostly industry. Within-industry disclosure intensity is the claim that would belong on a shortlist, and it is the claim their decomposition weakens.
- Negative attention-day returns for high AI-risk exposure (thesis) resemble Florackis’s state dependence. That is a risk-factor signature, and it does not imply a positive unconditional alpha once sector is removed.

### Gaps
- Barrios et al. long-term return magnitude, horizon, and factor model are not in the abstract.
- Ante and Saggu do not state a factor-adjusted post-filing alpha.
- No retrieved study shows 10-K AI language predicts returns after controlling for Babina-style AI employment, book-to-market, and profitability, except the thesis’s sector decomposition and Barrios’s alignment design (magnitude missing).
- Journal status of Basu et al. (2026) was not confirmed.

## 7. Human-capital, labor, union, and key-person risk disclosures

### Takeaway
Retrieved return evidence says these risks show up in volatility and in the stock-price drop when a key person leaves, not in a realized equity premium. A labor-risk paper finds no significant realized premium and a size-dependent option-implied premium. Mandatory human-capital disclosure has investor-perception evidence, not a return spread.

### Cited Findings
- “Key Human Capital,” JFQA (Cambridge core S0022109016000880). Construct: disclosure of key-man life insurance as the measure of concentrated, hard-to-replace employees. Exposed firms are younger, smaller, growth firms with abnormally high volatility. Total and idiosyncratic volatility about 5%–20% higher than unexposed firms. Following key-employee departure announcements, the most exposed firms lose 8% of value (abstract); the body text extracted says approximately 4% in the four days after departure. Key employees are much more educated (four times more likely to hold PhDs than top managers); these firms are more innovative. Portfolio sorts on the key-human-capital indicator: “no evidence of a premium.” The paper notes these are small growth stocks, which have anomalously low returns in Fama–French (1993), so the characteristic is entangled with size and value. — [JFQA PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4FAE1CB21BE77F7EDA608674A93C163C/S0022109016000880a.pdf/div-class-title-key-human-capital-div.pdf)
- Labor risk and realized returns: working paper at the FGV URL below (filename indicates Politi and Skiadopoulos; title on the pages is a corporate labor-misconduct / labor-risk study; confirm authors before citing names). Realized stock returns show no significant labor-risk premium over March 2007–January 2024. Option-implied expected returns (Chabi-Yo et al. 2023 proxy) show a size pattern: positive premium for large firms, negative for small firms; equal-weighted portfolios negative, value-weighted positive. The measure predicts enforcement outcomes in both size groups and is positively associated with future stock-return volatility. The authors emphasize that realized returns are noisy proxies for expected returns, citing Pástor et al. (2022) and Sautner et al. (2023). — [FGV PDF](https://eaesp.fgv.br/sites/default/files/uploads/politisskiadopoulos-corporate-labor-misconduct-risk.pdf)
- Human-capital narrative experiment, not a return test: Jennings, LaViers, Rouen, and Sandvik (SSRN, 19 June 2025). Professional investors say current human-capital disclosures are not sufficient, and they use the information when they can get it. Randomly providing turnover, contract-worker use, employee expenditures, and workplace-diversity metrics increases disagreement in risk assessments for every metric except employee expenditures. Perception and disagreement, not expected-return or alpha. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5065997)
- Disclosure practice only: Gibson Dunn’s December 2024 survey of S&P 100 human-capital disclosures over four years after the SEC rule. Example facts: 58% discuss workforce composition; 38% state that some of the workforce is unionized or under a similar agreement. No return test. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/four-years-of-evolving-form-10-k-human-capital-disclosures/)

### Inferences
- Key-person and labor-risk language are volatility and left-tail event features. The realized-return tests that exist fail to find a premium (key human capital; labor risk over 2007–2024). Option-implied expected returns can still show a premium that realized-return sorts miss, and that premium flips sign with size, so a long–short built on labor-risk words is not a stable positive alpha.
- Union and human-capital boilerplate expanded after the SEC’s human-capital amendment. A keyword count of “employees” or “union” will track the disclosure regime. The JFQA design is closer to what an LLM should do: is human capital concentrated in named, hard-to-replace people, as evidenced by key-man insurance or an explicit dependency, not whether Item 1 mentions the workforce.
- Because key-human-capital firms are small growth stocks, any raw return spread is the size/value anomaly until those factors are controlled. The paper already says the portfolio sort shows no premium.

### Gaps
- No retrieved excess-return paper on post-2020 human-capital Item 1 narrative, union-risk language, or employee-turnover disclosure.
- The 4% versus 8% departure-loss figures are both in the JFQA PDF extract (abstract versus a later paragraph). Reconcile in the published table before quoting one number.
- Author names and whether the labor-risk measure is 10-K text, violations data, or both were not fully extracted.

## 9. Promise-tracking: this period’s MD&A or outlook versus the firm’s own prior statements

### Takeaway
The serious academic evidence is Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen’s Lazy Prices (Journal of Finance 2020): firms that change 10-K/10-Q language subsequently underperform firms that do not, with no filing-day reaction and with the drift showing up over the following quarters as earnings, news, and bankruptcies arrive. That is inattention / delayed fundamentals, not a risk premium. A study that tracks disappeared targets, walked-back guidance, or changed KPIs as such, and reports a stock-return magnitude, was not found. Simple similarity already carries the large alpha; an LLM walk-back detector is an unproven refinement.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen, “Lazy Prices,” Journal of Finance 75(3), 1371–1415 (June 2020); NBER w25084. Sample: regular quarterly and annual filings, 1995–2014. A portfolio that shorts “changers” and buys “non-changers” earns up to 188 basis points per month in alpha (over 22% per year). Changes predict future earnings, profitability, news announcements, and firm-level bankruptcies. “Unlike typical underreaction patterns,” there is no announcement effect; returns accrue when the information is later revealed. Investors are described as inattentive to these changes. SSRN abstract highlights changes in language about the executive team (CEO and CFO), litigation, and the risk-factor section. HBS abstract says the reporting changes are concentrated in the MD&A, and that executive-team and litigation language are especially informative. Both sections are in the authors’ own abstracts; treat MD&A, risk factors, executives, and litigation as the hot spots they flag, and check the published tables before ranking them. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078); [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [JSTOR abstract](https://www.jstor.org/stable/45286294)
- Four similarity measures give similar answers, according to a 2026 practitioner write-up of the paper (cosine on term frequencies, Jaccard, minimum edit distance, word-level diff). That write-up also says value-weighted abnormal returns are about 30 to 58 basis points per month (about 7% per year) and that risk-factor changes reach 188 basis points per month with t = 2.76, with drift over 6 to 18 months and no reversal. The 188 bp figure matches the journal abstract; the 30–58 bp value-weighted band, the t-statistic, and the 6–18 month window are from the practitioner article, not from a table opened here. Use them as secondary. — [Quantt, 27 April 2026](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)
- Interpretation: mispricing from inattention. The return is delayed revelation of operating news the filing change already contained. Firms that do not change earn positive abnormal returns in the short–long as specified (long non-changers, short changers). It is not sold as compensation for risk. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)
- Explicit promise-tracking was not found. A 2026 practitioner note on 10-Q MD&A says SEC staff comment letters ask issuers to quantify each driver of a change and to align MD&A with specifics already said on the earnings call (example: price inflation discussed on the call but missing from MD&A). Boilerplate PSLRA cautionary language is described as weak if it is not company-specific. This is a disclosure-process source, with no return test. — [FinRep, 26 October 2026](https://www.finrep.ai/blog/10-q-md-a-requirements-2026-practitioner-walkthrough)
- Adjacent inconsistency result, different asset class: Liu, Madsen, and Zhou (SSRN, revised 2024) find that 40% of hedge-fund regulatory narratives omit operational and investment risks that appear in other public sources. Those inconsistent funds have predictably lower subsequent fund performance, but flows do not differ. Not a stock-level 10-K result. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3803729)
- A 2025 Columbia Blue Sky Blog piece on algorithm-written MD&A defines practitioner scores for machine-oriented prose and for rhetorical red flags (upbeat tone with poor financials, missing KPIs, vague aspirations). It reports rising AI-oriented MD&A scores from 2021 to 2024 and higher manipulation-style scores at firms with weak fundamentals. No stock-return test. — [CLS Blue Sky Blog, 16 July 2025](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2025/07/16/the-risks-of-algorithm-written-mdas/)

### Inferences
- For excess-return prediction, prior-document comparison is the strongest result in this whole memo, and it does not require an LLM. The economic content is “something material changed and the market did not price it at the filing.” Risk-factor, MD&A, litigation, and executive-team edits are the places the authors say to look.
- The sign is negative for changers. A feature that flags any edit will be dominated by firms with bad forthcoming news. It is not a positive “transparency” premium.
- Because there is no day-0 effect, a 3-day filing CAR will miss it. The holding period in the secondary write-up is 6–18 months, and the journal abstract’s portfolio alpha is a monthly strategy on the post-filing sample.
- Walk-backs, disappeared targets, and changed KPIs are a plausible refinement of Lazy Prices, not a documented improvement. Simple overlap measures already produce the 188 bp upper figure. An LLM adds value only if it separates cosmetic rewrites from dropped numbers. That horse-race was not found.
- The signal also predicts earnings and bankruptcies, so part of the alpha is delayed fundamental news. A test that includes subsequent earnings surprises will shrink it. That does not make it a risk premium; it makes the horizon the time until the news is released in a form investors do read.

### Gaps
- No academic magnitude for literal target walk-backs, guidance withdrawals inside MD&A, or KPI replacements.
- The split of the 188 bp across equal-weighted versus value-weighted, and across MD&A versus Item 1A, should be taken from JF Table results. This session has the abstract’s “up to 188 bp” and a secondary 30–58 bp value-weighted band.
- Post-2014 replication was not retrieved. The sample in the paper ends in 2014, before the LLM-disclosure era the Blue Sky note describes.

## 10. Specificity, named-entity density, and contradiction across Risk Factors, MD&A, and footnotes

### Takeaway
Specific risk-factor language is priced at the filing: Hope, Hu, and Lu (Review of Accounting Studies 2016) find a larger absolute three-day reaction when Item 1A is more specific, and better analyst risk assessment. That is price discovery, not a subsequent excess-return premium. Named-entity density is their specificity idea. A stock-return result for contradictions among Risk Factors, MD&A, and footnotes was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Hope, Hu, and Lu, “The benefits of specific risk-factor disclosures,” Review of Accounting Studies 21, 1005–1045 (2016). Construct: an algorithm scoring specificity of qualitative Item 1A risk-factor disclosure. Firms with high proprietary costs are less specific. Controlling for determinants of specificity and for other drivers of the filing reaction, specificity is positively and significantly associated with the absolute three-day cumulative abnormal return around the 10-K filing date. Analysts assess fundamental risk better when disclosure is more specific. Investors respond more to quantitative than to qualitative risk disclosure (stated in the PDF extract). Interpretation in the paper: specific risk factors are not “just boilerplate”; they help users. Not a signed post-filing drift test. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2457045); [IDEAS / RAST](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v21y2016i4d10.1007_s11142-016-9371-1.html); [PDF extract](https://www.sci-hub.st/storage/2024/6033/aa388b32abb2d20ef6d00c376c73d949/hope2016.pdf)
- Boilerplate is the opposing pole, discussed in the cybersecurity-disclosure literature as recycled generic text that reduces informativeness, with citations to Nelson and Pritchard (2016), Gao, Merkley, and Pacelli-type results are not to be inferred; the retrieved citation list in the ransomware paper is Li et al. (2018), Nelson and Pritchard (2016), and Gao et al. (2020). Those citations were not opened, and no return premium for “low boilerplate” was retrieved. — [Computers & Security article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167404825003153)
- Cross-section contradiction in equities: not found. The hedge-fund inconsistency paper (theme 9) is the closest performance result, and it is about fund returns. A 2026 legal note on Indian draft prospectuses describes the same fact (customer concentration) appearing as different percentages in the business section, risk factors, and MD&A; it is a diligence standard, not a return study. — [SSRN hedge funds](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3803729); [Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/corporate-commercial-law/one-fact-many-chapters-the-horizontal-diligence-problem-in-a-drhp)
- Lazy Prices is the return result for “the risk-factor section changed,” which is a within-firm time-series contradiction with last year’s Item 1A, not a within-document contradiction with the footnotes. Sign and horizon are in theme 9. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)

### Inferences
- Specificity is a moderator of whether other theme scores are information or boilerplate. Hope et al. imply that a generic “we face cybersecurity / climate / AI risk” sentence should get a smaller weight than a sentence with named actors, quantities, and scenarios. They show this in the filing-window absolute return, i.e. the market does react when the text is specific. That reaction at day 0 is a reason to expect less subsequent drift, not more, once specificity is public and readable.
- An LLM materiality judgment is the modern version of their specificity score: incident versus hypothetical, quantified versus generic, company-specific versus industry essay. The return object that specificity itself has been shown to predict is |CAR| at the 10-K, not alpha over the next year.
- Within-document contradiction (Item 1A versus MD&A versus notes) is a plausible negative signal by analogy to hedge-fund inconsistencies and to Lazy Prices’ “change is bad news,” but analogy is not evidence. Leave it as a design hypothesis.

### Gaps
- No retrieved signed post-filing return spread on specificity or named-entity density.
- No retrieved equity-return test of contradictions among Item 1A, MD&A, and footnotes.
- Hope et al.’s numerical CAR coefficient per unit of specificity was not in the extracts, only the sign and significance.

## 11. Which results are incremental to market, size, value, momentum, profitability, and investment?

### Takeaway
Only a few results are both about future stock returns and explicitly abnormal relative to standard factors, and they are not the same object. Lazy Prices is the clear mispricing alpha. Florackis et al. is a large spread the authors call a premium, with factor details not extracted here. Physical climate risk (working paper) and post-ChatGPT AI exposure claim factor-adjusted spreads that are small, new, or mostly sector. The carbon premium and the greenium should not be treated as incremental text alphas. Competition, human capital, specificity, and customer-concentration levels do not have a clean positive factor-adjusted alpha in the sources retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Incremental mispricing, filing text: Lazy Prices alpha up to 188 bp per month. The abstract calls it alpha and stresses the absence of an announcement effect. It also predicts subsequent earnings, so incrementality to a profitability factor measured today is not the same as incrementality to future earnings news. Sample 1995–2014. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)

- Political: QJE paper is volatility and investment, so it is not incremental alpha. Gorbatikov et al. 0.5–1.0% per year per standard deviation is small; factor list not in the abstract. ### Inferences
- Rank for a later excess-return shortlist, without making the shortlist: (1) year-over-year filing change, especially MD&A / Item 1A / litigation / executives, as mispricing; (2) Item 1A cybersecurity exposure as a candidate premium, pending a check that FF5+momentum and post-2018 returns still leave a spread; (3) physical-versus-transition climate language only if the Tran-style premium survives industry and emissions controls; (4) political risk only as a small premium and only if rebuilt on filings and only for firms that do not lobby it away. Do not rank carbon level, green-minus-brown realized returns, AI keyword counts, competition word counts, or human-capital word counts as incremental alpha on this evidence.
- “Incremental to factors” fails in three recurring ways in this literature: the variable is a size or revenue proxy (absolute emissions; key-person small growth; AI large-cap growth), the variable is industry (AI-risk thesis; IT-heavy cybersecurity, which at least loses money in a cyber state), or the variable is current profitability persistence (competition / moat).

### Gaps
- A common FF5+momentum horse-race across these text scores on one sample was not found.
- Florackis factor list and Cohen–Frazzini alphas remain unread in the original tables.

## 12. Where an LLM improves on keyword counts by judging materiality

### Takeaway
LLMs, or earlier BERT-style classifiers, matter when the same topic word covers opposite economics: physical versus transition climate, opportunity versus risk, a named incident versus a generic risk factor, and disclosure that matches real investment versus disclosure alone. They have not been shown to beat simple document similarity for the Lazy Prices alpha. The strongest direct statement that LLM sentiment beats dictionaries on SEC text is a citation inside a 2026 outlook paper, and the best-known ChatGPT return result is about news headlines, not 10-K themes.

### Cited Findings
- Headline LLMs, not filings: Lopez-Lira and Tang, “Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements?,” Journal of Financial Economics, forthcoming as of the SSRN revision dated 6 May 2026. ChatGPT scores news headlines with no specialized financial training. GPT-4 matches initial reactions at roughly a 90% portfolio-day hit rate for the non-tradable initial move, and the scores predict subsequent drift, especially for small stocks and negative news. Forecasting ability rises with model size. Strategy returns decline as LLM adoption rises. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4412788)
- Broad public text, including a filing comparison by citation: Lehner and Lopez-Lira, “ChatGPT as a Time Capsule” (SSRN / arXiv, 23 April 2026). Frozen LLM checkpoints with a fixed knowledge cutoff score about 7,000 US equities. The outlook score predicts analyst revisions, target-price changes, and one-month returns after market-implied valuation and standard factors. Pooled panel t = 6.02 with model fixed effects. In the primary GPT-4.1 specification (June 2024 cutoff), the one-month Fama–MacBeth-style result has t = 4.25 after size, quality, value, momentum, and four cheapness metrics. Predictability is stronger for high-analyst-coverage firms in the panel, which the authors read as a cost of aggregating dispersed text, not as inattention. The paper cites Lehner (2024) for the narrower claim that LLM sentiment of SEC filings correlates more strongly with price movements than dictionary or traditional machine-learning sentiment. The 2024 paper itself was not opened. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6632823); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21433v1)
- Climate materiality already changes the sign of the return result. Kölbel et al. (2022), as used by Tran et al., classify Item 1A sentences into physical versus transitional risk; only physical risk has the 1.5–2.9% premium. Sautner et al. separately measure opportunity, physical, and regulatory attention, and the pricing paper says the priced component is uncertainty about upside opportunities, not a single “climate word” count. Berkman et al. weight excerpts by relevance rather than raw keyword hits and beat emissions intensity and commercial ESG scores for valuation. — [Tran PDF](https://scholar.googleusercontent.com/scholar?q=cache:wjQ-azAhDYsJ:scholar.google.com/&hl=th&as_sdt=0,5); [Sautner pricing SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3792366); [Berkman abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361368224000072)
- AI materiality: 10-K keyword introductions have insignificant filing CARs, while managers’ call language is mostly opportunity (Basu et al.) and Item 1A AI-risk disclosure has become majority practice in the S&P 500 (17% to 77.6%). Barrios et al. find long-term outperformance for disclosure that lines up with AI employment, not for disclosure in isolation. The Stockholm thesis finds an LLM Item 1A risk premium that is mostly sector. A count of “artificial intelligence” pools these. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105752192500465X); [Basu PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/97605/1/Basu-etal-SSRN-2026-Firm-level-AI-exposure-measurement-determinants-and-pricing-implications.pdf); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/19/7/473); [Barrios SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5133107); [thesis](https://www.uppsatser.se/uppsats/725e74bfc6/)
- Cybersecurity: Florackis et al. already go beyond a single keyword by fitting language that distinguishes hacked firms. The ransomware-disclosure paper still flags boilerplate in Item 1A and treats phrase-frequency measures, including Florackis-style measures, as incomplete. No retrieved paper shows an LLM incident-materiality score earns a different premium from the RFS dictionary score. — [RFS via EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.); [Computers & Security](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167404825003153)
- Specificity without an LLM already separates boilerplate from named, quantitative risk factors and predicts |CAR| at the 10-K (Hope, Hu, and Lu). Customer type (government versus corporate) flips the cost-of-equity sign (JAE 2016). Competition-word counts already predict profitability persistence, and Li et al. find only limited evidence of strategic misreporting. — [RAST](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v21y2016i4d10.1007_s11142-016-9371-1.html); [JAE](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410115000336); [JAR extract](https://scispace.com/pdf/market-competition-earnings-management-and-persistence-in-5c97pxtlme.pdf)
- Lazy Prices: cosine, Jaccard, and edit distance agree. The large alpha does not depend on semantic materiality. An LLM that only rediscovers “the document changed” is not an improvement. An LLM that isolates dropped targets would be a different feature, and it is untested. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078); [Quantt secondary](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)

### Inferences
- Use an LLM where polarity is inside the topic: climate physical versus opportunity versus regulation; AI adoption versus AI risk versus AI boilerplate; cyber incident with quantified impact versus “we may experience cyberattacks”; customer dependency that is a single commercial buyer versus a government book; political risk the firm is not lobbying away. Those splits change the predicted sign in the papers above.
- Do not use an LLM merely to recount words for competition, document similarity, or generic human-capital discussion. Counts or overlap already map to the outcomes those papers actually measure, and the outcomes are often fundamentals or filing-day |CAR|, not alpha.
- Sector leakage is the practical failure mode of LLM theme scores (AI thesis; IT-heavy cyber). Materiality prompts should be within-industry and should require a named entity, a number, or a change from the prior filing before a point is awarded.
- Lopez-Lira and Tang’s decay of strategy returns as LLMs are adopted is a warning that headline-drift signals shrink once the tool is public. Filing-change inattention (Lazy Prices) had no day-0 effect even with a simple measure; that is a different, slower bottleneck. Lehner and Lopez-Lira’s 2026 result says the bottleneck can be aggregation cost even among well-covered firms, so an LLM that reads the whole filing can still predict one-month returns after valuation and factors. That result is not a license to build ten theme dictionaries.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head of LLM versus Florackis dictionary, versus Sautner keyword discovery, or versus Lazy Prices similarity, on the same return horizon.
- Lehner (2024) on SEC-filing sentiment was not opened; only the citing sentence was retrieved.
- Contradiction detection across Item 1A, MD&A, and footnotes has no return horse-race against keyword methods.

## 13. Holding period and event time

### Takeaway
Match the clock to the mechanism. Premiums that live in an annual Item 1A score are predictable for up to about 12 months after the information exists and must be lagged to the filing; using same-year text or emissions is look-ahead. Inattention to filing edits does not show up as a filing-day return and, on the secondary account of Lazy Prices, accrues over 6–18 months. AI keyword reactions at the 10-K are null; the AI return that exists is tied to the ChatGPT date and to call text. Customer-link predictability, if used, is timed off the customer’s news, not the supplier’s 10-K.

### Cited Findings
- Cybersecurity score predicts returns in Fama–MacBeth out to 12 months. The text source is the 10-K Item 1A, so the information date is the filing, and the economic horizon in the paper is inside a year. High-score firms underperform in high-concern periods, so a one-year market-neutral hold still has state risk. — [NBER PDF text mirror](http://dx.doi.org/10.3386/w28196)
- Lazy Prices: no announcement effect; the journal abstract says returns accrue later through news, events, or earnings. Secondary write-up: drift over 6 to 18 months, no reversal. Sample includes both quarterly and annual filings, 1995–2014. Formation is on the change once the filing exists, not on a three-day window. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078); [Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)
- Specificity is a three-day absolute CAR around the 10-K filing date. That is an event window, not a holding period for alpha. — [Hope, Hu, and Lu PDF extract](https://www.sci-hub.st/storage/2024/6033/aa388b32abb2d20ef6d00c376c73d949/hope2016.pdf)
- AI keywords: CAR −3 to +3 around the 10-K is insignificant. The Eisfeldt et al. result cited in the MDPI review is a two-week return after the ChatGPT release, which is a technology-news date, not a filing date. Basu et al.’s FF5 alpha exists only after that launch and is measured from call exposure, i.e. the earnings-call timestamp. Ante and Saggu’s significant AI-engagement result is also the ChatGPT event study. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105752192500465X); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/19/7/473); [Basu PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/97605/1/Basu-etal-SSRN-2026-Firm-level-AI-exposure-measurement-determinants-and-pricing-implications.pdf); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01763)
- Reshoring results are announcement reactions, not a multi-month hold of 10-K language. — [Management Science record](http://hdl.handle.net/10397/109378)
- Look-ahead is a first-order design error in this literature. The March 2026 column on Markwat, Hanauer, and Swinkels: carbon emissions for a calendar year were aligned with returns during that year, including months before the emissions occurred. Lagging to the investor’s information set removed the premium. The same rule applies to 10-K text: do not use a fiscal-year 2024 Item 1A, which is filed in 2025, to explain returns in 2024. — [Investment Officer](https://cms.investmentofficer.com/en/columns/gertjan-verdickt/carbon-premium-never-existed)
- Earnings calls are a different clock from the 10-K. Hassan political risk, Sautner climate exposure, and Basu AI exposure update quarterly at the call. The 10-Q/10-K often restates outlook the call already gave. SEC staff comments cited by a 2026 practitioner note specifically compare MD&A with the earnings call, which is evidence that the call is the earlier narrative. Incremental filing text is what was not in the call or the earnings release. — [Hassan data page](https://www.policyuncertainty.com/firm_pr.html); [FinRep](https://www.finrep.ai/blog/10-q-md-a-requirements-2026-practitioner-walkthrough)
- Lehner and Lopez-Lira (April 2026): one-month cross-sectional returns, with predictability that “broadly increases with the return horizon” after a non-monotonic dip. That is a research finding about aggregated public text scored by a frozen LLM, not a recommendation to hold a 10-K theme for a fixed number of months. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21433v1)
- Customer links: the UH paper confirms the link file is the filing-based major-customer disclosure. The event that would move the supplier is the customer’s later results. Timing the supplier trade on the supplier’s own 10-K acceptance misses that mechanism. Magnitude of the original Cohen and Frazzini delay was not re-retrieved. — [UH PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/yerramilli/CustomerConcentration.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a candidate premium (cyber Item 1A, physical climate Item 1A, political exposure): event time is EDGAR acceptance of the 10-K, or the earnings-call timestamp if the measure is a call measure. Skip the filing month if the test is about expected returns rather than the announcement reaction. Hold and refresh out to 12 months, because that is the horizon Florackis et al. document, and re-form when the next 10-K replaces Item 1A. Evaluate as a characteristic portfolio against FF5+momentum, and check the bad state (cyber-concern spikes, climate-news spikes, AI-regulation attention days). A premium that does not lose money in its bad state is probably delayed news, not risk.
- For filing-change / promise-tracking: event time is acceptance of the 10-K or 10-Q. Do not require a day-0 CAR; Lazy Prices says there is none. Hold several quarters to about a year and a half, and expect the alpha to overlap subsequent earnings announcements. Compare each section with the same section last year, not with a boilerplate peer average.
- For anything that merely repeats the earnings release, the economically correct clock is the earnings-release (or call) timestamp. The 10-K acceptance date is the right clock only for text that first appears there, chiefly a rewritten Item 1A, new quantified risks, and segment detail that was not in the release.
- Annual scores (Item 1A, Item 1C, human-capital discussions) should not be treated as quarterly signals. Quarterly MD&A and calls can update outlook, political risk, and AI talk. Mixing an annual boilerplate score into a monthly rebalance overstates independent observations.
- Never align a document with returns that predate its acceptance. The carbon-premium debate is the cautionary case.

### Gaps
- A direct comparison of 10-K acceptance-date portfolios versus earnings-announcement-date portfolios for the same text themes was not found, except the qualitative fact that Lazy Prices has no filing announcement effect and that AI keyword CARs at the 10-K are zero.
- Cohen and Frazzini’s exact monthly lag was not re-read.
- Optimal holding period after Item 1C (post-December 2023) is unknown because no return paper was found.
