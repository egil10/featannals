# Near-miss filing features: published stock-return magnitudes

Scope is US equities and post-disclosure excess or abnormal returns, not future earnings alone. Each feature is judged promote (return shortlist) / keep as fundamentals-only / kill. A number is included only when a paper, author slides of that paper, or a clearly labeled secondary summary states it. Where the journal text could not be opened, that is stated.

## 1. Customer–supplier return delay (Cohen and Frazzini 2008)

### Takeaway
Promote. The customer–supplier link is a filing disclosure, and the paper itself states a one-month customer-momentum long–short of about 1.5% per month (about 18% a year) with little standard-factor exposure. A later bias-correction study says those alphas are substantially overstated.

### Cited Findings
- Lauren Cohen and Andrea Frazzini, “Economic Links and Predictable Returns,” *Journal of Finance* 63(4), 1977–2011 (August 2008). Links are principal customers that firms must disclose under SFAS 131 in the financial statements (customers that are a large share of sales). The signal is the customer’s past stock return, not a prose topic. Sign is positive: good customer news predicts higher subsequent supplier returns (“customer momentum”). — [HBS abstract](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=31700); [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:63:y:2008:i:4:p:1977-2011); [paper PDF excerpt](https://scispace.com/pdf/economic-links-and-predictable-returns-1acnxz4kzc.pdf)
- Implementation lag is one month. Buying suppliers whose customers were in the highest return quintile in the previous month and shorting suppliers whose customers were in the lowest quintile “yields abnormal returns of 1.55% per month, or an annualized return of 18.6% per year.” A long–short of the top versus bottom 20% “delivers Fama and French (1993) abnormal returns of 1.45% per month (t-statistic = 3.61), approximately … 18.4% per year.” The published abstract rounds this to “monthly alphas of over 150 basis points, or over 18 percent per year.” The PDF says the strategy has little or no exposure to the standard traded risk factors. — [paper PDF excerpt](https://scispace.com/pdf/economic-links-and-predictable-returns-1acnxz4kzc.pdf); [HBS abstract](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=31700)
- A later replication of their Table III reports Fama–French three-factor monthly alphas of 155 basis points value-weighted and 116 basis points equal-weighted for customer returns in month t predicting supplier returns in month t+1. That write-up says the original paper argued the delay lasts up to one year, but after a bias correction the alphas are “overstated by more than 70%,” the value-weighted corrected alpha is statistically insignificant, and information diffusion is complete within one month. — [University of Washington dissertation excerpt](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/bitstreams/db3fb18e-5271-4bb9-8272-7c89f9c37c9f/download)

### Inferences
- Verdict: promote into the return shortlist. The feature an LLM can build is the disclosed customer name (and sales share) in the 10-K; the return input is the customer’s market return, which is not inside the filing. Horizon for the stated strategy is one month, with the paper claiming a longer delay that a later correction disputes.
- Factor controls in the number above are Fama–French three-factor alphas, not a bare raw return.

### Gaps
- The Journal of Finance PDF was not opened page-by-page, so the exact table cell (equal- versus value-weighted, quintile versus vigintile) is taken from a PDF excerpt and a replication, which disagree slightly (1.45% versus 1.55%). The “up to twelve months” sentence was not quoted from the JF text itself; it is how the dissertation describes the original.

## 2. Deferred revenue changes and subsequent returns

### Takeaway
Promote, with a source caveat. The conference draft of the main US paper states a hedge on the change in current deferred revenue of nearly 9% abnormal return over the next year after Fama–French factors, accruals, and conservatism. The published abstract confirms mispricing but does not restate the 9%.

### Cited Findings
- Rachna Prakash and Nishi Sinha, “Deferred Revenues and the Matching of Revenues and Expenses,” *Contemporary Accounting Research* 30(2), 517–548 (2013). Measured object is the change in the current deferred-revenue liability scaled by average assets (ΔDRC), from the balance sheet, not from MD&A prose. Sign is positive: net deferrals predict higher future margins and higher future abnormal returns, because expenses are recognized while revenue is deferred, so current margins mislead. — [SSRN abstract of the published paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1316286); [CAR record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/coacre/v30y2013i2p517-548.html)
- The 2009 FEA conference draft of that paper states: a hedge on ΔDRC, “after controlling for Fama-French risk factors, operating accruals and accounting conservatism, earns abnormal returns of nearly 9% over the next year,” in both regressions and portfolios. Horizon is one-year-ahead buy-and-hold size-adjusted returns over twelve months (their LEADSAR). They also report that a 1% change in ΔDRC maps to a nearly 20% change in year-ahead profit margins. Robustness: not only small firms or outliers. — [conference PDF](http://pbfea2005.rutgers.edu/20thFEA/AccountingPapers/Session3/Prakash%20and%20Sinha.pdf)
- The published SSRN abstract says revenue deferrals “result in significant errors in analyst forecasts of both sales and margins and in mispriced securities,” but it does not repeat “nearly 9%.” — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1316286)
- Later non-US papers treat the US result as mispricing of deferred-revenue changes (Prakash and Sinha 2013; Zhong, Wang, and Zhang on China) but do not restate a US portfolio percent. An Indian study (Wasan, Mulchandani, and Mulchandani, *Journal of International Accounting, Auditing and Taxation* 2022) forms a long–short of the top versus bottom quartile of deferred-revenue changes and says it earns positive abnormal returns, without a percent in the abstract. — [India abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jiaata:v:46:y:2022:i:c:s1061951821000665); [China discussion inside the India paper PDF](https://parsproje.com/articles/hesabdari/h794.pdf)

### Inferences
- Verdict: promote into the return shortlist. The number to cite is “nearly 9%” one-year abnormal return from the authors’ conference draft of the CAR paper, not from the published abstract. Filing source is the balance-sheet liability (Compustat-extractable and 10-K-extractable). It is a number, not prose.

### Gaps
- The published CAR PDF was not opened, so it is unverified whether the typeset article still says “nearly 9%” or only “mispriced securities.” No post-ASC 606 US replication of the 9% hedge was found.

## 3. Remaining performance obligations / cRPO after ASC 606

### Takeaway
Keep as fundamentals-only. A 2026 paper shows RPO and order backlog each predict future revenue, but no stock-return or abnormal-return magnitude was found for RPO or cRPO.

### Cited Findings
- Sara Toynbee, “Navigating Related SEC and FASB Disclosure Requirements: Evidence from Remaining Performance Obligations and Order Backlog,” *Journal of Financial Reporting* (accepted 11 August 2026, posted 17 September 2026). RPO is the ASC 606 disclosure of the transaction price allocated to unsatisfied performance obligations (10-K/10-Q revenue note); order backlog is the older SEC disclosure. After ASC 606, fewer than a quarter of firms (21.6%) disclose both, and among those the two numbers are equal only 32.2% of the time. Changes in both are positively associated with future revenue and with analysts’ sales-forecast revisions; each has incremental explanatory power for future sales. About 45% of firms elect a practical expedient that lets them omit eligible RPO. No return, alpha, or revenue-surprise trading result is stated. — [AAA abstract](https://publications.aaahq.org/jfr/article/doi/10.2308/JFR-2024-009/28077/Navigating-Related-SEC-and-FASB-Disclosure); [article PDF opening](https://publications.aaahq.org/jfr/article-pdf/doi/10.2308/JFR-2024-009/206170/jfr-2024-009.pdf)
- Practitioner definitions confirm cRPO is the portion of RPO expected to be recognized within twelve months and is not itself a separate GAAP line, but no academic return is attached. — [glossary, not a return study](https://orm-tech.com/glossary/remaining-performance-obligation/)

### Inferences
- Verdict: keep as fundamentals-only. Useful for a revenue or sales-surprise model. Do not promote to a return shortlist on current evidence. The practical expedient means the disclosed number is a selected subset.

### Gaps
- Return magnitude: not found. Queries used: “remaining performance obligations RPO stock returns ASC 606 alpha”; “remaining performance obligations future revenue OR earnings surprise OR stock return ASC 606 disclosure paper”; “current remaining performance obligations OR cRPO abnormal returns OR stock returns OR mispricing academic.” Hits were accounting-disclosure papers, SaaS explainers, and earnings-call news, not a long–short.

## 4. Post-Regulation G non-GAAP exclusions and subsequent returns

### Takeaway
Do not promote on a number. The large published hedge (about 30% over three years) is pre-Regulation G and is built from I/B/E/S exclusions, not from a filing reconciliation. A 2021–2026 paper finds post-Reg G mispricing of persistent exclusions but does not state a return magnitude in sources that could be opened.

### Cited Findings
- Jeffrey T. Doyle, Russell J. Lundholm, and Mark T. Soliman, “The Predictive Value of Expenses Excluded from Pro Forma Earnings,” *Review of Accounting Studies* 8(2), 145–174 (2003). Higher “other exclusions” predict lower future cash flows. A hedge long the decile with the lowest other exclusions and short the decile with the highest “earns an average of 29.9% over the three years subsequent to the earnings announcement,” positive in 10 of 11 formation years. Controls in the return regression: beta, size, book-to-market, and three anomalies (post-earnings-announcement drift, accruals, price momentum). The three-day announcement return already declines with exclusions, but not by enough. — [paper PDF excerpt](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-predictive-value-of-expenses-excluded-from-pro-forma-3gcemotcgv.pdf)
- That design uses the gap between GAAP earnings and I/B/E/S “actual” earnings. Regulation G is March 2003. The paper is not a post-Reg G, filing-extracted exclusion test. — [same PDF](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-predictive-value-of-expenses-excluded-from-pro-forma-3gcemotcgv.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=303563)
- Kurt H. Gee, Ken Li, and Benjamin C. Whipple, “Investor Mispricing of Persistent Non-GAAP Exclusions,” SSRN, posted 13 April 2021, revised 8 October 2026 (still a working paper as of that date). They write that prior research found little evidence investors are misled after Regulation G, then find that persistent exclusions are mispriced, more so when non-GAAP reporting changes, for both sophisticated and unsophisticated investors, and that insiders sell more after more persistent exclusions. No percent, alpha, or holding-period return is in the abstract. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3825314)
- Jeffrey T. Doyle, Jared N. Jennings, and Mark T. Soliman, “Do Managers Define Non-GAAP Earnings to Meet or Beat Analyst Forecasts?” (working paper around 2011; later journal version exists): the market discounts positive earnings surprises that are achieved with income-increasing non-GAAP exclusions. That is a weaker announcement reaction, not a stated subsequent long–short. — [SciSpace summary](https://scispace.com/papers/do-managers-define-non-gaap-earnings-to-meet-or-beat-analyst-2bz24dsepg)
- Jeremiah W. Bentley, Theodore E. Christensen, Kurt H. Gee, and Benjamin C. Whipple, “Disentangling Managers’ and Analysts’ Non-GAAP Reporting,” *Journal of Accounting Research* (2018), is about whether I/B/E/S is a valid proxy for managers’ non-GAAP numbers. No portfolio return was extracted. — [SciSpace related-paper blurb](https://scispace.com/papers/do-managers-define-non-gaap-earnings-to-meet-or-beat-analyst-2bz24dsepg)
- Black, Christensen, Ciesielski, and Whipple are cited in later reviews as the academic-versus-practice survey. No original long–short was extracted. Laurion (2020): no stock-return result was located.

### Inferences
- Verdict: keep downgraded. Do not promote Doyle, Lundholm, and Soliman (2003) as a post-Reg G filing feature. Gee, Li, and Whipple (2026) is the right post-Reg G mispricing paper, but without a stated return it stays off the return shortlist. Non-GAAP reconciliations live mainly in earnings releases (Item 2.02 8-K), not only in the 10-K, so “filing-extractable” is only partly true.

### Gaps
- Return magnitude for Gee, Li, and Whipple: not found. Query: “Gee Whipple persistent exclusions percent OR basis points hedge OR alpha return mispricing.” Query for the named set: “non-GAAP exclusions subsequent stock returns Doyle Bentley Laurion.” Laurion 2020 return result: not found.

## 5. Restatement announcement return versus post-announcement drift

### Takeaway
Kill as a post-disclosure drift an investor can still trade after the announcement. The large negative return is the two-day announcement window. The cost-of-capital papers are a discount-rate revision, not a continuation drift. The one post-announcement return that was found is a partial reversal, and it is an unpublished working paper.

### Cited Findings
- Zoe-Vonna Palmrose, Vernon J. Richardson, and Susan Scholz, “Determinants of Market Reactions to Restatement Announcements,” *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 37(1), 59–89 (2004). Sample: 403 restatements announced 1995–1999. Mean (median) market-adjusted abnormal return is −9.2% (−4.6%) over the two-day window from day 0 to day +1. More negative if fraud, more accounts, income-decreasing, or attributed to auditors or management rather than the SEC. Announcements that do not quantify the restatement have an extra penalty. This is the announcement return, not a subsequent drift. — [archived PDF](https://web.archive.org/web/20170812062241if_/http:/homepages.rpi.edu/home/17/wuq2/yesterday/public_html/restatement%20reference/Palmrose2004jae.pdf); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165410103000594)
- Paul Hribar and Nicole Thorne Jenkins, “The Effect of Accounting Restatements on Earnings Revisions and the Estimated Cost of Capital,” *Review of Accounting Studies* 9, 337–356 (2004). Restatements lower expected earnings and raise the cost of equity. Relative increases in the cost of equity average 7% to 19% in the month after the restatement, depending on the model (7.65% in one Easton-style estimate). After time passes and analyst-forecast bias is controlled, the increase still averages 6% to 15% in the most conservative setting. Auditor-initiated restatements and higher leverage produce larger increases. This is an implied required-return change, not a buy-and-hold abnormal return an investor earns by trading after day +1. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=488003); [archived PDF](https://web.archive.org/web/20170812062239if_/http:/homepages.rpi.edu/home/17/wuq2/yesterday/public_html/restatement%20reference/Hribar2004rast.pdf)
- Laura Frieder and Devin Shanthikumar, “Returns and Risk in the Year after a Restatement Announcement,” SSRN working paper (1 September 2009; an October 2008 draft was circulated to EFMA). They find statistically significant positive returns in the six months after negative restatement announcements, “roughly 5–10% over the three months in the 3–6 month window,” after size, book-to-market, and momentum. They interpret this as information risk falling, not as underreaction. Publication in a journal was not confirmed. — [EFMA draft PDF](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2009-Milan/papers/178.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1465991)

### Inferences
- Verdict: kill for a post-announcement drift shortlist. Shorting after the announcement fights the only post-window return that was found (a rebound). The −9% is real but it is the news window itself. A restatement 8-K is not a slow filing feature in the same sense as a deferred-revenue balance.
- Separate the two objects in any write-up: announcement CAR ≈ −9% over two days (Palmrose, Richardson, and Scholz); no published continuation drift was extracted.

### Gaps
- A published post-2004 continuation-drift magnitude (buy-and-hold abnormal return from day +2 onward) was not found. Query: “post-restatement OR after restatement announcements drift OR buy-and-hold abnormal return subsequent.” Results were the Frieder–Shanthikumar working paper, loss/profit drift (a different anomaly), and a conference table of small post-restatement BHARs that was not a clean long–short.

## 6. Goodwill and delayed impairment

### Takeaway
The predictor in the main paper is numeric (large goodwill and low profitability), not qualitative “headroom” language. The paper states that those firms have lower future stock returns, but the published return percent was not extracted. A secondary blog reads a figure as about −15.6% and says the figure moved across drafts. Kill headroom language as a US return feature.

### Cited Findings
- Kevin K. Li and Richard G. Sloan, “Has Goodwill Accounting Gone Bad?,” *Review of Accounting Studies* 22(2), 964–1003 (June 2017). Setting is SFAS 142, which dropped amortization and left a fair-value impairment test. Impairments are less timely after SFAS 142. “Investors do not appear to fully anticipate the untimely nature of post-SFAS 142 goodwill impairments.” In the post-SFAS 142 period, firms with high goodwill balances and low profitability have both a higher probability of future impairment and lower future stock returns. The working-paper text says this is robust to dropping 2008–2009, dropping pooling-of-interests firms, and controlling for operating complexity. No percent appears in the abstract or in the PDF pages that search snippets returned. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1466271); [ideas.repec abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v22y2017i2d10.1007_s11142-017-9401-7.html); [working-paper PDF snippet](https://assets.super.so/e46b77e7-ee08-445e-b43f-4ffd88ae0a0e/files/e8ffe096-7515-43bc-a676-3316b43325b2.pdf)
- Alpha Architect, summarizing a draft and a chart (secondary, 9 April 2011): the signal is an indicator for the highest quintile or decile of goodwill-to-assets and the lowest quintile or decile of ROA, plus the level of goodwill/assets, ROA, and the change in ROA. They write that the high-goodwill, low-profitability cell “lead[s] to crappy returns … −15.6%,” and that an earlier draft showed about −23%. They flag the chart as hypothetical, not an investor’s realized return, and note the results “shifted around quite a bit” across drafts. Do not treat −15.6% as the published RAS number. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/goodwill-gone-bad/)
- No paper was found in which qualitative goodwill “headroom” language (an IFRS-style sensitivity disclosure more than a US SFAS 142 number) predicts US stock returns.

### Inferences
- Verdict on the number: promote the numeric high-goodwill / low-ROA signal only as a candidate whose direction the RAS paper states (negative future returns, post-SFAS 142). Do not put −15.6% in a shortlist table unless it is labeled secondary and draft-unstable.
- Verdict on qualitative headroom language: kill for this US return screen. The paper’s predictor is the goodwill balance and profitability, both Compustat / face-of-the-statements items.

### Gaps
- Published long–short or alpha: not found in text that could be opened. Query: “Li Sloan goodwill abnormal returns high goodwill low profitability percent year” and “Li Sloan Table 6 one-year-ahead goodwill returns size-adjusted.” The RAS PDF download failed on one attempt. Horizon and factor model for the return test were not extracted from the journal.

## 7. Uncertain tax benefits (FIN 48 / ASC 740-10) and post-filing returns

### Takeaway
Keep as fundamentals-only. UTBs predict future tax cash payments. A signed post-filing long–short or alpha on the UTB balance or on UTB additions was not found. Nearby tax-expense anomalies are a different variable.

### Cited Findings
- FIN 48 (ASC 740-10) requires a reserve for unrecognized tax benefits and a rollforward in the tax footnote of the 10-K. Ciconte, Donohoe, Lisowsky, and Mayberry (seminar draft) find UTBs predict income-tax cash outflows up to three years ahead and explain 8.2% as much of the variation in those outflows as current tax payments do. That is a cash-flow result, not a return. — [seminar PDF](https://feb.kuleuven.be/drc/AFI/misc/2013-10-10-william-ciconte.pdf)
- R. Christopher Small and Jane Z. Song, “Disclosure of Tax-Related Uncertainty and Market Liquidity,” SSRN revised 1 November 2024. Current UTB additions are associated with less trading around the 10-K, wider bid–ask spreads after the 10-K, and a “lengthier post-disclosure drift,” concentrated in tax avoiders, poor reporters, and low institutional ownership. They do not state a signed portfolio alpha or a percent return. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3815951)
- A 2024 *Finance Research Letters* paper finds FIN 48 reduced the tax-expense anomaly of Thomas and Zhang (2011) for affected firms. That is evidence that the mandate made tax-expense surprises less predictive of returns, not a UTB long–short. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612323010589)
- Frischmann, Shevlin, and Wilson (2008) find the component of the first FIN 48 UTB disclosure that would affect the effective tax rate is positively associated with the three-day return at the first-quarter 2007 report. That is an adoption-window announcement association, not a post-filing drift a later investor still harvests. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410108000529)
- Jacob Thomas and Frank Zhang, “Tax expense surprises and future returns” (December 2007 draft): seasonally differenced quarterly tax expense is positively related to returns over the next two quarters, incremental to book-income surprise, size, book-to-market, accruals, and momentum. This is tax expense, not the FIN 48 reserve. An emerging-markets paper reports a tax-expense-surprise zero-investment portfolio of 1.06% per month. Neither is a UTB result. — [Yale PDF](https://repec.som.yale.edu/icfpub/publications/2531.pdf); [emerging-markets abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S221484502100106X)

### Inferences
- Verdict: keep as fundamentals-only (future tax cash). Do not promote to the return shortlist. Direction of any delayed price response to UTB additions is not established as a tradable sign in the sources above; Small and Song only say price discovery is slower.

### Gaps
- Post-filing long–short, alpha, or buy-and-hold abnormal return on UTB levels or additions: not found. Queries: “FIN 48 uncertain tax benefits stock returns post filing”; “unrecognized tax benefits stock returns OR future returns OR abnormal return FIN 48 portfolio”; “unrecognized tax benefit OR UTB additions future stock returns OR returns over the subsequent percent.”

## 8. Customer-concentration change versus cost of equity

### Takeaway
A tradable long–short is claimed for the change in concentration, but the journal abstract does not state the spread. The author’s own slides show about 10 percentage points of next-year return per one-unit change in the concentration index. Cost-of-equity papers find a small risk premium (about 21 basis points), which is not an abnormal-return strategy. Promote only the change, and do not quote a portfolio spread as if the journal stated one.

### Cited Findings
- Panos N. Patatoukas, “Customer-Base Concentration: Implications for Firm Performance and Capital Markets,” *The Accounting Review* 87(2), 363–392 (2012). CC is a concentration index built from SFAS 131 major-customer disclosures in the 10-K. Levels of CC are positively associated with accounting rates of return (efficiencies: lower operating expenses per sales dollar and higher asset turnover). Changes: investors underreact, and “a trading strategy that exploits investors’ underreaction yields abnormal stock returns over the thirty-year period studied.” The abstract does not give the percent. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1666497); [TAR record](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/87/2/363/3375/Customer-Base-Concentration-Implications-for-Firm)
- Author slides of that paper, Table 7: the coefficient on ΔCC in one-year-ahead stock-return regressions is about 0.097 to 0.101 (t-statistics about 3.8 to 4.1) across specifications that include size. Slides say results are insensitive to stock returns, customer-base stock returns, industry returns, CAPM beta, volatility, and distress. Because CC is a share-based index on the unit interval, 0.10 is the return associated with a full zero-to-one move in ΔCC, not a decile long–short. Contemporaneous return regressions (Table 6) have a ΔCC coefficient of about 0.14 to 0.18. — [author slides PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/presentation/7d4b/a069dd746f61c91f25e87457b802d155799e.pdf)
- Secondary only: Alpha Architect (15 February 2011) says top-decile concentrated suppliers outperform the bottom decile by 8.96% per year market-adjusted and 6.24% after risk controls. The wording is about the level of concentration, and it is a blog. Do not treat it as the paper’s stated long–short. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/customer-base-concentration-implications-for-firm-performance-and-capital-markets/)
- Dan Dhaliwal, J. Scott Judd, Matthew Serfling, and Sarah Shaikh, “Customer Concentration Risk and the Cost of Equity Capital,” *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 61(1), 23–48 (2016). A supplier that depends on major customers for at least 10% of revenue has a cost of equity 21.2 basis points higher (about $7.99 million a year of extra equity financing cost for the average supplier). Stronger when losing a customer is more likely or more costly. A concentrated base of government customers has a lower cost of equity. They also find a higher cost of debt. This is a priced risk, not a mispricing alpha. — [ScienceDirect abstract and highlights](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410115000336)

### Inferences
- Verdict: promote ΔCC only as a weak return candidate, and state the magnitude as a regression slope (~0.10 next-year return per unit ΔCC) from the author’s slides, not as a journal long–short. A tradable long–short is claimed in the TAR abstract but the spread was not extracted from the article. Do not put the cost-of-equity papers on a return shortlist; +21 bp of cost of equity is the opposite conceptual object (required return, not abnormal return) and is too small to be the Patatoukas effect.
- Same filing as feature 1 (major-customer table). The tradeable object is the change in the supplier’s own customer Herfindahl, not the customer’s stock return.

### Gaps
- The TAR portfolio table (formation rule, equal- versus value-weight, factor model, and the actual long–short percent) was not opened. Query: “Patatoukas trading strategy customer concentration abnormal return percent annual.” The 8.96% / 6.24% figures appear only on the secondary blog.

## 9. Piotroski F-score and Beneish M-score

### Takeaway
Promote both as numeric scores, not as prose features. An LLM can build them only by extracting financial-statement numbers (current year and prior year, which the 10-K already compares). The original F-score long–short is a 23% annual return inside high book-to-market stocks, 1976–1996. The M-score return result is later work: a bit under 1% a month risk-adjusted, not a number in Beneish (1999) itself.

### Cited Findings
- Joseph D. Piotroski, “Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information to Separate Winners from Losers,” *Journal of Accounting Research* 38 (Supplement), 1–41 (2000). Nine binary signals from the financial statements: profitability (ROA, cash flow from operations, change in ROA, accruals), leverage and liquidity (change in leverage, change in current ratio, equity issuance), and efficiency (change in gross margin, change in asset turnover). Applied only to high book-to-market firms. “The mean return earned by a high book-to-market investor can be increased by at least 7.5% annually” by keeping financially strong firms. “An investment strategy that buys expected winners and shorts expected losers generates a 23% annual return between 1976 and 1996.” A Chicago Booth restatement of the same paper: lowest-signal firms had a one-year market-adjusted return of −9.6%; highest-signal firms +13.4%; the high-score group beat the low-score group by 23% and beat the generic high book-to-market portfolio by 7.5%. One-sixth of the annual gap is earned in the four three-day windows around the next year’s quarterly earnings announcements. The edge is concentrated in small and medium firms, low share turnover, and no analyst following, and it is not just a low-price effect. The abstract says the 23% strategy “appears to be robust across time and to controls for alternative investment strategies.” — [JSTOR abstract](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2672906); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/separating-winners-losers)
- A later replication that says its raw returns match Piotroski’s Table 3 reports a one-year raw-return gap of 23.53% between F-scores of 8–9 and F-scores of 0–1 over 1976–2007. — [replication PDF](https://assets.super.so/e46b77e7-ee08-445e-b43f-4ffd88ae0a0e/files/8aa53cb4-ac47-4a64-95e4-c77fb0d0c834.pdf)
- Later and outside the original design, high-minus-low F-score premiums are smaller. A 2020 study cites Piotroski and So (2012) for a US market-wide F-score premium of 10.03% per year, and finds about 10% per year in developed non-US markets and about 12% in emerging markets, including after size, book-to-market, momentum, operating profitability, and investment. — [Journal of Asset Management 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-020-00157-2)
- Messod D. Beneish, “The Detection of Earnings Manipulation,” *Financial Analysts Journal* 55(5), 24–36 (1999). Eight numeric ratios (days-sales in receivables, gross margin, asset quality, sales growth, depreciation, SG&A, leverage, total accruals). The model flags about half of manipulators before public discovery. The abstract says discovered manipulators’ stocks “plummet,” but it does not state a long–short return. — [CFA Institute abstract](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/1999/the-detection-of-earnings-manipulation)
- Messod D. Beneish, Charles M. C. Lee, and D. Craig Nichols, “Earnings Manipulation and Expected Returns,” *Financial Analysts Journal* 69(2), 57–82 (2013). Higher M-score firms earn lower returns in every decile of size, book-to-market, momentum, accruals, and short interest. “These returns are economically significant (averaging just below 1% a month on a risk-adjusted basis) and survive a host of risk controls.” A large share of the abnormal return falls in the three-day windows around the next four quarterly earnings releases. In the lowest-accrual quintile, the size-adjusted spread between high and low M-score over the next 12 months is −19.8%. The M-score’s edge over accruals is strongest among low-accrual firms. — [ideas.repec abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v69y2013i2p57-82.html)
- A related Beneish, Lee, and Nichols text (“To Catch a Thief”) reports that firms flagged by the 1999 model, out of sample, had one-year-ahead size-adjusted returns of −6.6% versus +3.1% for unflagged firms, a 9.7% gap, and that PROBM predicts returns from 1993–2007 after size, book-to-market, momentum, accruals, and short interest. Treat 9.7% as that paper’s size-adjusted flag-versus-not gap, alongside the FAJ “just below 1% a month” risk-adjusted figure, not as a second independent anomaly. — [Academia.edu extract](https://www.academia.edu/122981262/To_Catch_a_Thief_Can_Forensic_Accounting_Help_Predict_Stock_Returns)

### Inferences
- Verdict: promote both onto a numeric-score shortlist, and label them as scores. They are not MD&A or footnote-prose features. F-score’s 23% is not a market-wide, post-2000 fact; the design is high book-to-market, 1976–1996, and later market-wide evidence is closer to 10% a year. M-score’s return evidence is Beneish, Lee, and Nichols (2013), sign negative for high manipulation probability, horizon about one year, with much of the return at later earnings announcements.
- An LLM “reading the annual report” can construct them only if it pulls the face statements and the cash-flow statement for year t and year t−1. Errors in extraction are accounting errors, not a text-understanding edge.

### Gaps
- The original Piotroski tables were not opened; the −9.6% / +13.4% split is from Chicago Booth’s account of the paper, and the 23% hedge is in the JSTOR abstract. Beneish (1999) portfolio abnormal return: not stated in the abstract. Query context was “Piotroski F-score hedge return Beneish M-score abnormal return magnitude.”

## 10. Year-over-year change in a specific MD&A topic, incremental to Compustat, 2015–2026

### Takeaway
The strong return paper in this window is Lazy Prices: year-over-year changes in 10-K/10-Q language, especially litigation language and the risk-factor section, earn up to 188 basis points a month of alpha. It was not shown, in sources opened here, to be incremental to the numeric Compustat variable for the same topic (litigation reserve, backlog, or margin). No paper was found that isolates YoY prose about backlog, margins, or demand and beats the corresponding Compustat number in a return test.

### Cited Findings
- Lauren Cohen, Christopher Malloy, and Quoc Nguyen, “Lazy Prices,” NBER Working Paper w25084 (September 2018; SSRN revised through January 2026). US 10-Ks and 10-Qs, 1995–2014. A portfolio that shorts firms that change their filings and buys firms that do not “earns up to 188 basis points in monthly alphas (over 22% per year).” Changes “referring to the executive (CEO and CFO) team, regarding litigation, or in the risk factor section” are especially informative. The changes predict future earnings, profitability, news, and bankruptcies. There is no announcement effect; returns show up later when the news arrives. The abstract does not say the factor model, and it does not say the litigation-language result is incremental to a recorded litigation reserve or other Compustat item. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)
- Secondary detail only: Alpha Architect says the broad changer/non-changer long–short is about 30–60 basis points per month (up to about 7.6% over the next year), and that the risk-factor-section sort specifically is a five-factor alpha of 188 basis points per month, t-statistic 2.76. Quantt repeats that split. Use these only as a labeled secondary decomposition of the abstract’s “up to 188.” — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/); [Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)
- Adjacent, not the requested test: Jun Duanmu, Qiping Huang, and Yongjia Li, “Litigation Risk and Stock Return Anomaly,” *Financial Analysts Journal* (2022). A dynamic logit of securities-litigation risk, not an MD&A topic count. Out-of-sample long–short annual alpha “exceeding 8%” / “over 8%,” mostly from the long side of low litigation risk, robust to other anomaly factors, and attributed to underreaction to changes in litigation risk. Not a filing-topic residual. — [CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2022/litigation-risk-stock-return-anomaly)
- Heather Li, “Litigation-Disclosure Modifications in the 10-K,” *Journal of Accounting, Auditing & Finance* (first published online 30 May 2021). YoY modification of Item 103 legal proceedings and the ASC 450 contingency note predicts future lawsuits. No stock-return magnitude in the abstract. — [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0148558X211017838)
- A 2025 topic-model paper on 10-K MD&A (sample through 2018) finds topic-level sentiment related to returns at different horizons (profit-and-loss sentiment in the short run; regulation and tax later; investment sentiment in the long run) and says overall MD&A sentiment is mostly unrelated to returns except at long horizons. It does not state a long–short alpha, and it does not show a YoY topic change incremental to the Compustat counterpart. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105752192500208X)
- MD&A tone and stock returns in Chinese A-shares (*Journal of Contemporary Accounting & Economics*, December 2024) is not US equities. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1815566924000407)

### Inferences
- Verdict: promote Lazy Prices-style year-over-year filing-language change, and specifically litigation-section and risk-factor edits, onto the return shortlist. Sign: changers underperform non-changers. Horizon: subsequent months, with no filing-day jump. Do not claim it is incremental to the numeric Compustat variable; that test was not in the abstract.
- Verdict on “YoY MD&A topic about backlog, margins, or demand, incremental to the Compustat number”: not found, so do not promote those three topics as return features. Order-backlog and RPO changes predict future revenue (see feature 3) without a return magnitude.
