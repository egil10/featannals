# Primary evidence for four filing-text return results

Scope is the four named return results plus the Hoberg–Phillips boom-bust result. Numbers below are only those read in a downloaded PDF or in a page/PDF excerpt the search index returned with surrounding table or section context. Where the publisher PDF was blocked, the number is labeled unverified against the typeset journal page.

## Florackis, Louca, Michaely, and Weber, cybersecurity risk

### Takeaway
The headline 66–69 bp/month is the equal-weighted long-short result (raw excess about 68 bp; Carhart alpha 69 bp; Fama–French five-factor alpha 66 bp), not a value-weighted or q-factor alpha. The text is Item 1A over roughly 2007–2018, returns run March 2008–March 2019, Item 1A length does not kill the premium, and there is no true post-2018 re-estimation of their score through 2026.

### Cited Findings
- Published paper: Chris Florackis, Christodoulos Louca, Roni Michaely, and Michael Weber, “Cybersecurity Risk,” *Review of Financial Studies* 36(1), January 2023, 351–407, https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhac024. Abstract: high-exposure portfolios outperform “by up to 8.3% per year,” and high-exposure firms “perform poorly in periods of high cybersecurity risk.” — [RFS abstract](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/36/1/351/6585907); [NBER w28196](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28196)
- All table numbers below are from the downloaded NBER Working Paper 28196, revised March 2022 (95 pages), not from the typeset RFS pages. The WP’s own abstract and Table 7 match the published 8.3% claim. — [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28196/w28196.pdf)
- Construction: cosine similarity of Item 1A cybersecurity-risk sentences to disclosures of firms hit by major attacks, using the one-year window before the filing. Disclosure window in the text is 2007–2018. Firms without Item 1A are dropped; firms that file Item 1A inside other sections are excluded, following the Hoberg–Phillips screen. — same NBER PDF, pp. 3–5 and 12 of the manuscript
- Portfolio design (Table 7 note and §4.1): from December 2007, sort each quarter into three groups. Portfolio 1 is firms with no cybersecurity disclosure; the rest are split at the median. Hold for the next quarter. Returns are March 2008–March 2019, both equal-weighted and value-weighted, in excess of the risk-free rate. Newey–West t-statistics use 12 lags. Firms with fewer than three years in sample and zero cyber disclosure throughout are dropped. — same PDF, manuscript pp. 22–23 and Table 7
- Table 7 Panel A, monthly percent, long-short P3−P1. Equal-weighted: excess return 0.678 (t = 4.56); CAPM alpha 0.673 (t = 4.69); Carhart four-factor (FFC: market, SMB, HML, momentum) alpha 0.686 (t = 4.80); Fama–French (2015) five-factor alpha 0.657 (t = 4.38). Value-weighted: excess return 0.609 (t = 3.02); CAPM alpha 0.660 (t = 3.41); FFC alpha 0.559 (t = 3.30); five-factor alpha 0.574 (t = 3.58). Levels: EW excess is 0.167 / 0.710 / 0.845 across P1 / P2 / P3. — same PDF, Table 7
- The manuscript’s own wording of the headline (§1 and the conclusion): an equal-weighted long-short “earns a statistically significant excess return of 66 to 69 basis points per month, or 8.3% per year”; value-weighted is “7.9% per year”; the conclusion repeats “66–69 basis points per month — up to 8.3% — in equal-weighted returns.” Simple annualization of the Table 7 EW FFC alpha is 0.686 × 12 ≈ 8.2%, and of the VW CAPM alpha is 0.660 × 12 ≈ 7.9%. The five-factor VW alpha annualizes nearer 6.9%, so 7.9% is not the five-factor value-weighted number. — same PDF, manuscript pp. 3, 5, and 42, read against Table 7
- Factors actually used in the headline table: CAPM, Carhart (1997) FFC, and Fama–French (2015) five factors. No Hou–Xue–Zhang q-factor appears in the portfolio section, Table 7, Table 8, Table 9, or Table 10. — same PDF
- Fama–MacBeth (Table 9): standardized cybersecurity index, Newey–West t-statistics. Univariate slope on next-month excess return is 0.298 (t = 6.28). With beta, size, book-to-market, momentum, reversal, illiquidity, coskewness, idiosyncratic volatility, asset growth, ROA, R&D, and MAX, the slope is 0.102 (t = 2.64). Adding log Item 1A length and log 10-K file size (readability) raises it slightly to 0.124 (t = 2.80); the length coefficient itself is −0.055 (t = −1.42). The same full specification is still positive at horizons t+2 (0.117, t = 2.86), t+3 (0.111, t = 2.82), t+6 (0.150, t = 3.11), t+9 (0.122, t = 2.98), and t+12 (0.115, t = 2.69). — same PDF, Table 9
- Item 1A volume is controlled, not ignored. Table 8 double sorts on log risk-section length: equal-weighted five-factor high-minus-low cyber alpha is 0.348 (t = 2.61) in the short-Item-1A half and 1.225 (t = 4.91) in the long-Item-1A half; value-weighted five-factor alphas are 0.559 (t = 2.87) and 0.488 (t = 3.03). The premium is larger, not smaller, among long risk-factor sections in the equal-weighted sort. They also cite Campbell, Chen, Dhaliwal, Lu, and Steele (2014) that risk-factor text is not boilerplate, and they say firm fixed effects remove generic disclosure in a validation regression. They do not residualize a separate boilerplate-similarity score inside Table 7. — same PDF, Table 8 continued, manuscript pp. 8 and 20, and the Table 9 length control
- Risk-premium interpretation, not a claimed underreaction drift. The factor is the average of two value-weighted high-score portfolios minus two value-weighted low-score portfolios, crossed with a median size split (§4.4). On daily data, March 2008–March 2019, a high Google-SVI day (“Data Breach” and “Hacker,” above mean plus 1.5 standard deviations) has coefficient −0.0004 on the factor in the five-factor specification (t = −2.64); the daily intercept is +0.0002 (t = 3.63). Table 10. — same PDF, §4.4 and Table 10
- Their own extension past 2018 is a forward-fill, not new 10-Ks. Scores stop in 2018; they replace missing 2019–2020 values with the last 2017 or 2018 score and rerun March 2008–December 2020. They say Internet Appendix Table IA.7 Panel K “continue[s] to support” a positive premium, but the panel’s coefficient is not in the WP body, so that post-2018 magnitude is unverified here. They also write that returns are higher in the second half of the original sample, again without a second-half alpha in the main text. — same PDF, manuscript pp. 40–42
- No replication of this cosine/Item 1A score through 2026 was found. The closest open critique is a different measure: Celeny, “Cyber risk and the cross-section of stock returns” (arXiv:2402.04775; EPFL thesis PDF also circulated by Natixis). Paragraph-vector score on the full 10-K, value-weighted quintiles, January 2009–December 2022. Table 5: P5−P1 average monthly excess return 0.56% (t = 1.72); five-factor alpha 0.41% (t = 2.21). The abstract’s 6.93% a year and 18.72% a year are the long-short and the high-quantile portfolio, not Florackis’s 8.3%. Before the Florackis SSRN posting (January 2009–October 2020) the long-short excess return is 0.94% per month (t = 3.23) and the five-factor alpha is 0.56% (t = 3.01). After it (November 2020–December 2022) the long-short excess return is −1.49% per month (t = −2.11); the CAPM alpha is −1.74% (t = −3.35), but the Carhart alpha (−0.39, t = −0.67) and five-factor alpha (−0.23, t = −0.34) are not significant. Celeny reports a 34% correlation with the Florackis score and argues the dictionary/attack-trained Item 1A method can mix post-attack recovery with latent risk. This is not a replication of Florackis, and it stops in 2022. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.04775v2); local text of https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04775, Tables 5, 14, and 15 and §4.5–4.6.3

### Inferences
- The existing shortlist is right on the equal-weighted 66–69 bp range, the 8.3% equal-weighted annualization, Fama–MacBeth predictability out to 12 months, the 2007–2018 Item 1A window, and the risk-premium reading. It is loose on value-weighted 7.9%: that figure lines up with the value-weighted CAPM alpha, not the value-weighted five-factor alpha (0.57% per month).
- Momentum is partialled out. The 69 bp number is the Carhart alpha; the 66 bp number is the five-factor alpha. Nobody in this paper runs a q-factor model.
- Item 1A boilerplate volume is not an omitted control in the cross-section: length and file size are in the Fama–MacBeth specification, and the double sort shows the spread inside both short and long Item 1A halves. That is not the same as a boilerplate-similarity residual.
- Nothing in hand shows that the Florackis score, re-estimated on 10-Ks after 2018, still earns 66 bp through 2026. Their own “through 2020” test carries forward old scores. A different cyber-text score loses its long-short excess return after November 2020 and is estimated only through December 2022.

### Gaps
- Typeset RFS page numbers and any Internet Appendix coefficient (including the forward-filled 2019–2020 alpha and the second-half split) were not read. The Liverpool author-accepted manuscript and the Oxford PDF returned 403.
- No q-factor column was found. If the published RFS added one, it is not in the March 2022 NBER revision that was read.
- No paper that recomputes the Florackis cosine score on Item 1A through 2025 or 2026 was found.

## Buehlmaier and Whited, textual financial constraints

### Takeaway
The 6.5% / 3.7% / 3.0% figures are annualized Fama–French five-factor alphas on zero-cost portfolios, largest for debt-market constraints, and the authors treat them as a risk premium. The published PDF is paywalled, so the monthly alpha, its t-statistic, and the exact sample end date were not verified. Momentum is not in the factor model they cite for the published result.

### Cited Findings
- Published paper: Matthias M. M. Buehlmaier and Toni M. Whited, “Are Financial Constraints Priced? Evidence from Textual Analysis,” *Review of Financial Studies* 31(7), July 2018, 2693–2728, https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhy007. Abstract: three text measures (equity markets, debt markets, and external finance in general); constrained firms earn higher returns that “cannot be explained by the Fama and French (2015) factor model”; the strategy “is most profitable for large, liquid stocks”; “a portfolio based on this [debt] measure earns an annualized risk-adjusted excess return of 6.5%.” Received 4 April 2016; accepted 17 December 2017. Unpaywall lists the article as not open access. — [RFS abstract](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/31/7/2693/4824924); [Ideas/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v31y2018i7p2693-2728..html)
- The authors’ own summary, posted 10 March 2018 and tied to the forthcoming RFS article, repeats the portfolio definition and the three alphas. They sort on the text measures, find higher returns for constrained firms, and “regress these returns … on well-known risk factors.” A zero-cost factor that averages out size quantiles, regressed on the Fama–French five factors, has an annualized alpha of 7.2% for the debt measure inside the top market-capitalization percentile. The annualized risk-adjusted excess returns on the zero-cost portfolios are 6.5% (debt), 3.7% (general external finance), and 3.0% (equity). They interpret the result as compensation for financial risk, and they say debt constraints matter because equity issues are rare and firms that can issue debt in bad times have lower systematic risk. They also say the premium is in large-cap and mid-cap constrained stocks, not small-cap, and that explicit trading costs do not meaningfully reduce the returns. Momentum is not mentioned on this page. — [Harvard Law School Forum, 10 March 2018](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/03/10/are-financial-constraints-priced-evidence-from-textual-analysis/)
- How the score is built, from that same author summary plus the search-indexed 5 November 2017 manuscript (HKU handle 10722/261048; this is a pre-publication revision, not the typeset RFS): (1) human or machine reading isolates a training sample of firm-years that look constrained or unconstrained; (2) the probability of being constrained is estimated from the words; (3) the fitted model scores every U.S. firm. Three training samples produce three probabilities: general external finance, equity issuance, and debt issuance. The indexed manuscript says the classifier is naïve Bayes on word counts, infrequent words are dropped, and the text is the MD&A of the 10-K, following Li (2010), Hoberg and Maksimovic (2015), and Bodnaruk, Loughran, and McDonald (2015). The Harvard summary says “narrative portions of company annual reports,” not a word list. — [indexed 5 November 2017 manuscript](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/261048/1/Content.pdf); Harvard post above. Direct download of that PDF returned 403, so the naïve-Bayes and MD&A sentences are from the indexer’s text extract, not a locally opened file.
- An appendix table in that same indexed 2017 manuscript (the extract labels it around Table A27, debt training sample, liquidity factor of Pástor and Stambaugh included) reports annualized alphas of −2.88 (t = −2.26) for low constraints, 0.84 (t = 0.76) for the middle portfolio, 3.54 (t = 2.39) for high constraints, and 6.42 (t = 3.19) for high-minus-low, with about 200 monthly observations. The extract says these alphas are annualized and after trading costs. This is a robustness table, not the main 6.5% specification, and the table number may not match the published appendix. — same indexed manuscript
- The November 2014 draft (“Looking for Risk in Words,” HKU 10722/213634) is a different paper in the numbers. Compustat runs 1 January 1994–31 December 2010; 10-K MD&A is downloaded for 1994–2010; naïve Bayes models P(constrained) from MD&A word counts. That draft’s zero-cost excess returns are 6.7% (debt), 0.1% (equity), and 4.6% (general), and it says the factor is not explained by Fama–French and momentum, with an annualized risk-adjusted excess return of about 7%. Those are not the published 6.5 / 3.7 / 3.0 figures. — [2014 draft PDF](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/213634/1/Content.pdf)

### Inferences
- The shortlist’s 6.5%, 3.7%, 3.0%, and 7.2%-inside-the-top-size-percentile figures match the authors’ own account of the forthcoming RFS paper. They are annualized risk-adjusted returns, not monthly alphas. Dividing 6.5 by 12 would be an inference, not a reported monthly alpha.
- The published claim is a premium (“investors need compensation for taking on financial risk”), not post-announcement drift. The long-short is constrained minus unconstrained, with size averaged out so the sort is not a size sort.
- “Not explained by FF5” is what the abstract and the author summary actually say. Momentum is not in FF5. The 2014 draft did mention momentum, but its returns are not the published returns. The existing note that momentum is not clearly partialled out of the published 6.5% is consistent with the sources that could be read.
- Enough of the pipeline is public to aim an LLM at the same object: MD&A of the 10-K, three separate constraint concepts (debt, equity, general), a training sample of clear constrained and unconstrained firm-years, naïve Bayes (or a successor classifier) on word counts, then a probability used as a cross-sectional sort. The training-sample rules, the stop-word cutoff, and the exact portfolio breakpoints were not in the open excerpts.

### Gaps
- The typeset RFS PDF and the 5 November 2017 manuscript could not be downloaded (HTTP 403). Monthly alpha and its t-statistic for the headline 6.5% portfolio were not found. The 6.42 (t = 3.19) figure is an appendix-style debt high-minus-low alpha in the indexed 2017 file, and it should not be quoted as the published main-table t-statistic.
- The sample end date of the published paper was not in the abstract or the Harvard summary. Only the 2014 draft states 1994–2010. Do not treat 1994–2010 as the RFS sample without the published data section.
- No post-publication replication of the return spread was read.

## Material-weakness drift (Ghosh, Ikäheimo, Myllymäki, and Sihvonen)

### Takeaway
The paper is about post-disclosure stock drift. The 3% and 6% figures are the material-weakness firms’ own buy-and-hold abnormal returns over 60 and 120 trading days; the 5.3–6.2% figure is the gap versus effective-control firms over the long window; 10–16% and 11–12% are annualizations of that drift, not a second sample. The design is SOX 302, 2007–2023, size and size/book-to-market matching. The announcement move is under 1% but statistically detectable.

### Cited Findings
- Exact citation: Al (Aloke) Ghosh, Seppo Ikäheimo, Emma-Riikka Myllymäki, and Jukka Sihvonen, “Long-Run Stock Returns Following Internal Control Disclosures,” *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting* 53(1), February 2026, 131–149. Early online 16 September 2025. https://doi.org/10.1111/jbfa.70012. The article is open access; the PDF download returned 403, so the table text below is from the Wiley HTML extract, not a locally saved PDF. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jbfa.70012); [Aalto record](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/d8633c4d-4ed8-422e-9cb5-0c745ae6aa3b/); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jbfnac:v:53:y:2026:i:1:p:131-149)
- Abstract, verbatim in substance: SOX Section 302 disclosures, 2007–2023; “negligible market reaction around announcement dates”; “economically large negative drift over the following two quarters, representing approximately 10%–16% annualized underperformance.” The drift “survives risk-based explanations, the post-earnings-announcement drift, and other anomalies linked to accruals, momentum, distress risk, and penny stocks.” Mispricing “is confined to firms with low institutional ownership.” Investors “fail to incorporate differences in conditional (79%) and unconditional (6%) probabilities of subsequent material weaknesses.” — same abstract on Wiley, Aalto, and EconPapers
- Body, as extracted from the Wiley page: U.S. firms with mandated quarterly SOX 302 disclosure-control reports, 2007–2023. The 4-day abnormal reaction is “economically weak, less than 1%.” Buy-and-hold abnormal returns over +60 and +120 trading days use firm size matching and size-and-book-to-market matching. “BHARs are approximately 3% (6%) for a 60-(120-) trading-day window. The difference between the returns for effective firms and material weakness firms is approximately 5.3%–6.2%, which corresponds to approximately 11%–12% annualized return.” — [Wiley HTML extract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jbfa.70012)
- Table 3 is “Buy-and-hold abnormal returns and internal control disclosures: matched sample.” Panel A uses the earnings-announcement date. SAR is the size-matched buy-and-hold abnormal return; SBMAR is the size- and book-to-market-matched return. Windows are [−2, +1], [+2, +60], and [+2, +120]. Effective controls (EFF), in percent: [−2, +1] SAR 0.09 (t = 1.86), SBMAR 0.08 (t = 1.65), N = 181,818 and 174,351; [+2, +60] SAR −0.36 (t = −4.78), SBMAR −0.29 (t = −4.16); [+2, +120] SAR −0.54 (t = −5.03), SBMAR −0.45 (t = −4.01). Material-weakness firms (MW): [−2, +1] SAR −0.67 (t = −4.98), SBMAR −0.58 (t = −4.08); [+2, +60] SAR −3.54 (t = −8.23), SBMAR −2.93 (t = −6.75); [+2, +120] SAR −6.72, SBMAR −5.77 (t-statistics for those two cells were cut off in the extract). A difference row in the same extract is −0.61 (t = −4.73), −0.47 (t = −3.75), −2.98 (t = −6.45), −2.60 (t = −5.90), −5.87 (t = −7.90), −5.33 (t = −7.50), with a neighboring observation count of 13,610 and 12,655 that the extract attaches to the MW block. — same Wiley extract
- The prose under that table says the [+2, +60] and [+2, +120] SAR gaps (described there as EFF minus MW, but the sign is the MW-minus-EFF sign) are −3.17% (t = −7.30) and −6.17% (t = −8.34). Those prose gaps do not equal the difference-row cells −2.98 and −5.87. Both sets of numbers are in the same Wiley extract; the conflict is unresolved without the PDF. SBMAR means for MW firms are given in prose as −2.93% and −5.77%, which do match the table cells. A later paragraph gives filing-date SARs for MW of −2.64% and −5.56% at 60 and 120 days, EFF −0.26% and −0.39%, differences −2.38% (t = −3.71) and −5.17% (t = −5.17). A regression coefficient on MW of −4.71 (t = −8.48) for the 120-day SBMAR, and “approximately 5%” after controls including size, book-to-market, leverage, and Z-score, is also in that extract (coefficients −5.07 and −4.71 are both mentioned). — same Wiley extract
- The paper is a drift paper. The abstract’s mechanism is underreaction to the persistence of internal-control problems, and the return windows start after the announcement ([+2, +60] and [+2, +120]). — same sources

### Inferences
- The two wordings are not the same number. About 3% and about 6% are the material-weakness firm’s own abnormal return over 60 and 120 trading days (Table 3 SAR is −3.54% and −6.72%; SBMAR is −2.93% and −5.77%). About 5.3–6.2% is the gap versus effective firms over the 120-day window (difference-row SBMAR −5.33%; the prose also states a −6.17% SAR gap). About 11–12% is the authors’ annualization of that 5.3–6.2% gap. About 10–16% is the abstract’s annualization of “the following two quarters.” A 120-trading-day window is roughly half a year, so doubling a 5.3–6.2% gap produces the 11–12% figure. The 16% upper end is a looser annualization, not a third holding-period return.
- “Announcement reaction negligible” means economically small (under 1%: SAR −0.67%, SBMAR −0.58%), not statistically zero. Both announcement t-statistics are about −4 to −5.
- The benchmark is a matched-firm buy-and-hold abnormal return (size, and size plus book-to-market), not a Fama–French alpha. The abstract says the drift survives risk adjustments and several anomalies, but those coefficients were not in the extract.
- This is SOX 302 quarterly disclosure-control reporting. It is not, on the pages that were read, a SOX 404 auditor-attestation study.

### Gaps
- The PDF itself was blocked (403 on Wiley and on the Aalto bitstream). Table 3’s MW t-statistics at 120 days, the MW observation count, and the institutional-ownership split table were not fully visible. The 79% versus 6% persistence figures are abstract-only here.
- No SOX 404 sample or 302-versus-404 comparison appeared in the extracted text. If one is in a later table, it was not read.
- The prose gap (−3.17 / −6.17) and the difference row (−2.98 / −5.87) disagree by a few tenths of a percent. Do not collapse them into one t-statistic.

## Doyle, Lundholm, and Soliman, pro forma exclusions, and one post-Regulation G return test

### Takeaway
The paper’s own return result is a size- and book-to-market-adjusted three-year hedge of 29.9% (31.2% in a regression with risk and anomaly controls), not “up to 45 percent.” The Michigan Ross sentence is larger than any return number found in the paper. Post-Regulation G work does test subsequent returns, but the open record does not support another 30% three-year hedge; the clean published statement is a correction “in the following months,” and the persistent-exclusion mispricing paper is still an SSRN working paper without a readable return table.

### Cited Findings
- Paper: Jeffrey T. Doyle, Russell J. Lundholm, and Mark T. Soliman, “The Predictive Value of Expenses Excluded from Pro Forma Earnings,” *Review of Accounting Studies* 8(2–3), 2003, 145–174, https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1024472210359. The PDF download (SSRN, Deep Blue, Springer) was blocked. The quotations and the table below are from the search index’s text extract of the published article as mirrored at SciSpace. Treat the pagination as the published RAS article, but the file was not opened locally. — [SciSpace mirror extract](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-predictive-value-of-expenses-excluded-from-pro-forma-3gcemotcgv.pdf); [Deep Blue record](https://hdl.handle.net/2027.42/47728); [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=303563)
- Sample, from that extract (§1.3): “The full sample consists of 143,462 firm-quarter observations from 1988 to 1999” with Compustat, CRSP, and I/B/E/S. That window ends before Regulation G (2003). — same extract
- Cash-flow results in the extract’s introduction, not the Ross paraphrase. One dollar of pro forma earnings in a quarter predicts 7.895 dollars of cash from operations over the next three years. One dollar of total exclusions predicts 3.328 fewer dollars. “Special items are generally unrelated to future cash flows.” One dollar of other exclusions predicts 6.422 fewer dollars over three years. A later sentence, restricted to the nonzero-exclusion sample, says one dollar of other exclusions predicts 4.17 fewer dollars, against 7.09 dollars for a dollar of pro forma earnings. The Ross page’s “special items predict +$1” and “other exclusions −$3” does not match either pair. The −$3 figure is close to the total-exclusion coefficient (−3.328), not to other exclusions. — same extract; Ross page, which is the source of the 45% sentence: [Michigan Ross archive](https://www.bus.umich.edu/FacultyResearch/InTheNews/Archive/ProFormaEarnings.htm)
- Return result, Table 7 Panel B in the extract, portfolios on other exclusions. The columns are size- and book-to-market-adjusted returns RMA_YR1, RMA_YR2, and RMA_YR3, in percent. Low decile, year 3: 17.3. High decile, year 3: −12.6. Hedge (low minus high): 4.4 in year 1, 5.0 in year 2, and 29.9 in year 3. The hedge is positive in 10 of 11 formation years. The extract says the 29.9% three-year other-exclusions hedge, and an 11.3% three-year total-exclusions hedge, beat a book-to-market and size match with randomization p-values 0.001 and 0.016. A regression that adds beta, size, book-to-market, post-earnings-announcement drift, accruals, and price momentum raises the three-year other-exclusions estimate from 29.9% to 31.2%. The year-1 other-exclusions hedge is described as unclear; the result “is very strong for three years ahead.” — same extract, Table 7 Panel B and the surrounding prose (RAS pp. 148 and 170 in the extract’s running heads)
- The string “45 percent” or “45%” did not appear in the indexed paper text that was returned. The Ross archive does say: “in the three years after the earnings announcement, stock returns are up to 45 percent lower for firms with relatively large exclusions, compared with those firms with small exclusions.” That sentence is not the paper’s reported hedge. — [Ross archive](https://www.bus.umich.edu/FacultyResearch/InTheNews/Archive/ProFormaEarnings.htm) versus the RAS extract above
- The paper’s interpretation is mispricing, not a risk premium. Investors “do not fully appreciate the lower cash flow implications at the time of the earnings announcement,” and the hedge “persists after controlling for various risk factors and other anomalies.” — RAS abstract on [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=303563) and the extract
- Post-Regulation G, published return evidence that was actually readable is about a correction over subsequent months, not a three-year hedge. Sarah E. McVay, Edgar Rodriguez-Vazquez, and Sara Toynbee, “Experience with Non-GAAP Earnings and Investors’ Pricing of Exclusions,” *The Accounting Review* 99(3), 2024, 397–427, https://doi.org/10.2308/TAR-2021-0645. Abstract: experience is how often managers or analysts gave a non-GAAP number over the prior eight quarters. “Absent prior experience with non-GAAP earnings metrics, investors appear to overestimate the persistence of exclusions at the earnings announcement, which corrects in the following months.” No return percentage, portfolio spread, or t-statistic is in that abstract. The sign of the correction is the undoing of overestimated exclusion persistence; the abstract does not state whether the abnormal return is positive or negative in basis points. — [AAA citation / abstract](https://publications.aaahq.org/Citation/Download?resourceId=11828&resourceType=3&citationFormat=1); [ResearchGate abstract mirror](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374896848_Experience_with_Non-GAAP_Earnings_and_Investors'_Pricing_of_Exclusions)
- A closer analogue to Doyle, still not a journal article as of 8 October 2026: Kurt H. Gee, Ken Li, and Benjamin C. Whipple, “Investor Mispricing of Persistent Non-GAAP Exclusions,” SSRN 3825314, posted 13 April 2021, date written 8 October 2026. Abstract: “prior research has found little evidence” that non-GAAP reporting misleads investors after Regulation G; mispricing, if it exists, should be where firms exclude persistent items; they build a firm-quarter persistence measure and “find that investors misprice persistent exclusions”; the mispricing is larger when non-GAAP reporting changes and shows up for both sophisticated and unsophisticated investors; insiders “sell more shares after firms exclude more persistent items,” “consistent with insiders exploiting inflated market values.” No horizon in months, no hedge return, and no t-statistic is in the abstract. The implied sign is overpricing (inflated values, insider sales), i.e. subsequent negative returns for high persistent-exclusion firms, but that return was not read in a table. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3825314)
- Much of the post-Regulation G literature that surfaced in this search is about earnings persistence and announcement-window response, not a Doyle-style multi-year return. Examples read only at abstract level: Regulation G and later C&DIs made exclusions more transitory for future operating earnings and changed earnings-response coefficients (UTS thesis / “Market reaction to non-GAAP earnings around SEC regulation”); Whipple’s “other items” paper argues post-Reg G other exclusions are mostly informative rather than opportunistic; Bratten, Larocque, and Yohn study unexpected street exclusions and earnings-announcement returns, sample 94,071 firm-quarters from 2004 to 2016, not a post-announcement drift table. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1815566917300309); [Whipple SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2480663); local text of the Bratten–Larocque–Yohn draft at https://belkcollegeofbusiness.charlotte.edu/accountingseminar/wp-content/uploads/sites/1269/2020/02/Bratten_UnexpectedExclusions.pdf (published version is *Contemporary Accounting Research*, 2024)

### Inferences
- Use 29.9% over three years, size- and book-to-market-adjusted, low-other-exclusions minus high-other-exclusions, as the paper’s return result. The regression analogue is 31.2% after beta, size, book-to-market, PEAD, accruals, and momentum. The one-year hedge is only 4.4% and is the weak horizon. “Up to 45 percent” is a Ross communications sentence and should not be cited as the paper’s result.
- The cash-flow sentence to keep is the paper’s: other exclusions are strongly negative for future cash flow (about −$6.42 per dollar over three years in the introduction’s full-sample statement; −$4.17 in the nonzero-exclusion sample), while special items are “generally unrelated,” not “+$1.” The Ross “+$1 / −$3” line mixes or rounds a different contrast.
- Doyle is a drift / mispricing result on a pre-Regulation G sample (1988–1999). It is not evidence about recurring addbacks after Regulation G.
- After Regulation G, it is not true that the literature “only” shows earnings persistence. McVay, Rodriguez-Vazquez, and Toynbee (TAR 2024) explicitly put the correction in the months after the announcement, and Gee, Li, and Whipple (SSRN, revised October 2026) explicitly test mispricing of persistent exclusions. What is missing from the open record is a Doyle-sized, tabled, multi-year hedge with a t-statistic. The dominant post-2003 results that were readable are persistence and announcement ERCs.

## Hoberg and Phillips, which paper has the post-boom return

### Takeaway
The return result is in “Real and Financial Industry Booms and Busts,” *Journal of Finance* 2010, not in the product-market-synergies merger paper. The abnormal-return gap is a style-matched DGTW return, and the competition split is a fitted sales Herfindahl on three-digit SIC codes. It is not a text-based competition measure.

### Cited Findings
- The paper that states the result is Gerard Hoberg and Gordon Phillips, “Real and Financial Industry Booms and Busts,” *Journal of Finance* 65(1), February 2010, 45–86, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01523.x. Abstract: in competitive industries, high industry valuation, investment, and financing are followed by lower operating cash flows and abnormal stock returns; in concentrated industries the relations are weak. — [Wiley abstract](https://dx.doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01523.x); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1109124)
- The same sentences are in the downloaded NBER Working Paper 14290 (August 2008) and in a search-index extract of the published *Journal of Finance* PDF. Published-PDF extract: “a one standard deviation increase in relative industry valuation is associated with a 3% decline in operating cash flows, and a one standard deviation increase in industry financing is associated with a 5.5% decline in operating cash flows. In competitive growth industries, annual abnormal stock returns for an industry-level portfolio in the highest quintile of relative industry valuation are over 3 percentage points lower than those for a portfolio in the lowest quintile. If we weight by firm rather than by industry, this abnormal return difference exceeds 10% points.” — [NBER w14290 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14290/w14290.pdf); the Journal of Finance wording was returned from the indexed file previously hosted at https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/gordonph/intellcont/JF%202010-1.pdf (that URL failed on direct fetch)
- Return definition, from the NBER WP §E and the Table XI note (the published PDF was not opened locally, so this wording is the working-paper wording). A firm’s abnormal return is its raw monthly return minus the monthly return of a portfolio matched on NYSE/AMEX breakpoints of size, industry-adjusted book-to-market, and past-year return, as in Daniel, Grinblatt, Titman, and Wermers (1997). Industry abnormal returns are equal-weighted averages of those firm abnormal returns inside the three-digit SIC code. They also re-estimate with a Mitchell–Stafford alpha: each July–June year, regress the twelve monthly returns on the three Fama–French factors plus momentum, then subtract the average intercept of the firm’s size / book-to-market / momentum benchmark. The WP says the main results survive that adjustment. — NBER w14290 PDF, manuscript pp. 28–32
- Magnitudes in the WP, which line up with the published extract’s “over 3” and “exceeds 10” summary. Introduction: in competitive industries, one standard deviation of relative industry valuation lines up with a three percent decline in operating cash flows, and one standard deviation of industry financing with a 5.5 percent decline. In competitive growth industries, the industry-level high-minus-low valuation quintile is “over three percentage points” in annual abnormal return; firm-weighted, the difference “exceeds ten percentage points.” In concentrated industries the quintile pattern is non-monotonic and “less than half as large.” The Table XI prose is tighter than the summary: at the industry level in the competitive-growth panel, the top valuation quintile underperforms the bottom by over 3.3 percentage points a year; at the firm level the gap is roughly 4.2 percentage points in one panel and 15 percentage points in the competitive-growth panel. The conclusion repeats “over three percentage points” for industry-weighted returns and “more than ten percentage points” for firm-weighted returns. The “exceeds 10” claim is the firm-weighted competitive (especially competitive-growth) contrast, not every quintile spread. — same NBER PDF, manuscript pp. 2 and 28–33
- Competition is not text-based. Industries are three-digit SIC. Miscellaneous Census industries are dropped. Financials (SIC 6000–6999) and utilities (SIC 4900–4999) are dropped. The sample in the WP is 1972–2004, 108,522 firm-years. The Herfindahl is a fitted sales HHI: actual Commerce Department manufacturing HHIs (public and private firms) are regressed on the Compustat public-firm HHI (segment tapes from 1984), BLS employees per firm, Compustat employees per firm, and interactions; fitted values are applied to all industries. Lowest fitted-HHI tercile is competitive; highest is concentrated. Correlation of the fitted HHI with actual manufacturing HHIs is 54.2%, versus 34.1% for the Compustat segment HHI. They do not put the fitted HHI into the regression as a continuous regressor; they use it only to split terciles. — same NBER PDF, §III.A
- “Product Market Synergies and Competition in Mergers and Acquisitions” is the other Hoberg–Phillips paper from the same period. It is the text-based one (similarity of product descriptions). It is not the paper that reports the post-boom cash-flow and abnormal-return result. The boom-bust paper cites Hoberg–Phillips product-market language only as a later, separate literature. The 2010 *Journal of Finance* boom-bust article’s competition measure, in the working paper that became it, is the fitted SIC Herfindahl. — NBER w14290 data section; the synergies paper is not the source of the 3% / 5.5% / 10-point sentences quoted above

### Inferences
- Quote “Real and Financial Industry Booms and Busts,” *Journal of Finance* 2010. The existing claim (firm-weighted abnormal-return difference exceeds 10 percentage points; one standard deviation of relative industry valuation with a 3% cash-flow decline; financing with 5.5%) matches both the working paper and the published-PDF extract. The 10-point figure is firm-weighted, and the WP’s table discussion puts the really large gap (about 15 points) in competitive growth industries.
- The return is a characteristic-matched abnormal return (DGTW), then checked with a Fama–French-plus-momentum alpha. It is not a raw return and not a text-similarity return.
- The competition measure is not text-based. Using this paper as a filing-text feature would misstate the design. The text-based Hoberg–Phillips industry measures are a different project.
