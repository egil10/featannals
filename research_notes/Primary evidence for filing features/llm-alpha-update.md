## Which 2024–2026 papers report a long-short, alpha, or Sharpe from an LLM or transformer read of filings or earnings releases?

### Takeaway
No verified 2024–2026 paper found here reports a long-short, alpha, or Sharpe from an LLM reading of US 10-Ks, 10-Qs, or earnings releases. The one clean monthly alpha is Choi and coauthors on S&P 100 earnings calls, not filings. Shaffer and Wang read 10-Ks and stop at future earnings. Kim and Nikolaev’s contextualized profitability is a narrative-adjusted accounting factor, still an SSRN working paper, with no Sharpe in the abstract. A Chinese annual-report paper states a 1.06 percent monthly excess return and is not a US EDGAR result.

### Cited Findings
- Choi, Kim, Yu, Cha, Golkhou, Halperin, Papaioannou, M. Kim, Wang, Kwon, M. Kim, Lopez-Lira, and Lee, “From Text to Alpha,” arXiv:2510.03195, submitted 3 October 2025, current version v5 on 15 March 2026, not withdrawn. The design is “LLM as extractor, embedding as ruler”: Gemini-2.5-Pro extracts performance metrics from the full earnings-call transcript; text-embedding-3-large scores year-over-year metric change with maximum cosine similarity, then a piecewise map with thresholds α = 0.4 and β = 0.6. Those thresholds were “selected from a sweep at 0.2 intervals based on both predictive performance and interpretability.” — [arXiv HTML v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v5)
- Input text is earnings-call transcripts, not a 10-K, 10-Q, earnings release, or headline. Sample: S&P 100, January 2010–December 2024, 64 quarters, 5,615 firm-quarters, 16,675 return observations. The paper says it follows Cohen and Nguyen (2024) but restricts to the S&P 100 because of LLM cost. — [arXiv HTML v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v5)
- Portfolio: quintiles of the moving-targets score; the reported spread is Q5 minus Q1, high shifting minus low shifting. LLM method, monthly: excess return −0.41 percent (t = −2.08); three-factor alpha −0.48 percent (t = −2.40); five-factor alpha −0.52 percent (t = −2.55). NER baseline on the same calls: excess return −0.31 percent (t = −1.48); five-factor alpha −0.14 percent (t = −0.70), not significant. Fama–MacBeth regressions are of next-month returns. Controls named in the table note: log size, log book-to-market, return (−1, 0), return (−12, −1). The text does not say the spreads are net of costs. No Sharpe ratio is reported. The sections read do not test whether Gemini-2.5-Pro or the embedding model had already seen the calls or the later returns. — [arXiv HTML v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v5)
- Shaffer and Wang, HBS working paper “Harnessing Large Language Models for Core Earnings Measurement,” cited as October 2026. They apply LLMs to 10-K text of US public companies from 2000 to 2023. A one-shot prompt given only a definition and the full 10-K conflates core earnings with other concepts such as EBITDA. A sequential prompt (unusual losses, then unusual gains, then tabulate and aggregate) “outperforms GAAP Net Income and Compustat’s OPEPS and OIADP in predicting average future earnings in most standard tests.” The HBS abstract does not name the model and does not report a stock long-short, alpha, or Sharpe. — [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67100)

### Inferences
- Choi is the paper that actually scores year-over-year narrative change with an LLM and compares it with a simpler extractor. The simpler extractor is a named-entity baseline on the same calls, not Jaccard or cosine similarity of the whole document. High metric shifting earns lower next-month returns. The five-factor figure is a high-minus-low monthly alpha of −0.52 percent, so the long-low / short-high book is the one with the positive sign. It should not be copied onto MD&A.

- Shaffer and Wang strengthen the case that a procedural prompt can read a 10-K for core earnings better than a one-shot prompt. They do not add a stock anomaly. Borrowing the Rouen–So–Wang return for the LLM measure would mix two designs.
- Contextualized profitability is still the hybrid factor already on the shortlist: narrative is used to clean an accounting characteristic, not as a standalone text long-short. No new tradable magnitude was found. Journal publication was not confirmed.

- Anna Kovalchuk, OSF replication package “Semantic similarity of textual edits and the Lazy Prices effect” (osf.io/v9h56, updated 7 August 2026). No alpha, Sharpe, or basis-point figure is on the page. - A second OSF registration by the same contributor (osf.io/ta6jf, 19 July 2026) summarizes the package as the paper “Semantic vs. Lexical Textual Change: A Reversal of Lazy Prices,” while the description line still uses the “refine and confirm” title. No return number is on that page either. The two public descriptions disagree on the sign of the semantic increment. — [OSF ta6jf](https://osf.io/ta6jf/overview)
- Choi’s horse race is LLM extraction plus embedding cosine versus NER, on earnings calls, not versus full-document Jaccard or cosine, and not on 10-Ks. The LLM five-factor Q5–Q1 alpha is −0.52 percent (t = −2.55) against an insignificant NER five-factor alpha of −0.14 percent (t = −0.70). — [arXiv HTML v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v5)

### Inferences
- The shortlist’s statement that an LLM “what changed” score has not been shown to beat section-level Jaccard still holds for US filings. Choi shows that a semantic match of extracted metrics beats a keyword extractor on calls. That is a different race.
- Kovalchuk is a warning, not a result to trade. If the wiki version is right, semantic edits keep the lazy-prices sign and the lexical effect is unstable across subperiods. If the other title is right, semantics reverse the sign. Until one paper is readable, neither should change the feature rank. The subperiod claim (fade after 2015, gone in COVID, back from 2022) would qualify a “has not decayed” sentence, but it is not yet a citable alpha.

## How should a backtest avoid look-ahead when an LLM reads EDGAR?

### Takeaway
Sarkar and Vafa’s practical rule is a model whose pretraining corpus contains only language from before the decision date and was not selected with later information. Telling the model to ignore the future, or stripping the firm name and the date, does not establish that. Later critiques agree that anonymization is incomplete, and they locate the clean test after the model’s cutoff; they do not offer a prompt that makes a frozen modern LLM safe on old EDGAR text.

### Cited Findings
- Sarkar and Vafa, “Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models,” first version 11 March 2024, SSRN version dated 28 June 2024 and revised 18 October 2024. They write that prompting-based approaches have limitations, and: “The issues we raise can be addressed by using models whose pretraining data is free of survivorship bias and contains only language produced prior to the analysis period of interest.” — [SSRN 4754678](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4754678); [NBER conference PDF](https://users.nber.org/~confer/conf_papers/2055/f205530/f205530.pdf)
- On earnings calls from September–November 2019, with the model asked for 2020 risks, the sequence “COVID-19,” which did not exist at the time of the calls, appears in 6.8 percent of generations. An instruction not to use future information does not eliminate the leakage. Censoring the firm name does not either: the model predicts 70 percent of firm names from censored earnings-call segments, and the year inferred from a date-censored segment has correlation 0.79 with the actual year. The tests are earnings calls and candidate biographies, not a 10-K trading backtest. — [NBER conference PDF](https://users.nber.org/~confer/conf_papers/2055/f205530/f205530.pdf)
- Glasserman and Lin, arXiv:2309.17322 (29 September 2023), study GPT sentiment on news headlines, not filings. Inside the training window, anonymized headlines outperform named headlines, which they read as a distraction effect larger than look-ahead bias, especially for large firms. Their abstract still treats overlap between training and backtest as a bias, and they offer anonymization as a de-biasing device for headlines, not as proof that memory is gone. — [arXiv:2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)
- Wu, Yang, Ying, and Zhou, “Anonymization and Information Loss” (arXiv PDF 2511.15364, retrieved text dated in the file header as 2026). On S&P credit-downgrade prediction, raw text beats anonymized text, and they say look-ahead bias does not explain the gap: masking numbers and named agents removes information. They cite Glasserman and Lin and Sarkar and Vafa as the look-ahead warnings, and anonymization as a proposed remedy that has a cost. This is not a stock long-short. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15364)
- Gao, Jiang, and Yan, “A Test of Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts,” December 2025 conference draft. The tasks they name are news headlines predicting stock returns and earnings-call transcripts predicting capital expenditure. They describe masking, anonymizing, and date-stripping as prompting strategies that try to mitigate leakage, and point to models trained on a controlled information set as the alternative. The draft retrieved does not state a 10-K protocol. — [NBER conference PDF](https://users.nber.org/~confer/conf_papers/2372/f237283/f237283.rev0.pdf); listed as [arXiv:2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23847) by Look-Ahead-Bench
- Look-Ahead-Bench, arXiv:2601.13770, submitted 20 January 2026 (Mostapha Benhenda). It compares Llama 3.1 (8B and 70B) and DeepSeek 3.2 with point-in-time models and says standard LLMs show lookahead bias “as measured with alpha decay,” while the point-in-time models do not. The HTML introduction says alpha decay between in-window and later periods exceeds −15 percentage points. The benchmark is not a 10-K study. Model-by-model point estimates were not re-extracted from the tables in this pass. — [arXiv:2601.13770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13770); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13770v1)
- Li, Wang, and Ma, EMNLP 2026, propose an inference-time decoder (FinCAD) to dampen memorized outcomes. Across five 7–14B models and five mega-cap stocks, the largest model-level mean in-sample return correction is −67.1 percent. For the three larger models, 2025 out-of-sample results stay within $8,000 and mean Sharpe within ±0.10 of the uncorrected baseline. This is a mitigation experiment on mega-cap return paths, not an EDGAR filing backtest, and it does not replace a cutoff-dated model. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.510/); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24564v2)

### Inferences
- A usable EDGAR rule, stacked from these sources and no stricter than they are: freeze a model whose corpus ends before the filing’s acceptance time; do not fill the earlier years with a 2024–2026 model and then drop the firm name; do not treat “ignore events after date D” as a fix; judge the strategy on the period after the cutoff, or on a model retrained only on text from before each decision. Survivorship in the pretraining corpus is part of Sarkar and Vafa’s condition, not an optional extra.
- Anonymization cuts both ways. Sarkar and Vafa show the model can recover the firm and the year. Glasserman and Lin show that, on headlines, removing the name can raise the in-sample return by killing distraction rather than by proving there is no memory. Wu, Yang, Ying, and Zhou show that the same mask can delete the signal. None of that is a 10-K recipe that salvages a memorized backtest.
- The withdrawn financial-statement paper’s own abstract said the statements were anonymous and that the predictions did not come from training memory. That claim is not usable: the paper was withdrawn for replication inconsistencies, and the inputs were numeric statements rather than narrative filings. Anonymization did not turn that paper into evidence.

### Gaps
- The exact in-window alpha decays for Llama 3.1 70B and DeepSeek in Look-Ahead-Bench were not re-read. Only the abstract’s “alpha decay” and the HTML’s “exceeding −15 percentage points” are used.
- Gao, Jiang, and Yan’s numerical result for how much of an in-sample headline effect their test attributes to leakage, including any 2024 out-of-sample zero, was not re-extracted from the PDF.
- No source retrieved states an EDGAR acceptance-timestamp rule, a 16:00 Eastern cutoff, or a filing-specific walk-forward design. Those operational details remain outside this note.

## What should move on the existing shortlist?

### Takeaway
Do not add an LLM filing long-short, and do not promote year-over-year language change, walked-back KPIs, contextualized profitability, or core-earnings extraction on the basis of this pass. ### Inferences
- Leave feature 1 in Tier A. Do not replace Jaccard with an LLM redline. Kovalchuk’s OSF package is too internally inconsistent, and too unpublished, to demote the post-2015 Research Affiliates claim or to promote embeddings over lexical overlap.
