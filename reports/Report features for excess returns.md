Future excess returns that can be read out of a 10-K or 10-Q are mostly a slow reaction to what changed, not a tone score and not a chatbot’s opinion of the business. The one result that is both a clean post-filing drift and a document an investor could have read is year-over-year language change: firms that rewrite the filing subsequently underperform firms that do not, there is no filing-day jump, and the number a liquid book can defend is a few percent a year, not the Journal of Finance abstract’s ceiling of 22 percent. A minority of other features also pay after the document is public, and they are different bets: narrative debt constraints and Item 1A cybersecurity exposure are priced premia, while material-weakness conclusions, recurring non-GAAP exclusions, and the change in MD&A tone are delayed reactions with narrower, older, or unquantified evidence. Large language models add disambiguation and structured extraction — a reworded metric, a recurring cash addback, a physical risk rather than a transition platitude — and they have not added a replicated long-short on the filing itself. The two famous ChatGPT-and-disclosure papers from Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev were withdrawn in 2025; the third scores earnings calls, not 10-Ks. Ignore Harvard word lists, Fog, gross file size, filing-day trades, moat language, green-word counts, and any backtest that lets a pretrained model remember the year.

## What survives after the filing is public

**Drift, a premium, and a short-window reaction are three different objects, and most famous filing-text results are the third.** Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen’s lazy-prices portfolio is the drift: short firms that change 10-K and 10-Q language, long firms that do not, with no announcement effect, and with the return arriving later through news, earnings, or bankruptcy ([Journal of Finance abstract via HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)). The abstract’s **“up to 188 basis points per month (over 22 percent per year)”** is a ceiling, not a base-case sort. Alpha Architect’s 2016 restatement of the working paper puts the broad long-short at a statistically significant **30–60 basis points per month**, “up to 7.6 percent over the following year,” and assigns the **188 basis-point** five-factor alpha (**t = 2.76**) to the risk-factor slice ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/lazy-prices/)). A 2026 secondary write-up repeats a **30–58 basis-point** value-weighted band for the broad sort ([Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)). The internet-appendix cells that were actually read are neither of those headlines. On risk-factor text only, post-Sarbanes-Oxley 2003–2014, equal-weighted Jaccard’s excess intercept is **1.11 percent a month (t = 3.15)** and value-weighted cosine is **0.91 percent (t = 2.39)**; the five-factor model in that table is market, size, value, momentum, and liquidity, not profitability and investment ([NBER appendix](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w25084/w25084.appendix.pdf)). Research Affiliates then re-ran Jaccard on the **1,000 largest** US stocks from April 1995 through September 2025 and got about **2.9 percent a year** (big changers **−1.5 percent**, steady language **+1.4 percent**), with four-factor annualized alphas of **−1.49 percent (t = −2.29)** and **+1.76 percent (t = 2.59)**. Their out-of-sample window, October 2015–September 2025, is **wider**, not narrower (**−2.4 percent** versus **+1.7 percent**). Annual rebalance compresses the spread to about **−1.2 percent** versus **+1.1 percent**. Inside already-risky cohorts the high-versus-low similarity gap is **3.5–5.3 percent a year**; cheap-and-stable returned **12.3 percent** versus **8.5 percent** for cheap-and-rewritten ([Research Affiliates](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf)). Do not average 188 basis points with 2.9 percent. The first is the top academic slice in an older, broader sample. The second is what one large-cap, cap-weighted, post-publication test still finds, and it has not decayed. A fundamental PM’s claim that quants have already compressed recurring filing patterns sits unresolved next to that wider 2015–2025 gap ([Caughran](https://x.com/FundamentEdge/status/1914694781462343979)).

**The cybersecurity result is a premium, not a slow reading of Item 1A.** Florackis, Louca, Michaely, and Weber build a 10-K Item 1A score from language that distinguishes hacked firms, and high-exposure portfolios outperform: equal-weighted long-short excess return of **66–69 basis points per month**, stated as **8.3 percent a year**, value-weighted **7.9 percent**, with Fama–MacBeth predictability out to **12 months**. High-score firms do badly when cybersecurity concern rises, which is the authors’ risk-premium signature ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28196); [RFS via EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.)). The retrieved pages do not say whether that spread survives profitability and investment, so it is not a five-factor alpha until the table is re-opened. A different construct, Celeny and Maréchal’s doc2vec proximity of the **whole** 10-K to MITRE ATT&CK descriptions, is the one whose own split shows decay: the long-short averaged **11.88 percent a year** before the November 2020 public mention of a cyber-risk premium and about **6.93 percent** over the full 2007–2022 window ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/cyber-risk-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)). Those are not two estimates of one number.

**Loughran–McDonald negative tone is a four-day filing reaction, and a fragile one.** Higher financial-negative word share lines up with lower excess returns over days **[0, 3]** (t = 2.64 with size, book-to-market, turnover, and industry). On days **[0, 1]** none of the proportional measures are significant. The paper does not report a 3-, 6-, or 12-month long-short, and the basis-point coefficient was not recoverable from the text layer ([Loughran and McDonald 2011](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)). A 15-page 2019 student thesis finds the four-day pattern in 1994–2008 and reports that it disappears in 2008–2018. That is a weak decay flag, not a journal replication ([Aalto thesis](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/253e57c2-b522-4078-82cb-4524ac8c3d2e/content)). Across 97 published predictors generally, portfolio returns are about **26 percent** lower out of sample and **58 percent** lower after publication ([McLean and Pontiff](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12365)). Firms already write around machine-negative lists: mechanical downloads of 10-Ks and 10-Qs rose from **360,861** in 2003 to around **165 million** in 2016, and documents became easier for machines and less negative on algorithm word lists ([Cao, Jiang, Yang, and Zhang](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27950)).

**What an LLM adds, on the evidence that is still standing, is a better measurement of a change or a residual, not a new alpha.** FinBERT’s **88.2 percent** sentiment accuracy versus **62.1 percent** for the Loughran–McDonald dictionary is on labeled analyst-report sentences, and the paper does not report a 10-K long-short ([Huang, Wang, and Yang](https://doi.org/10.1111/1911-3846.12832)). Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer’s price-supervised lists raise earnings-call announcement R² from **2.1 percent** (Loughran–McDonald terms that do not overlap) to **4.6 percent**; that is a short-window fit, and the supervisor is the price reaction ([Journal of Financial Economics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X22002422)). Shaffer and Wang’s sequential prompt on the full 10-K predicts future earnings better than GAAP net income; the one-shot prompt drifts toward EBITDA; no stock long-short is reported ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67100)). Choi and coauthors’ embedding of LLM-extracted metrics on earnings calls, not 10-Ks, reports a five-factor spread of **−0.52 percent (t = −2.55)** against an insignificant named-entity baseline. The retrieved pages do not show whether that figure is high-minus-low or low-minus-high, so the sign should not be flipped, and the transfer to MD&A is untested ([arXiv:2510.03195](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03195v4)). The tasks a dictionary cannot do — inferring a risk the document does not name, judging a moat, declaring guidance credible — are also the tasks that leak the future. Prompted with September–November 2019 calls, Llama 2-70B emits “COVID-19,” a string that did not exist yet, in **6.8 percent** of generations ([Sarkar and Vafa](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4754678)).

**Two Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev papers are not evidence.** “Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models” ([arXiv:2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)) was withdrawn by Valeri Nikolaev on **20 February 2025** after a co-author found inconsistencies while trying to replicate. The input was anonymized balance sheets and income statements, not Item 1, Item 1A, or MD&A. Do not cite the chain-of-thought accuracy, the equal-weighted monthly Sharpe of **3.36**, or the **84** and **37** basis-point six-factor alphas. A practitioner pass that saw only the abstract page did not find the reason printed there; the withdrawal record quoted in the LLM-era note does state it. “Bloated Disclosures” ([arXiv:2306.10224](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10224)) was withdrawn on **9 October 2025** because a co-author’s replication did not support the findings. Do not cite any magnitude from it. “From Transcripts to Insights” ([arXiv:2310.17721](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.17721)) was not withdrawn. It scores earnings-call transcripts from January 2018–March 2023. Equal-weighted quintile five-factor alphas are **5.28 percent** for political risk (t = 1.51, not significant), **6.72 percent** for climate (t = 1.90), and **6.36 percent** for AI risk (t = 2.31). A Booth brief’s “around 5 percent” is coarser than those three numbers and should not replace them. The asset-pricing sample pools the model’s training window with later calls. None of this is a 10-K result. Headline drift is the wrong base case as well: Lopez-Lira and Tang, in the Journal of Financial Economics in 2026, score news, call the initial reaction about **90 percent** accurate and non-tradable, and find a subsequent drift of one to two days that has weakened as the tool spread ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X26001066)). Secondary daily Sharpes near 3 are a reading of tables, not a filing factor ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/chatgpt-forecast-stock-price/)).

The thirty features below are ranked for future excess returns after the text is public. **Tier A** has a published post-document drift or a priced premium; the label says which. **Tier B** has a real result whose future-return evidence is short, old, decayed, unquantified, or tangled up with an accounting number. **Tier C** is what serious sources tell an LLM to extract, with no verified tradable return. Seven, fifteen, and eight. That split is the point.

## Seven features clear a post-filing return test

**1. Year-over-year section change. Tier A, drift.** Score how much this 10-K or 10-Q differs from the firm’s own prior filing, by section, and rank that change against other filers. Prefer the edit over the level of pessimism. **Read** Item 7 MD&A, where the edits concentrate, and the slices the abstracts flag as especially informative: Item 1A risk factors, litigation and Item 3, and language about the CEO and CFO. On the 10-Q, use Item 2 and the updated risk factors. Peer-check the score: Citigroup’s 2006-to-2007 Jaccard fell from **0.72 to 0.50** while JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and Bank of America edged up, so the signal was not “banks” ([Research Affiliates](https://loved-novelty-f15595edb6.media.strapiapp.com/1117_nlp_for_long_horizon_factor_investing_d39e26f476.pdf)). **Sign:** high similarity, higher subsequent returns. Long non-changers, short changers. Positive-language similarity is the exception and is feature 10, not a reason to flip this one. **Horizon:** no filing-day return. The appendix design enters in the month after the filing is public and holds three months ([NBER appendix](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w25084/w25084.appendix.pdf)). A secondary write-up describes drift over **6–18 months** with no reversal ([Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)). Research Affiliates find the long-short gap still widening at **12 months**, and a quarterly rebalance beating an annual one. **Magnitude:** disputed, and the dispute is the result. Abstract ceiling **up to 188 basis points per month**. Broad sort in the 2016 working-paper restatement **30–60 basis points per month**. Appendix risk-factor cells about **0.4 to 1.2 percent a month**, not the 188 cell, and not a profitability-and-investment factor model. Research Affiliates, top 1,000, through September 2025: about **2.9 percent a year**, stronger out of sample, and **3.5–5.3 percent** only inside speculative cohorts. **Source:** Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen, Journal of Finance, 2020 ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=57331); [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12885)). **Caveat:** an LLM “what changed” score that correlates with Jaccard is this anomaly until a horse race says otherwise. No such horse race was found. The signal did not flag Enron, WorldCom, or SVB, because fraud can keep the boilerplate stable and a liquidity crisis can open between filings. There is no post-2020 journal replication; the Research Affiliates test is one firm’s backtest, and it is the out-of-sample number to put next to 22 percent rather than instead of a careful reading of the Journal of Finance tables.

**2. Narrative debt-market constraints. Tier A, priced premium.** A filing feature that says the firm is shut out of debt markets, equity markets, or external finance generally, scored as narrative rather than as a list of constraining words. **Read** the annual report’s liquidity, capital-resources, and debt discussion, not a generic negative-word rate. **Sign:** constrained firms earn higher average returns. The authors treat this as compensation for financial risk, not as slow reading. Debt constraints are the strongest of the three. The sign is the opposite of “gloomy language, low returns.” **Horizon:** a characteristic portfolio. Sample start and end dates were not extracted. **Magnitude:** annualized risk-adjusted excess return of **6.5 percent** (debt), **3.7 percent** (general external finance), and **3.0 percent** (equity). The authors’ summary also reports a **7.2 percent** annualized alpha on the debt measure inside the top market-capitalization percentile. The spread is not explained by the Fama–French five-factor model and is largest among large, liquid stocks. Momentum is not named among the factors they partial out ([Buehlmaier and Whited](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/31/7/2693/4824924); [Harvard Law summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/03/10/are-financial-constraints-priced-evidence-from-textual-analysis/)). **Caveat:** Bodnaruk, Loughran, and McDonald’s constraining-word frequency predicts dividend omissions, missed dividend increases, equity recycling, and underfunded pensions. It does not have this alpha ([manuscript](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/wbs/subjects/finance/events/seminars/using_10-k_text_to_gauge_financial_constraints.pdf)). Hoberg and Maksimovic’s text constraints predict investment cuts, not a return premium in the abstract. Ask whether the constraint is new, and do not fold it into a sentiment score.

**3. Item 1A cybersecurity exposure. Tier A, priced premium.** A firm-level cyber-risk score from Item 1A language that matches firms later hit by attacks, not a count of the phrase “cybersecurity.” **Read** Item 1A. Do not pool Item 1C, the governance disclosure required from December 2023, which has no return evidence in the sources retrieved. **Sign:** high exposure, higher average returns, and poor returns when cybersecurity attention rises. That state dependence is why this is a premium rather than underreaction. **Horizon:** up to **12 months** in Fama–MacBeth tests. The textual comparison described by the authors’ university summary uses 10-Ks from **2007 to 2018**. **Magnitude:** equal-weighted long-short excess return **66–69 basis points per month**, **8.3 percent a year** equal-weighted and **7.9 percent** value-weighted ([NBER](http://dx.doi.org/10.3386/w28196)). **Source:** Florackis, Louca, Michaely, and Weber, Review of Financial Studies, 2023 ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:1:p:351-407.)). **Caveat:** the factor list behind those returns was not in the retrieved pages. No post-2022 replication was found. The score can still load on boilerplate volume. It is not feature 14.

**4. A concluded material weakness. Tier A, drift.** An actual SOX 302 weakness conclusion, separating company-level from account-specific weaknesses when the text allows it. **Read** 10-Q Item 4 and 10-K Item 9A, not the earnings press release. **Sign:** negative subsequent returns. The announcement reaction is negligible. **Horizon:** the next two quarters. **Magnitude:** buy-and-hold abnormal returns of about **3 percent over 60 trading days** and **6 percent over 120 trading days**; the gap versus effective-control firms is about **5.3–6.2 percent** (about **11–12 percent** annualized). The same summary also describes roughly **10–16 percent** annualized underperformance. Both wordings are in the source; they are not one reconciled number. The drift survives risk models, post-earnings-announcement drift, accruals, momentum, distress, and penny stocks, and it is confined to low institutional ownership. The authors’ persistence fact is a **79 percent** conditional probability of another weakness versus a **6 percent** unconditional probability ([Ghosh and coauthors, 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jbfa.70012)). **Caveat:** the company-level versus account-specific split is an accruals-quality result from Doyle, Ge, and McVay, not a split shown in the drift snippet ([archived PDF](https://web.archive.org/web/20100611220053if_/http:/www.business.utah.edu/humis/docs/person_1373_1193775147.pdf)). Do not borrow this drift for restatements, auditor changes, or Item 4.02 non-reliance. No holding-period magnitude for those was re-extracted.

**5. Recurring costs labeled non-recurring. Tier A, drift.** The amount and type of “other exclusions” in the non-GAAP reconciliation — stock-based compensation, transformation costs, acquisition retention, recurring cash costs — as distinct from special items such as a restructuring. **Read** the earnings release furnished under 8-K Item 2.02, Exhibit 99.1, not a keyword count of “adjusted EBITDA.” **Sign:** higher other exclusions, lower subsequent returns. Investors price some of this at the announcement and not enough of it. Special items predict about one more dollar of future cash flow per dollar excluded; other exclusions predict about **three fewer dollars** over three years. **Horizon:** the years after the announcement. **Magnitude:** the authors’ school summarizes stock returns over the **three years** after the announcement as **up to 45 percent** lower for relatively large exclusions than for small ones. Sample era **1988–1999**, before Regulation G ([Michigan Ross](https://www.bus.umich.edu/FacultyResearch/InTheNews/Archive/ProFormaEarnings.htm); [RAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v8y2003i2d10.1023_a1024472210359.html)). **Caveat:** “up to” is the school summary. A follow-on valuation paper says the pricing error is sometimes the opposite sign of a simple underreaction story ([discussion PDF](https://scispace.com/pdf/implications-of-components-of-income-excluded-from-pro-forma-46n07qn1nu.pdf)). A 2025 Journal of Accounting and Economics paper does not reconfirm a negative return to exclusions; its design is investment performance of consistent non-GAAP reporters, not a replication of the hedge ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410124000909)). No post-Regulation G exclusion hedge was found. The published object is the classified amount. Adjectives around the table were not shown to predict returns after that amount.

**6. MD&A tone change versus the prior periodic filing. Tier A, drift.** The change in positive-versus-negative tone of the MD&A relative to the previous 10-Q or 10-K. Not the level of tone, and not the full document. **Read** Item 7 and 10-Q Item 2. **Sign:** the tone change is associated with the short-window filing return after accruals and earnings surprises, and with an incremental drift beyond those controls. Part of the drift is the next earnings surprise, and not all of it. The incremental information is stronger when the firm’s information environment is weaker. **Horizon:** from two days after the SEC filing through one day after the next quarter’s preliminary earnings announcement. **Magnitude:** no verified portfolio return in percent or basis points. A page quoting roughly 0.20, −0.31, and 0.37 percent looked machine-summarized and is not used. **Source:** Feldman, Govindaraj, Livnat, and Segal, Review of Accounting Studies, 2010 ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v15y2010i4d10.1007_s11142-009-9111-x.html)). **Caveat:** no size, value, momentum, profitability, or investment test is in the abstract. This is easy to double-count with the earnings-release tone in feature 8. Score the change from the prior filing, not a second copy of the press release.

**7. Text-based competition after an industry boom. Tier A, conditional mispricing.** Competitive industries, identified from 10-K product and competition text, that have just combined high industry valuation, investment, and new financing. **Read** Item 1 product descriptions and competition language, then interact with the industry’s valuation and financing, not with a moat adjective. **Sign:** lower subsequent abnormal stock returns and lower operating cash flows. In concentrated industries the relations are weak. A static short of “many competition words,” or a long of “economic moat,” is not the result. **Horizon:** the abnormal returns that follow the boom. **Magnitude:** a one-standard-deviation increase in relative industry valuation lines up with a **3 percent** decline in operating cash flows, and a one-standard-deviation increase in industry financing with a **5.5 percent** decline. In competitive growth industries, the highest quintile of relative industry valuation has annual abnormal stock returns **over 3 percentage points** lower than the lowest at industry level; firm-weighted, the abnormal-return difference **exceeds 10 percentage points** ([Hoberg and Phillips, Journal of Finance, 2010](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/gordonph/intellcont/JF%202010-1.pdf)). **Caveat:** Li, Lundholm, and Minnis show that more competition references predict faster decay of profitability, and the published abstract does not state a stock-return result ([JAR](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2012.00472.x)). Product uniqueness raises valuation and, in a 2012 working paper, does not reverse in subsequent returns ([Hoberg and Phillips](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/gordonph/intellcont/hoberg_phillips_The_stock_market_and_product_uniqueness-1.pdf)). Fluidity predicts lower payouts and higher cash, not a premium in the Journal of Finance 2014 paper retrieved here. The boom result predates profitability and investment as the maintained factor model.

## Fifteen more are real, and easy to overtrade

**8. Abnormal positive tone in the earnings release. Tier B, reversal over one to two quarters.** Positive tone in the earnings press release residualized on quantitative performance, risk, and complexity. **Read** the 8-K Item 2.02 release. Applying the same residual to a 10-K is an extrapolation, not a published result. **Sign:** positive at the announcement, negative over the next one and two quarters. That is the opposite of post-earnings-announcement drift. The residual predicts negative future earnings and cash flows and lines up with meet-or-beat, later restatements, seasoned equity offerings, and mergers. **Horizon:** one to two quarters. **Magnitude:** no verified basis-point figure. **Source:** Huang, Teoh, and Zhang, The Accounting Review, 2014 ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1960376)). **Caveat:** raw optimism that matches the numbers is a different variable. Balance-sheet-constrained and older firms use tone management more relative to accruals management. A same-day FinBERT result on press releases raises R² from **3.3 percent** to **4.4 percent** and is not this drift ([arXiv:2509.24254](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.24254v1)).

**9. Year-over-year change in order backlog. Tier B, a number the narrative only backfills.** The change in backlog, not the level. **Read** Item 1 and the MD&A when the Compustat tag is missing. The tests used the number. **Sign:** the market underweights changes. Long high change, short low change. Analysts are overoptimistic when backlog falls. The level has the opposite error: the market overweights backlog’s contribution to future earnings, and a hedge on the level earns significant future abnormal returns with no magnitude in the abstract. **Horizon:** the year after formation for the change hedge. **Magnitude:** **13.7 percent** in the following year, on a sample described as **32,738** firm-years, **1971–1999**. Authors were not named on the pages read ([backlog-change PDF](https://scispace.com/pdf/changes-in-order-backlog-and-future-returns-8l5w7mlk5r.pdf); level result, [Rajgopal, Shevlin, and Venkatachalam](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1027364031775)). **Caveat:** this is not cleanly separated from a known accounting item. Do not infer a remaining-performance-obligation drift from it. No academic post-disclosure abnormal-return magnitude for RPO or current RPO was found, and RPO excludes cancellable periods and many short contracts.

**10. Similarity of positive language only. Tier B, the sign flip.** Year-over-year similarity computed on positive wording, not on the whole filing and not on uncertainty language. **Read** the same sections as feature 1, but score only the positive-language overlap. Brain’s change block is the dataset this test actually traded. **Sign:** opposite of lazy prices. Low similarity outperforms. In the same project, uncertainty-language similarity kept the Cohen sign. **Horizon:** one-month hold, February 2007–May 2020, roughly the largest 1,000 names. **Magnitude:** bottom-minus-top **5.47 percent** a year, volatility **6.48 percent**, return-to-volatility **0.84**, maximum drawdown about **−10 percent**. Bottom decile annualized return **14.58 percent** versus **9.11 percent** in the top. The author says standard models and the change in filing sentiment do not explain it ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)). **Caveat:** a blog backtest, not a journal paper, and it stops in May 2020. Research Affiliates’ overall Jaccard survived after 2014; that does not say this flip did. The post’s opening sentence states the sign backwards relative to its own table.

**11. Going-concern language. Tier B, large, unstable, and often a lottery stock.** The auditor’s going-concern opinion and management’s own “substantial doubt” or inability-to-continue sentence. **Read** the Item 8 auditor’s report and the MD&A liquidity discussion. **Sign:** negative subsequent performance where a negative result survives at all. It is not a uniform distress premium, and it is not the filing-date dip on the phrase “substantial doubt,” which is a short-window fact only ([Loughran and McDonald](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1510188)). **Horizon:** about twelve months in the UK study and in the 2026 US lottery paper. **Magnitude:** United Kingdom, **24 to 31 percent** underperformance in the year after disclosure, depending on the benchmark, raw returns about **−17 percent** in a rising market ([Taffler, Lu, and Kausar](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410104000813)). A US and Australia study finds no significant negative abnormal returns in Australia, and in the US the negative returns disappear under factor models or after momentum ([JAE 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410107000250)). A 2026 paper: five-factor twelve-month post-announcement abnormal return of **−10.6 percent** for the full US going-concern sample, **−25.8 percent** for lottery-like names, and a non-significant **−4.8 percent** for the rest. Lottery-like names underperform other going-concern stocks by up to **−21 percent**. It cites first-time reports at about **−14 percent** over twelve months. Transaction costs are noted as a limit ([ScienceDirect 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386418125000515)). **Caveat:** do not export the UK number. MD&A going-concern sentences predict bankruptcy incremental to ratios, the market, and the auditor’s opinion, including three years out, but that paper is fundamentals only. Fewer than **40 percent** of bankrupt firms discuss going concern in the MD&A, and only about **40–50 percent** receive the auditor’s opinion ([Mayew, Sethuraman, and Venkatachalam](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2272463)).

**12. Customer-concentration change, kept separate from the level. Tier B, the signs disagree.** The change in how concentrated the customer base is, versus the fact of a major customer above **10 percent** of revenue. **Read** Item 1 (Regulation S-K Item 101) and the ASC 280 segment footnote. **Sign:** increases predict positive one-year-ahead returns in Patatoukas, with a disproportionate share of the effect around later earnings announcements. The level points the other way in a University of Houston working paper: firms that disclose a major customer have lower excess returns after standard factors, read as lower systematic risk, on sorts from January 1985 through December 2022 ([UH PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/yerramilli/CustomerConcentration.pdf)). A Journal of Accounting and Economics paper finds the opposite cost-of-capital sign: major-customer dependence is associated with a cost of equity **21.2 basis points** higher, and government customers lower the cost of equity while corporate customers raise it ([JAE 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410115000336)). **Horizon:** one year for the change. **Magnitude:** the change regression coefficient is about **0.10** with size and other controls. Do not turn that coefficient into a portfolio return. The **21.2 basis points** are an implied cost, not a realized alpha. Cohen and Frazzini’s supplier-return delay was not re-read; no magnitude is stated. **Caveat:** these cannot all be a positive alpha. The prose adds something only when it names a relationship the customer file misses. Corporate concentration is also tied to crash risk, which is a tail outcome. A supplier’s return after the customer’s news is timed off the customer, not off the supplier’s next 10-K.

**13. Pension-footnote funded status and severe underfunding. Tier B, partly distress.** The off-balance-sheet piece of funded status and the projected benefit obligation, and a severe underfunding flag. Not pension adjectives. **Read** the ASC 715 footnote. **Sign:** in the original US result, severely underfunded sponsors earn lower subsequent returns, and prices do not reflect footnote changes at the 10-K. The recognized, on-balance-sheet piece does not predict. **Horizon:** catch-up through later quarterly earnings for footnote changes; at least five years after underfunding appears in the original return result. **Magnitude:** none in the Picconi abstract ([The Accounting Review, 2006](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/81/4/925/2885/The-Perils-of-Pensions-Does-Pension-Accounting)). A later description quotes a monthly four-factor alpha of **43 basis points (5.16 percent a year)** over the succeeding five years. Treat **43 basis points as secondary**. The same later paper finds the predictability concentrated in distressed sponsors, gone once sponsor distress is controlled, and faded when underfunding comes from bad pension-asset returns or has lasted more than five years ([Franzoni and Marín](https://ideas.repec.org/p/upf/upfgen/871.html); [later critique](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1059056016301174)). A dynamic strategy described at **1.51 percent a month** for 1989–2004 is not the baseline sort. German evidence has the opposite sign. **Caveat:** downgrade from hidden-liability alpha to a distress mix. After SFAS 158 much of the off-balance-sheet gap moved onto the balance sheet. An LLM adds a return only if it captures a database lag or assumption language the papers did not test. Do not port the US expected-return-on-assets assumption into IFRS, where IAS 19 does not run it through earnings the same way.

**14. Whole-document cyber embedding. Tier B, a premium that shrank once it was written down.** Doc2vec proximity of the entire 10-K, not the cyber section alone, to MITRE ATT&CK descriptions, averaged over the top 1 percent of paragraph similarities. **Read** the full filing, which is a different object from feature 3. **Sign:** long high cyber-risk text. **Horizon:** monthly. Sample January 2007–December 2022, more than 7,000 US listings. **Magnitude:** high-quantile excess return **18.72 percent a year**. Long-short average monthly excess **0.56 percent**, about **6.93 percent a year**, still significant against five factors in their tests. Before the November 2020 SSRN mention of a cyber-risk premium, the long-short averaged **11.88 percent a year**. Monthly excess returns rise from **0.88 percent** (low) to **1.44 percent** (high). Dictionary counts do worse ([Quantpedia, restating Celeny and Maréchal, SSRN 4587993](https://quantpedia.com/cyber-risk-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)). **Caveat:** journal publication was not confirmed. The decay split belongs to this paper, not to a replication that killed Florackis. Do not blend 8.3 percent with 6.93 percent.

**15. Physical climate risk in Item 1A, split from transition language. Tier B, a working-paper premium.** Item 1A sentences classified as physical climate risk, kept separate from transitional risk and from opportunity language. **Read** Item 1A, with a classifier in the spirit of the Kölbel BERT labels Tran and coauthors use, not a count of climate words. **Sign:** physical risk, higher average returns in that paper; no consistent transitional-risk premium. **Horizon:** an annual premium. **Magnitude:** **1.5–2.9 percent a year**, not attributed by the authors to other known risk factors or to industry. Working paper dated 24 May 2023, not a journal confirmation retrieved here ([author PDF](https://scholar.googleusercontent.com/scholar?q=cache:wjQ-azAhDYsJ:scholar.google.com/&hl=th&as_sdt=0,5)). **Caveat:** call-based climate exposure has an insignificant unconditional realized premium and expected-return proxies of at most about **0.5–1 percent a year**, lower after 2015 ([Sautner, van Lent, Vilkov, and Zhang](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3792366)). Berkman, Jona, and Soderstrom’s 10-K climate score explains valuation and loses money when climate concerns rise; it is a hedge, not a positive alpha ([AOS 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361368224000072)). The carbon premium is emissions, not text, and later work argues it fails for intensities, disclosed rather than estimated emissions, and properly lagged data ([Investment Officer, summarizing Markwat, Hanauer, and Swinkels](https://cms.investmentofficer.com/en/columns/gertjan-verdickt/carbon-premium-never-existed)). Green stocks’ high past returns are, in Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor, a surprise in climate concerns, so expected green returns are lower ([Journal of Financial Economics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X22001672)). A Li, Shan, Tang, and Yao 10-K climate dictionary was not found and is not cited.

**16. Aggregate manager sentiment. Tier B, a one-month contrarian market factor, not a firm’s own 10-K.** A monthly index of tone pooled across 10-Ks, 10-Qs, and conference calls. **Read** the corpus as a market state. Do not buy the stock whose own filing is negative and call it this factor. **Sign:** higher aggregate optimism, lower future market excess returns, and lower cross-sectional returns especially where valuation is hard and arbitrage is costly. **Horizon:** one month. **Magnitude:** a one-standard-deviation increase is associated with a **−1.26 percent** change in the expected excess market return the next month. In-sample R² **9.75 percent**. Out-of-sample R² **8.38 percent** over January 2007–December 2014 ([Jiang, Lee, Martin, and Zhou](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302770)). **Caveat:** calls are inside the published index. A pure filing version was not tested. A full size, value, momentum, profitability, and investment sort of the cross-section was not extracted.

**17. A disclosed covenant violation. Tier B, subsequent performance is positive, and the magnitude was not extracted.** The firm reports a financial-covenant breach. Compustat does not flag this cleanly. **Read** the debt footnote and the liquidity discussion. **Sign:** positive subsequent operating and stock-price performance, not a distress short. The mechanism in the papers is creditor intervention: cuts to acquisitions, capital spending, leverage, and payouts, higher CEO turnover, and, in a related paper, new capital-spending restrictions lining up with higher market value and ROA. About **10 to 20 percent** of US nonfinancial firms reported a violation in a given year over 1996–2008. **Horizon:** the period after the violation. **Magnitude:** no verified stock-return figure. **Source:** Nini, Sufi, and Smith ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1344302); [JFE 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X09000233)). **Caveat:** this is not underreaction to bad prose. A constraining-word list is feature 2’s cousin and does not carry this sign.

**18. Profitability with the narrative context left on. Tier B, a hybrid factor, not a text long-short.** A transformer-style model uses the filing narrative to separate persistent profit from transitory profit and replaces ordinary operating profitability with that contextualized version. **Read** the language around operating results, not a free-form quality score. **Sign:** the authors say the context-adjusted factor outperforms conventional profitability and removes the alpha on small extreme-growth portfolios, described as the largest failure of the five-factor model. **Horizon:** a cross-sectional factor. **Magnitude:** in the June 2023 draft, the three-factor alpha on small high-growth stocks is **−0.51**; adding profitability and investment cuts it to **−0.29**; replacing profitability with the context-adjusted factor cuts it to **−0.13** and the alpha loses significance. The draft does not label those units. Do not call them percent per month. **Source:** Kim and Nikolaev, “Contextualizing Profitability” ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4459383); [BFI working paper](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/BFI_WP_2023-76.pdf)). **Caveat:** journal publication was not confirmed. This paper was not in the withdrawn set. It is not the financial-statement GPT strategy and not “Bloated Disclosures.” A related attention model finds that the market reacts to segment information, goodwill and intangibles, revenues, and operating income, and least to sustainability and governance. That is contemporaneous information content, not a future-return book ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5047947)).

**19. Forward-looking MD&A tone that contradicts the accrual. Tier B, an interaction that shuts off another anomaly.** Naive-Bayes tone of sentences identified as forward-looking, not dictionary tone of the whole MD&A. **Read** forward-looking sentences in Item 7 and the 10-Q MD&A, and tag operations versus liquidity versus litigation. **Sign:** average tone is positively associated with future earnings. The return result is conditional. When tone is positive and accruals are negative, or tone is negative and accruals are positive, accruals are not associated with future returns. Diction, General Inquirer, and LIWC do not positively predict future performance. **Horizon:** the accrual-return window this interaction turns off. No standalone tone holding period was claimed. **Magnitude:** no standalone long-short. **Source:** Li, Journal of Accounting Research, 2010 ([Wiley](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00382.x)). **Caveat:** Muslu, Radhakrishnan, Subramanyam, and Lim find that more forward-looking MD&A, especially operations-related sentences, improves how well prices reflect future earnings. That is price efficiency, not a trading-rule alpha ([Management Science, 2015](https://scispace.com/papers/forward-looking-md-a-disclosures-and-the-information-3niyzindg0)).

**20. Complexity as a multiplier on the filing’s own news. Tier B, the text does not supply the sign.** A long or complex 10-K, with the filing-window return supplying the direction. **Read** the document’s length only after stripping exhibits and markup, and only as a weight on the initial reaction. You and Zhang’s own complexity formula was not re-read; a citing paper says they use a larger word count. **Sign:** the filing-window move lines up with future profitability, and prices keep drifting for twelve months, more so when the filing is complex. Complexity does not, in the abstract, predict the sign. **Horizon:** twelve months. **Magnitude:** no verified hedge return. **Source:** You and Zhang, Review of Accounting Studies, 2009 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-008-9083-2)). **Caveat:** Brown and Tucker find a larger absolute three-day reaction when the MD&A was modified, and that reaction weakened as modification scores fell ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1452977)). Lazy prices find no announcement effect. Absolute reaction, signed drift, MD&A-only, and cross-sectional unusualness are different tests and were not reconciled. Fog is the wrong readability measure, and gross file size is contaminated by HTML, XML, and embedded PDFs ([Loughran and McDonald 2014](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2014/06/24/measuring-readability-in-financial-disclosures/); [Bonsall, Leone, Miller, and Rennekamp](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-plain-english-measure-of-financial-reporting-readability/)). Length growth from 1996 to 2013 is mostly fair value, internal controls, and risk-factor requirements ([Dyer, Lang, and Stice-Lawrence](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2741682)).

**21. Financial-negative word share. Tier B, a short-window filing reaction.** The proportion of the parsed 10-K on the Loughran–McDonald financial negative list. **Read** the full parsed 10-K with exhibit segments removed. MD&A-only counts are not a clearer file-date signal. **Sign:** higher negative share, lower excess return over days [0, 3]. Positive and litigious proportions are not the significant lists in the proportional panel. With tf-idf weights, every list except litigious is significant for that same short window. **Horizon:** days **[0, 3]**. Not significant on **[0, 1]**. Not a verified 12-month factor. Year-to-year differences of the proportion keep the short-window sign and do not, in the authors’ view, solve the noise problem. **Magnitude:** no basis-point coefficient was recovered. About **73.8 percent** of Harvard-IV negative counts in these 10-Ks are not financially negative. Mean financial-negative share is about **1.39 percent**. **Source:** Loughran and McDonald, Journal of Finance, 2011 ([PDF](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf)). **Caveat:** the only re-test located, a 2019 student thesis, says the four-day pattern is gone in 2008–2018 ([Aalto](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/253e57c2-b522-4078-82cb-4524ac8c3d2e/content)). Firms manage the words machines flag ([NBER w27950](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27950)). A later working paper that sorts on pessimism change times past returns is not this result and is not treated as established.

**22. Return-weighted or price-supervised tone. Tier B, the shelf life is two weeks or the filing window.** Term weights fit on past price reactions, or dictionaries learned from those reactions with bigrams and a cross-validation holdout, instead of equal weights inside a hand list. **Read** the 10-K for Jegadeesh and Wu; Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer train across calls, 10-Ks, and the Wall Street Journal and validate on announcement returns. **Sign:** unlike equal-weighted positive counts, weighted positive language is related to filing-period returns. **Horizon:** Jegadeesh and Wu say the relation lasts **up to two weeks** and is then gone. Garcia’s verified win is out-of-sample prediction of price movements and of 10-K filing reactions, not a 12-month alpha. **Magnitude:** no basis-point long-short was extracted for word power. On the earnings-call event, a baseline R² of **1.7 percent** rises to **2.1 percent** with non-overlapping Loughran–McDonald terms and to **4.6 percent** with the non-overlapping machine-learned list; the overlap list has the largest univariate R², **5.4 percent** ([Jegadeesh and Wu](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13002328); [Garcia, Hu, and Rohrer](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3630898)). **Caveat:** because the supervisor is the price reaction, this is not a pure ex ante score. Moving the short-window win into a drift factor would be a new claim. FinBERT’s accuracy edge is a classification result on analyst sentences, not a license to trade a 10-K sentiment quantile.

## Eight extraction targets stay unproven as alpha

**23. New severe risks versus risks that were actually retired. Tier C, unproven.** Classify each Item 1A edit as a new operational, legal, financing, or going-concern risk, scored for novelty, severity, and persistence across filings, or as old uncertainty that was deleted, softened, or replaced only after the operations or the balance sheet turned. A wording tidy-up does not count. **Read** Item 1A against the prior Item 1A, then check the financial statements before crediting a deletion. **Sign:** the 2026 practitioner posts treat new severe risks as a long-avoidance filter and treat resolved language as a different signal. **Horizon:** none tested. **Magnitude:** no verified magnitude. **Source:** Quant Beckman, 29–30 October 2026 ([post](https://x.com/quantbeckman/status/2116169414718464225)); S&P Global’s “not so lazy” column urges topic, materiality, and polarity on the risk-section edit and publishes no new return ([HedgeNordic](https://hedgenordic.com/2026/06/not-so-lazy-prices/)). **Caveat:** unproven. Feature 1 already says changers underperform. This is the refinement that would have to beat Jaccard. It has not.

**24. Specific risk language as a weight, not as a book. Tier C for subsequent returns; the published object is a three-day absolute reaction.** Score whether Item 1A names a customer, a lawsuit, a covenant, a number, or a scenario, as opposed to an industry essay, and use that flag to down-weight generic cyber, climate, and AI sentences inside other features. **Read** Item 1A. **Sign:** Hope, Hu, and Lu find a larger absolute three-day filing reaction and better analyst risk assessment when specificity is higher, after controls. They do not find a signed post-filing drift. A day-0 reaction is a reason to expect less subsequent drift, not more. **Horizon:** the three-day window around the 10-K. **Magnitude:** no coefficient per unit of specificity was extracted ([RAS 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v21y2016i4d10.1007_s11142-016-9371-1.html)). **Caveat:** unproven as a holding-period excess return. Kravet and Muslu associate risk-disclosure increases with volatility and volume, not with a mean-return long-short, and they read industry-year-deviant risk text as more boilerplate, which conflicts with the specificity result and should not be averaged away ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1736228)). Adding or removing individual risk-factor captions is associated with a lower variance risk premium. That is an options result, not an equity long-short ([The Accounting Review, 2023](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-review/article/98/6/327/11227/Changes-in-Risk-Factor-Disclosures-and-the)).

**25. Walked-back targets and redefined KPIs. Tier C, unproven.** This period’s MD&A or outlook versus the firm’s own prior filing and versus the earnings call: disappeared quantitative targets, a KPI whose definition moved, guidance that was on the call and missing from the MD&A. **Read** Item 7 against the prior Item 7 and against the 8-K earnings release. **Sign:** not established. The coarse published sign is still feature 1’s: changers do worse. **Horizon:** none. **Magnitude:** no academic magnitude for literal walk-backs, guidance withdrawals inside MD&A, or KPI replacements. **Source:** the gap is stated against lazy prices ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3254078)). A 2026 practitioner note describes SEC staff asking issuers to quantify drivers and to align MD&A with the call, with no return test ([FinRep](https://www.finrep.ai/blog/10-q-md-a-requirements-2026-practitioner-walkthrough)). A hedge-fund study finds omitted risks predict lower subsequent fund performance; that is not a stock ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3803729)). **Caveat:** unproven. Cosine similarity already carries the large lazy-prices figure. An LLM adds a return only if dropped numbers beat overlap. That test was not found.

**26. Multi-year changes in accounting estimates. Tier C, unproven.** Year-over-year moves in useful lives, residual values, and reserve methods in the notes. **Read** Item 8, across several years, not one adjective in MD&A. **Sign:** none tested. **Horizon:** none. **Magnitude:** no verified magnitude. Hudson Labs’ advertised examples — Amazon’s server life cut from six years to five in 2025, Oracle’s raised from five to six — are a product demo ([Hudson Labs](https://www.hudson-labs.com/post/product-announcement-industry-leading-multi-year-ai-analysis-of-10-ks-press-releases-and-more)). **Caveat:** unproven. Generalist models miss these inside a long filing, which is an extraction fact, not an alpha.

**27. Contradictions across Item 1A, MD&A, and the notes. Tier C, unproven.** The same fact — a customer share, a risk, a number — stated differently in the risk factors, the MD&A, and the footnotes. **Read** those three places against each other, not against last year. Last year is feature 1. **Sign:** none for equities. **Horizon:** none. **Magnitude:** no equity-return test was found. **Caveat:** unproven. Analogy to lazy prices or to hedge-fund inconsistencies is not evidence. A legal note on mismatched percentages in an Indian draft prospectus is a diligence standard, not a return study ([Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/corporate-commercial-law/one-fact-many-chapters-the-horizontal-diligence-problem-in-a-drhp)).

**28. Procedural core-earnings extraction. Tier C for returns; the published win is next year’s earnings.** Identify unusual losses, then unusual gains, then aggregate, with the spans kept. **Read** the full 10-K in serial passes. One unconstrained prompt conflates core earnings with EBITDA. **Sign:** no stock-return sign. The sequential measure predicts future earnings better than GAAP net income in most of the authors’ tests and is not systematically income-increasing the way Compustat operating income is. **Horizon:** one- and two-year earnings, not a return horizon. **Magnitude:** no stock long-short. A secondary summary reports an autoregressive coefficient of **0.917** versus **0.849** for GAAP net income, next-period net-income R² of **70.86 percent** versus **60.87 percent**, a median gap versus GAAP under **10 cents**, and the sequential measure above GAAP only **61 percent** of the time versus **87 percent** for Compustat operating income after depreciation ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67100); [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/can-artificial-intelligence-outsmart-seasoned-equity-analysts/)). **Caveat:** unproven as a return. The return result that does exist for addbacks is feature 5, and it lives in the reconciliation table. On a secondary description of a tagging benchmark, generative models extract the number and then assign the wrong GAAP concept most of the time, with tag-selection accuracy about **0.17**. That figure is second-hand and is only a warning to keep an audit trail.

**29. AI language only when a reported line backs it. Tier C, unproven as a filing alpha.** Adoption, employment, patents, or a revenue or backlog line that matches the AI disclosure, as opposed to an Item 1A keyword, a speculative mention, or a sector label. **Read** Item 1 and Item 1A against the headcount, product, and revenue discussion. **Sign:** introductions of AI keywords, actionable language, speculative language, and irrelevant language have insignificant cumulative abnormal returns from three days before the 10-K to three days after. One displayed keyword coefficient is **0.003 (t ≈ 0.65)** ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105752192500465X)). Barrios, Campbell, Johnson, and Liu say firms that align high AI disclosure with substantial AI employment “significantly outperform peers in long-term stock returns,” and the abstract has no basis-point figure ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5133107)). **Horizon:** the filing window is a null. The long-term claim has no horizon in the abstract. **Magnitude:** no verified 10-K long-short after industry, profitability, and a real-activity control. Call-based AI exposure is a different asset: about **1.37 percent** higher annual returns per standard deviation, significant only after ChatGPT, with a full-sample five-factor alpha of **37 basis points a month** that “appears only in the post-ChatGPT period” ([Basu and coauthors](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/97605/1/Basu-etal-SSRN-2026-Firm-level-AI-exposure-measurement-determinants-and-pricing-implications.pdf)). A student thesis reports post-2023 monthly five-factor alphas of **0.59–0.77 percent** on LLM Item 1A AI-risk scores and finds **54–66 percent** of the spread is between sectors ([SSE thesis](https://www.uppsatser.se/uppsats/725e74bfc6/)). **Caveat:** unproven. Item 1A disclosure that AI is a risk rose from **17 percent** of the S&P 500 in fiscal 2022 to **77.6 percent** in fiscal 2025, so the keyword is a regime, not a firm insight ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/19/7/473)). The two-week **5 percent** Artificial-Minus-Human return after the ChatGPT release is a workforce-exposure event, not a filing score.

**30. Firm-specific political and regulatory risk in the filing. Tier C, unproven on 10-Ks.** Topic-specific political, trade, or regulatory exposure in Item 1A or the MD&A that is idiosyncratic rather than an industry-time wave, and only if the firm is not already lobbying or donating the exposure away. **Read** Item 1A and the MD&A policy discussion. Do not paste a call transcript score onto the filing. **Sign:** Hassan, Hollander, van Lent, and Tahoun’s canonical measure is the share of the earnings call devoted to political risk. It predicts volatility, lower investment, and lobbying. It is not a cross-sectional return premium ([Quarterly Journal of Economics](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24029)). A related working paper finds **0.5 to 1.0 percent** higher annual returns per standard deviation, and only for firms that do not manage the exposure. The abstract does not say the text is a 10-K, and journal publication was not confirmed ([Gorbatikov, van Lent, Naik, Sharma, and Tahoun](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3480494)). **Horizon:** not established for filings. **Magnitude:** no verified 10-K tradable return. The call-based political-risk alpha in the transcripts paper is **5.28 percent** annualized and not significant. **Caveat:** unproven. Robeco’s tariff charts measure worried versus neutral sentences on calls and do not publish a stock-level excess return ([Robeco](https://www.robeco.com/es-latam/vision-de-mercado/2025/06/quant-chart-whos-afraid-of-the-tariff-man)).

## Timestamp the section, then beat a simpler score

**Trade after the document exists, and do not invent a clock the notes did not verify.** Lazy prices enter the month after the filing becomes public. Feldman starts two days after the SEC filing date. Research Affiliates see no jump at release and a gap that widens for twelve months. Padysak ranks each month on the latest report already in hand, which is a different and earlier rule than Cohen’s. The withdrawn financial-statement strategy waited until 30 June for December year-ends. The call-risk paper traded on 31 March of the next year. A score dated at the fiscal period-end is not available at the 10-K, and a score traded before the text’s timestamp is ordinary look-ahead. The exact EDGAR field — acceptance datetime versus the filing-date stamp — and any 16:00 Eastern cutoff were not re-read from Loughran and McDonald or from the Notre Dame documentation in these notes, and no practitioner source here stated an acceptance-clock rule. Use the acceptance timestamp once it is in the data, start the holding period only after it, and if that timestamp is after the cash close do not pretend the same session was available. That after-the-close convention is market structure, not a figure quoted from these papers. Never align a fiscal-year Item 1A filed the next spring with returns inside the fiscal year. That is the carbon-premium timing error, and lagging was enough to remove the premium in the critique that was summarized.

**Difference the firm against itself, then against peers, and do not pour the 10-K into one prompt.** Changes beat levels for the drifts that exist: document overlap, MD&A tone, backlog, customer concentration, and pension footnotes. Levels of negative words mostly show up inside four days. The sign of “change” is not uniform. Overall and uncertainty-language similarity: changers do worse. Positive-language similarity: changers do better, in one stale large-cap backtest. Debt-constraint language: the constrained firm earns more, as a premium. Customer-concentration increases and the level of having a major customer do not share a sign. Backlog changes and backlog levels do not share a pricing error. Rank inside the cross-section, using the prior year’s distribution of scores as Cohen do, and check that peers did not all rewrite at once. Segment before the model sees the file. Emit separate scores for Item 1, Item 1A, Item 3, Item 7, and the notes. One Edinburgh preprint finds the full 10-K more informative at sector level and Item 1A more informative at the single-stock level, especially for volatility; it is unreplicated and is a warning against “risk factors always,” not a new standard ([arXiv:2607.14174](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14174v1)). A single pass over a full 10-K failed at core-earnings extraction even when the model accepted the input. The supported design is a procedural prompt with extracted spans. Chain-of-thought changed accuracy from **52 percent** to **60 percent** in a paper that was later withdrawn, which is a fact about prompt sensitivity, not a result to trade.

**Look-ahead is a documented failure mode.** Prompting a model to ignore knowledge after a date does not fix it. Sarkar and Vafa’s recommendation is a model whose pretraining corpus stops before the decision date and is free of survivorship bias. On headlines, Glasserman and Lin find that inside the training window, anonymized text outperforms named text, so firm-specific memory can hurt a sentiment read even while outcome memory should help. A positive in-sample alpha is not evidence that memory is absent ([arXiv:2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)). A look-ahead propensity score is positive through the training window and collapses after the cutoff; applied to the headline design, the interaction accounts for a large share of the in-sample next-day effect and is statistically zero in 2024 ([arXiv:2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v2)). Models recall pre-cutoff index levels, unemployment, and GDP, with errors concentrated after the cutoff ([arXiv:2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v1)). In one trading benchmark, in-window alphas decay by more than **15 percentage points** once the window moves past the model’s knowledge, including **−15.25 points** for Llama 3.1 70B and **−21.77 points** for a DeepSeek model ([Look-Ahead-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13770v1)). Those benchmarks are not 10-K studies. They are why a filing backtest has to freeze the model or walk it forward. Anonymizing the Kim financial-statement inputs did not save that paper, and the paper was not reading narrative anyway.

**The baseline to beat is section-level overlap plus a finance dictionary, on a post-filing horizon, net of the known accounting factors.** Research Affiliates’ result is that a simple vocabulary overlap still works, at a few percent a year in the top 1,000, and that its best job is a value-trap filter rather than a market-neutral book. Brain’s filing metrics — sentiment, uncertainty, litigious, constraining, and interesting shares, readability, and year-over-year similarity, for the full report and for risk factors and MD&A — are what practitioner backtests actually wire up. No vendor datasheet in this search publishes a live net-of-cost Sharpe for a filing-text factor. Generic psychosocial dictionaries are a settled failure: about three-quarters of Harvard negative counts are not negative in a 10-K. Accruals are a base-rate warning, not a target. By 2011 the accruals hedge was, on average, no longer reliably positive ([Green, Hand, and Soliman](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1320)). Sloan’s original hedge was not re-read and is not quoted. Post-earnings-announcement drift is the wrong comparison for a 10-K feature and the right one for an earnings-release feature, and a language score on the release still has to show incremental drift after the surprise. The practical test for any new score is whether it predicts the next return after section-level Jaccard, the Loughran–McDonald change, accruals, asset growth, profitability, a distress proxy, and the latest earnings surprise. Nothing retrieved has run that horse race for an LLM.

## What the shortlist leaves out, on purpose

Fog, gross file size, and the Bog index predict complexity, volatility, or forecast dispersion, not a mean excess return that these notes could verify. Beneish, Piotroski, Sloan accruals, asset growth, and abnormal capital spending are numeric. Sentences that restate them are not an incremental return on the evidence here, and the accruals hedge had already decayed in the 2011 study. No return magnitude was located for remaining performance obligations, net retention, churn, bookings versus revenue, price versus volume, organic versus acquired growth, warranty text, lease footnotes, auditor changes, restatement holding-period drift, or buyback-completion language. Deferred-revenue changes are described as mispriced in the US, and a portfolio return was not extracted from the primary paper. Uncertain-tax-benefit additions are tied to a longer post-filing drift whose direction and size were not quantified; a 2026 paper’s citation says the association with abnormal returns is positive, so this is not a hidden-liability short. High goodwill combined with low profitability is followed by lower returns and delayed impairments after SFAS 142, but qualitative “headroom” sentences were not the tested variable, and a blog’s −15.6 percent is not the paper’s number. Lexical density has a strategy-page Sharpe of **0.69** in a thin snippet, without a retrieved annualized return. Key-person and labor-risk disclosures show up in volatility and in the stock-price drop when someone leaves, not in a realized equity premium in the tests retrieved. Human-capital word counts track a disclosure regime. Moat and product-uniqueness language track profitability or the valuation level and do not deliver a standalone premium here. A hard cut to the greenest text damaged one manager’s alpha exposure by nearly half even though the score did predict peer-relative carbon-intensity declines ([Acadian](https://www.acadian-asset.com/investment-insights/esg/a-machine-learning-based-approach-to-thematic-esg-investing)). Item 1C, maintenance-versus-growth capital-spending sentences, and segment or geographic narrative shifts residual to the reported numbers had no verified post-filing equity spread. A cached working paper on risk-factor peers reports a monthly alpha near **1.8 percent**; journal publication was not confirmed, and it is not canon.

The two withdrawn GPT papers, the call-transcript risk premia, and next-day headline drift do not belong on a filing shortlist. Neither does the 188 basis-point figure as a number to underwrite, the four-day negative-word rule as a twelve-month factor, or a cyber score that forgets to say whether it is Florackis’s premium or Celeny’s decayed embedding.

## Conclusion

The book these notes actually support is a slow, section-level redline, used hardest inside value and low quality, plus a short list of signed exceptions that must not be averaged into “bad text.” Debt constraints and Item 1A cyber exposure pay as premia and should lose money in their bad state. Positive-language rewrites, backlog increases, and reported covenant violations do not carry the lazy-prices sign. Material weaknesses and recurring non-GAAP exclusions are drifts with a narrower audience or an older regime. Everything an LLM is uniquely tempted to judge — moat, credibility, a risk the filing never names — is either untested or the channel through which the model remembers the future. Spend the model on spans a dictionary misses, freeze the weights before the backtest, and keep the score only if it still predicts after Jaccard. Until that residual exists, the prompt is a research program sitting on top of a simpler anomaly, not a replacement for it.
